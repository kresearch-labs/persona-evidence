## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Eight people — Josiah B. Moore (43), his wife Sarah (39), their children Herman (11), Katherine
(10), Boyd (7), Paul (5), and overnight guests Lena (11) and Ina (8) Stillinger — were
bludgeoned to death with an axe in the Moore home at 508 E. Second Street, Villisca, Iowa,
during the night of June 9–10, 1912. The bodies were found about 8:30 a.m. June 10. The scene
was heavily contaminated by townspeople before professional processing. The case produced a
coroner's inquest (June 1912), a grand jury that returned no murder indictments (October 1912),
a federal serial-killer theory (1913), a detective-agency campaign against a local banker
(1916–17), and two trials of Rev. George Lyn Kelly (September 1917, hung jury; November 24,
1917, acquittal). No one was ever convicted. **The offender's identity is not established.**
Every named suspect theory depends on interested or single-source evidence; the strongest
supported observations concern the crime scene, not the culprit. The strongest surviving
physical item — the axe — survives with a documented (though broken in 1923–45) chain of
custody and now rests in the Montgomery County History Center, Red Oak, Iowa.

- Lena's assault: prosecutor's 1917 "work of a pervert" characterization vs doctors' explicit
  negative finding — the medical record controls; prosecution characterization was advocacy.

## 5. INVESTIGATION AND PROSECUTION HISTORY (verified actions)

1912: county investigation; coroner's inquest June 11–18; militia guard; bloodhounds;
McClaughry scene visit (prints inconclusive); transient-arrest churn (V-18); October grand
jury — no murder indictments [V-19]; AG Cosson retains Burns detectives (Tobie → Gordon) by
September [V-20]. 1913: McClaughry's federal serial-killer theory (May 1913) [V-21].
1914: Kelly's federal obscene-mail conviction; St. Elizabeths. 1916: Burns/Wilkerson era;
Mansfield arrest; special grand jury refuses indictment 4–3 [V-24]; Jones v. Wilkerson verdict
for Wilkerson (Dec 1916) [V-25]. 1917: Havner grand jury — 100+ witnesses, no Jones
involvement found; Kelly indicted for Lena Stillinger's murder; confession Aug 31; first trial
hung 11–1; second trial acquittal Nov 24, 1917 (first ballot) [V-27–29]; Havner's reserved
retrial never exercised [V-30]. After 1917: no state charges ever again; investigative energy
migrates to private historians (Epperly from 1955; Marshall 2003; Jameses 2017).

Investigative failures (documented): scene contamination before professional control [V-10,
S12]; weapon moved and handled [EV-01]; prints scattered and inconclusive [EV-12]; time-of-
death window left broad; witness accounts cross-contaminated before inquest (Bowers 2010 via
[S33]); two official bodies (1912 grand jury; 1917 Havner grand jury) reached different working
conclusions than the Burns campaign (Jones/Mansfield) that publicized them.

## 6. SUSPECT-BY-SUSPECT ANALYSIS

Format: origin → support → contradiction → official treatment → independence → falsifier → residual.

1) Rev. George Lyn (Lyn George Jacklin) Kelly — itinerant minister, 5'1", ~115 lb, left-handed.
   Origin: his own 1912–13 letters (elicited by an investigator's bait letter) + July 17, 1914
   press; prosecution by AG Havner 1917 [S13][S41].
   Support: present in Villisca June 8–10 conducting the Children's Day services; left on the
   5:19 a.m. train; left-handed (matches Linquist's spatter inference); documented sexual-
   deviance record (peeping, obscene mail, St. Elizabeths); bloody clothing reportedly sent to
   Macedonia laundry; allegedly told fellow passengers at dawn of "eight" killed; returned a
   week later posing as a Scotland Yard detective to view the house; signed 1917 confession
   ("I killed the children upstairs first and the children downstairs last…") [S13].
   Contradiction: confession extracted after hours of interrogation and recanted; the train-
   station couple who buttressed his foreknowledge "changed their story"; the Macedonia laundry
   evidence was never established as bloody; no physical evidence linked him; small stature;
   first jury 11–1 for acquittal, second unanimous acquittal after ~5 hours [S04][P02].
   Official: indicted April 1917 (Lena Stillinger count); hung jury Sept 1917; acquitted
   Nov 24, 1917; Havner reserved retrying for seven other deaths — never done [S33].
   Independence: multiple institutions in play (federal mail case, state trials, recanting
   witnesses, press) — not single-source, but its strongest affirmative items are recantations
   or non-physical.
   Falsifier (for guilt): nothing short of a confession-quality record or physical match;
   already materially weakened by the juries and recantations. Residual: he was in town, he
   was odd, and he knew details — a coherent but unproven candidate; the acquittal is not
   proof of innocence, and the confession is not proof of guilt.

2) F.F. (Frank Fernando) Jones — banker/ex-employer of Josiah Moore.
   Origin: town gossip June 10, 1912 (Methodist/Presbyterian split); formal accusation 1916 by
   Burns detective J.N. Wilkerson [S13].
   Support: documented business motive (Moore quit Jones's store in 1907, took the John Deere
   account across the street); alleged affair with Jones's daughter-in-law (unsupported —
   [S24]); town conviction persisted even after the Mansfield collapse [S13][S11].
   Contradiction: case rested on hearsay-grade witnesses (Tompkins, Willard — who first named
   Albert Jones then switched to Frank; Noell's shed eavesdropping); no physical evidence;
   1917 grand jury "found no evidence of any involvement by Frank Jones or anyone else" [S15];
   Wilkerson's credibility later damaged (1918 false-registration arrest) [S09].
   Official: never charged; his libel suit against Wilkerson failed (verdict for Wilkerson,
   Dec 1916) — which proves only the statements were not provably false, not that Jones was
   guilty. Independence: LOW — single-investigator construct over community gossip.
   Falsifier: any documented Jones–Mansfield contact in June 1912 (never produced).

3) William "Blackie" Mansfield — alleged hired killer.
   Origin: Wilkerson 1916 ("Insane Blackie"). Support: 1914 Blue Island, IL axe murders of his
   own family (pattern plausibility); Thorpe's identification of him at Clarinda station
   morning after (would break the alibi) [S24][S09].
   Contradiction: payroll records placed him working in Illinois [S13]; special grand jury
   refused indictment 4–3; Judge Woodruff released him July 21, 1916 [S09]; he won damages
   against the Burns agency (amount $2,225 vs $2,250 across secondaries — drift). Independence:
   LOW (Wilkerson's candidate) with an independent documentary alibi.
   Falsifier: independent verification of the payroll alibi — which the 1916 grand jury
   effectively accepted.

4) Henry Lee Moore — the 1913 federal serial-killer candidate.
   Origin: Bureau of Investigation agent M.W. McClaughry, May 1913, linking Colorado Springs
   (Sept 1911), Monmouth IL, Ellsworth KS, Paola KS (June 5, 1912), Villisca, and Columbia MO
   (Dec 17, 1912) [S15][S38][S12].
   Support: convicted axe murderer (Columbia — his own mother and grandmother); released from
   Kansas reformatory before the chain began; signature overlaps (covered victims, covered
   mirrors/telephones, washed weapon, rail proximity).
   Contradiction: discredited alibi-side evidence — reformatory superintendent Amrine reported
   Moore was corresponding with him and at home in Columbia during (at least one) chain murder;
   weapon variation across the chain (pipe at Monmouth; coal pick at Paola); five-state
   geography in 14 months; the Columbia killings had a family/inheritance motive — "it is rare
   for a wandering serial killer to return home and kill his own family" [S12]; Joseph
   Stillinger's lookalike boarder "Hellum" most likely was not Moore [S24]. Independence: a
   single-agent theory, never adopted by any court; Wikipedia's infobox ("Perpetrator: Henry
   Lee Moore (heavily theorized)") launders theory into fact — see §8.
   Falsifier: documentary placement of Moore away from any chain site he is assigned; his
   Villisca presence was never established.

5) Andy Sawyer — railroad worker found with the crew the discovery morning.
   Origin: grand-jury testimony of foreman Thomas Dyer [S11].
   Support: arrived Creston 6 a.m. June 10 with muddy shoes, wet-to-the-knee pants; hired on
   the spot; bought and read the murder's newspaper alone; slept clothed with an axe; admitted
   being in Villisca Sunday night and fleeing "afraid of being taken as a suspect" [S11].
   Contradiction: Osceola vagrancy arrest + sheriff's recollection of putting him on a train
   ~11 p.m. the murder night — an alibi that officials accepted [S11]; behavior equally
   consistent with an eccentric drifter; documentation ends there.
   Official: detained, released, put on a train out of Villisca; never charged.
   Independence: essentially one witness (Dyer) plus county records as recounted later.
   Falsifier: verification of the Osceola jail record (county-level, plausibly extant).

6) Local and transient candidates (Sam Moyer — brother-in-law, documented threats, arrested
   June 13, 1912, cleared; Joe Ricks — bloodstained shoes innocently explained; Bohland,
   Roberts, Van Alstine — arrested/released 1912; Leroy Robinson/George Meyers 1931 Detroit
   confession — "does not tally with the record" (six vs eight) [S09]): all press-era arrests,
   none indicted; the 1931 confession was contemporaneously debunked.

7) Paul Mueller — "The Man from the Train" (Bill James & Rachel McCarthy James, 2017).
   Origin: the book [S23][S30]. Support: ~30-trait signature cluster (rail proximity, flat-of-
   blade strikes, covered heads, covered windows, no robbery, no dog) across 1898–1912 family
   murders "stopping abruptly after 1912"; Villisca fits the cluster.
   Contradiction: Mueller's identification rests on ~500 words of 1890s press about the
   West Brookfield, MA manhunt; no record places him in Iowa in June 1912; reviewers note the
   identification is "circumstantial conjecture" once the pattern is accepted; speculative
   extensions (Hinterkaifeck 1922) strain credibility; Epperly (the case's foremost historian):
   "I wasn't in a position to prove or disprove that argument. But that is a viable theory"
   [S31]. Independence: fully independent of the period suspects; the identity itself is a
   two-researcher newspaper-mining construct.
   Falsifier: any documentary record placing Mueller elsewhere on June 9, 1912; or showing
   chain-member crimes with different signatures in the same window.

8) The still-unnamed possibility: no arrest, confession, or accusation in the accessible record
   survived verification (V-18, V-19, V-31). If a transient committed the crime, the killer may
   never have been named. This possibility is fully consistent with the official record.

No defensible ranking exists among 1–8: none has affirmative physical evidence; the differences
between them are differences in documentation quality, not demonstrated probability.

## 7. COMPETING-HYPOTHESIS MATRIX

H1 — A known local individual with a personal motive killed the family (Kelly independently;
or the Jones-commission variant).
- Core claims: familiarity with house/routine; personal grievance (Kelly: compulsive sexual
  deviance + opportunity; Jones variant: business/sexual humiliation motive, hired hand).
- Explains: no robbery; the killer's unhurried post-attack behavior; knowledge of household
  layout; town conviction that someone local did it.
- Struggles: no physical link to Kelly; Jones theory's witnesses were recanting or interested;
  grand juries found nothing on Jones; the covering ritual is unlike a business revenge.
- Assumptions: that scene calm implies familiarity; that witness recantations were lies.
- Increase confidence: verified placement of any candidate in Villisca that night (documentary);
  a coherent account of the torn chain fragment; independent corroboration of any pre-attack
  surveillance by a local.
- Weaken/falsify: documentary alibi covering 12:30–4:30 a.m. for the candidate; evidence the
  offender was a stranger (e.g., chain-fragment traceable to a non-local).
- Distinguishable from H2/H3 only via new documentary or physical evidence.

H2 — A transient serial offender (Midwest 1911–12 chain; McClaughry's line, refined by the
Jameses as "the man from the train").
- Core claims: the Villisca murders are one node in a multi-state series sharing signature
  details; offender arrived/departed by rail; never named in any record.
- Explains: scene signatures shared with Ellsworth/Paola/Colorado Springs (lamp discipline,
  coverings, washed axe, no robbery); the town's failure to identify anyone; the 1–2 a.m.
  movement toward the train lines; lack of local motive evidence.
- Struggles: weapon variation across the chain; Henry Lee Moore's Columbia motive problem and
  alibi problems; no record of the offender's arrival/departure; the "abrupt stop after 1912"
  claim is argued from absence.
- Assumptions: that signature similarity across ~30 traits outweighs weapon/geography variance;
  that series membership survives skeptical re-coding of each member crime.
- Increase confidence: a verified new series member with tighter geography/weapon match;
  railroad-arrival evidence in Villisca that night.
- Decrease: documented alibis for chain candidates at multiple nodes; demonstrated independent
  local motive.
- Distinguishable: yes, in principle — via re-coded case-by-case comparison and any surviving
  member-case evidence; currently underdetermined.

H3 — A local offender unknown to history (not Kelly, not Jones's orbit).
- Core claims: a Villisca-area person with knowledge of the household, never publicly accused
  (or accused only obscurely), committed the crime.

## 8. MOTIVE AND BEHAVIORAL ANALYSIS (with explicit limitations)

Documented (source-anchored):
- No robbery: money, checks, memo books, keys, watch left in place [EV-10].
- Post-attack behaviors: victims' faces covered; mirrors and windows draped with household
  garments; doors darkened with apron/skirt [V-08; EV-03]; bloodied water/untouched food in the
  kitchen [EV-07]; axe wiped [EV-01].
- Lena Stillinger: found crosswise, arm wound (possible defensive), nightdress up, no
  underclothes; doctors found no evidence of sexual assault [S12][S24; EV-08]. The prosecutor's
  1917 "pervert" characterization was argument, not medical finding.
- Time discipline and lamp use: chimney removed, wick bent/dimmed [EV-02] — calculated low-
  light movement in a dark house.

Inference from scene (labeled INFERRED): the combination (own-weapon use, dimmed lamp, in-place
coverings, unhurried multi-room sequence, lingering) is consistent with either (a) an offender
familiar with the house, or (b) an experienced night offender following a practiced ritual —
both are supported by the record; neither is established. The Midwest series overlap (H2) makes
"ritual" at least as parsimonious as "familiarity."

General speculation (explicitly NOT evidence): psychological readings of the mirror-covering
(wake custom, counter-surveillance, compulsion) are unresolvable from the record; the bacon
slab's meaning (sexual? provisioning? ritual?) is unknown; the absence of dog-barking evidence
cannot distinguish familiarity from a quiet approach or from the absence of a dog.
