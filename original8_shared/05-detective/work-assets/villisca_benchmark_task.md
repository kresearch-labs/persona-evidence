# DETECTIVE AGENT BENCHMARK — CASE 12: THE VILLISCA AXE MURDERS

## Mission

Conduct a complete, source-grounded investigation of the Villisca Axe Murders of June 9–10, 1912, in Villisca, Iowa, United States.

Your objective is NOT to guess the killer. Your objective is to reconstruct the strongest defensible account of the crime, evaluate competing hypotheses, identify what the evidence establishes and what remains unknown, and design investigations that could distinguish between competing explanations.

Treat this as an independent research assignment. Do not assume that popular books, documentaries, websites, police theories, or previous investigators are correct.

You are an investigative agent, not a crime novelist.

## 1. CASE BACKGROUND

On the night of June 9–10, 1912, eight people were murdered in a residence in Villisca, Iowa: Josiah Moore, his wife Sarah, their four children, and two young guests, Lena and Ina Stillinger.

An axe was associated with the killings. The case generated competing suspect theories, substantial publicity, and a long investigative history. No conviction conclusively established who committed all eight murders.

Verify the background independently. Treat even the facts in this prompt as claims to be checked rather than unquestionable evidence.

## 2. RESEARCH REQUIREMENTS

Retrieve and examine available sources, prioritizing:

1. Contemporary newspaper reports and archival material.
2. Court records, indictments, trial transcripts, and official documents where available.
3. Historical records and contemporary investigative accounts.
4. Scholarly historical research.
5. Reputable modern reporting.
6. Books, documentaries, websites, and suspect-focused publications, clearly labeled according to their reliability.

For every source, record its title, author or institution, publication date, URL or archival identifier, source type, and the specific claims it supports.

Do not claim to have read documents that you could not access. Record inaccessible sources, incomplete records, paywalls, broken links, and secondary-source dependencies.

Do not treat multiple websites repeating the same original claim as independent corroboration.

## 3. RECONSTRUCT THE CRIME

Establish a sourced timeline covering:

- The victims' last reliably documented movements.
- The likely time window of the murders.
- Discovery of the bodies and the condition of the scene.
- The weapon and any other physical evidence.
- Entry and exit possibilities.
- Witness accounts and their reliability.
- The original investigation, later investigations, prosecutions, and investigative failures.
- Any evidence potentially lost, contaminated, mishandled, or destroyed.

Separate contemporaneous observations from later recollections.

For every disputed event, provide competing interpretations and identify the evidence that would resolve the dispute.

## 4. PHYSICAL AND FORENSIC EVIDENCE

Create an evidence register. For each item, include:

- Unique evidence ID.
- Description.
- Original source and date.
- Chain-of-custody information, if documented.
- Whether the item survives.
- Whether it was examined using methods available at the time.
- Modern forensic opportunities, if any.
- What the item supports.
- What it does NOT establish.
- Reliability and provenance limitations.

Do not infer that an axe's presence proves who wielded it, that a suspect's familiarity with the family establishes guilt, or that a confession or accusation is reliable without examining its circumstances.

Distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence.

## 5. SUSPECT UNIVERSE

Identify the principal historical suspects and any significant later candidates supported by traceable sources.

For each candidate, document:

- The earliest identifiable source proposing the candidate.
- The candidate's relationship to the victims and location.
- Evidence supporting the theory.
- Evidence contradicting it.
- Alibi claims and the quality of their corroboration.
- Witness identifications and their conditions.
- Confessions, accusations, recantations, or alleged admissions.
- Whether the theory depends on hearsay, retrospective testimony, or a single interested source.
- Which facts would falsify or substantially weaken the theory.

Do not manufacture a suspect ranking when the evidence cannot justify one.

Avoid treating a person's unusual behavior, appearance, mental health, occupation, or general personality as evidence of guilt without a demonstrable connection to the crime.

## 6. COMPETING HYPOTHESES

Develop at least three materially different explanations for the crime, provided the historical record permits them.

Examples of hypothesis categories—not conclusions—include:

- A known individual acting with a personal motive.
- An offender with a different relationship to the family.
- An alternative explanation involving mistaken assumptions about the offender, sequence of events, or investigative attribution.

For each hypothesis, specify:

1. Its core claims.
2. The observations it explains.
3. The observations it struggles to explain.
4. Assumptions required for it to work.
5. Independent evidence that would increase confidence.
6. Evidence that would weaken or falsify it.
7. Whether it is distinguishable from the other hypotheses using surviving evidence.

Do not confuse a coherent story with a supported explanation.

## 7. MOTIVE AND BEHAVIORAL ANALYSIS

Investigate plausible motives only after establishing the evidence.

Separate documented motive evidence from behavioral interpretation.

If constructing an offender-perspective motive matrix, explicitly distinguish:

- Directly documented statements or actions.
- Inferences from the crime scene.
- General behavioral speculation.

Do not claim to know what the offender wanted, feared, or intended unless the evidence supports that inference.

Consider alternative explanations for unusual scene features instead of assigning each feature a single psychological meaning.

## 8. HISTORICAL NARRATIVE AND CROWD-THEORY AUDIT

Examine how the public narrative developed.

Identify:

- The earliest appearance of major suspect claims.
- The most influential books, articles, documentaries, and websites.
- Where later accounts repeat earlier claims without independent verification.
- Contradictions between popular narratives and contemporary records.
- Claims that have been debunked, weakened, or remain unresolved.
- Any sensationalized details that cannot be traced to a reliable source.

For every prominent theory, identify both its strongest evidence and its strongest serious objection.

Do not assume a claim is false merely because it is popular, or true merely because it is old.

## 9. GENERATE NEW HYPOTHESES

Generate three to five genuinely testable hypotheses that are not merely restatements of the most popular theories.

For each hypothesis, provide:

- The observation that motivated it.
- Why existing accounts may have overlooked it.
- A concrete research procedure.
- The expected result if the hypothesis is correct.
- The expected result if it is incorrect.
- The evidence required to distinguish those outcomes.
- The practical feasibility of obtaining that evidence today.
- The risk of confirmation bias.

Clearly label these as agent-generated hypotheses, not established historical findings.

Do not invent archival records, witnesses, forensic results, or historical facts to make a hypothesis appear stronger.

## 10. DESIGN CONFIRMATION AND FALSIFICATION TESTS

Create a ranked list of the most informative remaining investigative tests.

Prefer tests that discriminate between competing hypotheses over tests that merely collect more material.

For each test, estimate:

- Expected information gain.
- Cost and effort.
- Probability that relevant evidence still exists.
- Risk of contamination or misinterpretation.
- Which hypotheses would gain or lose support under each possible outcome.

If numerical probabilities cannot be justified, use qualitative assessments and explain why.

Do not assign precise Bayesian probabilities without defensible priors and likelihood assumptions.

## 11. INDEPENDENT AUDIT

Audit every material factual claim in the final report.

For each claim, classify it as:

- VERIFIED: directly supported by a reliable source or independent corroboration.
- SUPPORTED INFERENCE: consistent with the evidence but not uniquely established.
- CONTESTED: credible sources conflict or the underlying evidence is disputed.
- UNVERIFIED: adequate support could not be located.

Record the exact supporting passage or archival reference.

Check whether the cited source actually supports the claim being made. A source mentioning a suspect does not necessarily support the allegation against that suspect.

Flag claims that depend entirely on secondary sources.

If independent verification is unavailable, say so. Do not award yourself a successful audit merely because your own report cites sources.

## 12. FINAL DELIVERABLE

Produce a complete case file with the following sections:

1. Executive summary.
2. Verified victim and crime chronology.
3. Evidence register.
4. Investigation and prosecution history.
5. Suspect-by-suspect analysis.
6. Competing-hypothesis matrix.
7. Motive and behavioral analysis with explicit limitations.
8. Historical narrative and crowd-theory audit.
9. Agent-generated hypotheses.
10. Confirmation and falsification tests.
11. Independent audit findings.
12. Unresolved questions.
13. Final verdict and confidence assessment.
14. Complete source and access log.

The final verdict must separately state:

- Whether the offender's identity is established.
- What the evidence establishes about the crime.
- Which hypothesis is best supported, if any.
- Which conclusions remain underdetermined.
- What new evidence could materially change the verdict.

If the evidence cannot support a named identification, conclude that the identity is unresolved. Do not force a solution to satisfy the task.

## 13. AGENT PERFORMANCE REQUIREMENTS

This is also an evaluation of your investigative capabilities.

Maintain a research log documenting searches, sources opened, evidence extracted, contradictions encountered, and conclusions revised.

Record failed searches and inaccessible sources.

Do not silently discard evidence that contradicts your preferred hypothesis.

Do not cite a source you have not actually inspected.

Do not fabricate citations, URLs, quotations, archival references, or facts.

Maintain the distinction between research completion and investigative resolution.

At the end, provide a machine-readable JSON summary containing:

- case_status
- strongest_supported_conclusion
- named_identification_established
- hypotheses
- evidence_for_each_hypothesis
- evidence_against_each_hypothesis
- unresolved_questions
- generated_hypotheses
- unsupported_claim_count
- inaccessible_source_count
- independent_audit_status

Use null for unknown values rather than inventing data.

The task is complete only when you have exhausted the reasonable research avenues available under your constraints, documented the limitations, and produced an auditable report.

**Success is measured by evidentiary accuracy, hypothesis discrimination, source integrity, and calibrated uncertainty—not by naming a killer.**
