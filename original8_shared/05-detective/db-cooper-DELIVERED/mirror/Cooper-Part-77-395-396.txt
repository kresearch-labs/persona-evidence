Stewardess Mistook Hijacker's Note
If WAS NO DINNER INVITATION

By KATHRYN BOARDMAN
Staff Writer

A 23-year-old Northwest Airlines stewardess thought the note a passenger handed her Wednesday just out of Portland, Ore., was a mash note — or an invitation to dinner in Seattle.

So Florence Schaffner, one of the three stewardesses on the Boeing 727 jet, slipped it into her purse, smiled at the passenger and went on with her in-flight paper work.

"But he stared at me so hard I kept looking at him," Miss Schaffner said at a press conference Friday. "Then he told me that I had better read the note right away. I did and almost fainted. It was not a mash note. He said he was hijacking the plane.

"I sat down in the seat beside him and realized I had about 50 seconds to make up my mind whether I'd panic or play it as cool as he was. I decided not to panic," she said.

She then passed the note to Tina Mucklow, 22, who telephoned the cockpit to tell the captain that the plane had been hijacked. Miss Schaffner then got out her pen and wrote down the orders from the most daring hijacker in the history of aviation.

"He wanted $200,000," she said. "He wanted four parachutes — two front packs and two back packs. He wanted his directions followed with no fuss and he wanted no police at the Seattle-Tacoma [guess: a co-m a] airport, where we were to land."

Miss Schaffner took the orders to the cockpit and Tina, a girl with rosy cheeks and long blonde hair, sat down beside the unwelcome passenger.

From just outside of Portland — on the way to Seattle — until some six hours later, Tina sat near the hijacker in the plane.

But the three pilots in the cockpit never caught a glimpse of the man who commandeered their jet plane. All communication between the hijacker and the cockpit was through Tina.

"Tina was the contact with him," Capt. William Scott, 51, the 727 pilot, said at the press conference at the Minneapolis-St. Paul International Airport.

"Three of us in the cock-pit talked it over and since Tina had established communication with him and he seemed to trust her we decided to work through her," Scott said.

The other two men are First Officer William Rataczak, 32, and second officer, Harold Anderson. The three men and Tina all live in Minneapolis suburbs.

The four who were with the hijacker the entire time of the ordeal and the two other stewardesses on the flight, Miss Schaffner and Miss Alice Hancock, sat down before a line-up of cameras and reporters to tell their story.

When it became necessary to stall to meet the demands of the hijacker before landing at Seattle, Scott said, the passengers were told that there was some difficulty and the jet was using up fuel.
