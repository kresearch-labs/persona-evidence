Section 3.22 presents two distinct sets of issues — how to construe a customer-specific "material adverse change" representation, and how to enforce it against the agreement's survival and exclusive-remedy architecture — and on both the better-reasoned disposition is that the fraudulent-inducement count proceeds while the contract count is governed, and likely barred, by the fifteen-month survival period.

**I. Issues presented.** (1) What qualifies as a "material adverse change in the business relationship" with a Material Customer — and whether declining revenue is such a change or mere "fluctuation"; (2) how the two sentences of the clause interact (actual adverse changes versus noticed threats to cease or change terms); (3) the temporal reach of "Since the Lookback Date"; (4) whether the fraud/intentional-breach carve-out in the exclusive-remedy provision extends the fifteen-month survival period or only preserves remedies; (5) whether the fraudulent-inducement count is pleaded with the required particularity; and (6) what remedies each surviving claim permits.

**II. Construction.** The representation is a relationship-level MAC clause, not a company-wide one: it warrants the continued health of the Company's dealings with each Material Customer (and Material Vendor), measured customer by customer. Properly construed, "material adverse change in the business relationship" means a change materially adverse to the quality, terms, or expected continuation of the Company's relationship with that customer — loss of the account, a significant reduction in the customer's business attributable to dissatisfaction or deteriorating terms, or a customer's expressed threat to leave — viewed from the signing date and durationally significant, not a temporary dip. Revenue decline is the natural evidence of such a change but is not automatically one: a drop reflecting market-wide forces or the customer's own fortunes may fall outside the clause, while a sustained, concentrated decline in the largest accounts — the very accounts a contemporaneous pre-signing presentation flagged as "with decreasing revenues" — plausibly evidences deteriorating relationships rather than fluctuation. Because the rep covers "any" Material Customer, a qualifying adverse change as to even one of the ten suffices; the top three customers' subsequent reduction or termination of business, tied to a pre-signing trend, makes materiality (heightened by customer concentration) a plausible, triable issue rather than a conclusory one. Sellers' "fluctuations of revenue growth and decline" argument is a factual defense going to weight, not a ground for dismissal where the alleged trend is sustained and concentrated in the largest accounts.

The two sentences allocate different risks and should not be merged. Clause (i) captures actual, even silent, deterioration; the companion sentence captures *noticed* threats to cease doing business or to change pricing or other terms "in any material respect." Reading the notice sentence as the exclusive measure of a relationship MAC would render clause (i) surplusage. The practical consequence: the buyer, which alleges only clause (i) changes and no received notice, may proceed on clause (i) alone; any claim premised on the notice sentence fails for want of alleged notice. Temporally, "Since the Lookback Date" fixes the measurement window running to effectiveness: the adverse change must have occurred or begun within that window. Post-closing losses are not themselves breaches; they are probative of a pre-closing trend — and here the pre-signing deck identifying the two largest accounts as declining is direct evidence that the deterioration began within the lookback period, satisfying the buyer's allegation of changes "before the agreement was effective."

**III. Enforcement.** The fifteen-month survival period operates as a contractual limitations period for representation claims: a breach claim not brought within it is barred, and the time bar is enforced on a motion to dismiss where untimeliness appears on the face of the complaint. The carve-out for "[a]ctions for fraud, intentional misrepresentation or intentional breach" sits in the exclusive-remedy provision, which addresses *what remedies* are available, not *how long* representations survive. The better-reasoned view — the Delaware approach exemplified by *Winshall v. Viacom International* — is that a remedy carve-out does not extend the survival period: survival and remedy provisions serve different functions, and a buyer cannot revive an untimely contract count by relabeling it "intentional breach." Accordingly, if the buyer sued more than fifteen months after closing, the Section 3.22 count should be dismissed as time-barred — unless the survival provision itself excepts intentional breach, in which case the buyer must plead a knowing breach, i.e., that sellers knew the representation was false when made; the internal presentation identifying the two largest accounts as "with decreasing revenues" supports exactly that inference.

The fraudulent-inducement count stands on different footing. The carve-out preserves fraud claims from the exclusive-remedy bar, and such claims sound in tort — a seller's knowing misrepresentation in a contract representation is not excused by the contract's own disclaimers (*Abry Partners*). To proceed, the buyer must plead with particularity who made the representation, what was false (no MAC in relationships with Material Customers since the Lookback Date), scienter (the deck supplies a strong, document-based inference), intent to induce, justifiable reliance, and damages. Reliance is justifiable if the presentation was internal and not produced to the buyer; if it was shared in diligence, sellers may attack reliance, but that is a merits question. Damages flow from the collapse of the top three customer relationships, the failed integration, and the earnout shortfall. If fraud is proven, the buyer may recover tort damages outside the contract's indemnification caps; if only timely breach is proven, recovery is limited to the contract's indemnification remedy.

**Conclusion.** Construed, Section 3.22 is breached only by an actual, material, durationally significant adverse change in a Material Customer relationship occurring since the Lookback Date — sustained decline and loss of the largest accounts, corroborated by the pre-signing "decreasing revenues" presentation, qualifies; ordinary revenue fluctuation does not. Enforced, the contract count is governed by the fifteen-month survival period and should be dismissed if untimely, because the exclusive-remedy carve-out governs remedies rather than survival; the fraudulent-inducement count survives dismissal if pleaded with particularity, which the contemporaneous internal presentation largely supplies, and remains subject to justifiable reliance and proof at trial.
