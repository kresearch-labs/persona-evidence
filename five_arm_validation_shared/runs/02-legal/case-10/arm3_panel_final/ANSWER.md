# Case 10 — Section 3.22: Material Adverse Change in Material Customer/Vendor Relationships

## Issues the clause presents

**1. A flat, per-relationship materiality standard.** Section 3.22 is not a deal-level MAC clause; it is a representation that no "material adverse change in the business relationship" has occurred between the Company and *any* Material Customer or Material Vendor since the Lookback Date. Materiality is measured at the level of each individual relationship, not against the Company as a whole, and the disjunctive "any" makes each of the ten Material Customer relationships its own warranty. A change can therefore breach the rep even if the enterprise overall was not materially affected.

**2. No qualifiers.** As quoted, the rep carries no knowledge qualifier, no ordinary-course carve-out, no "would reasonably be expected" softener, and no exception for disclosed matters. It is strict: an actual material adverse change during the lookback period breached the rep when made, regardless of what the sellers knew. Seller knowledge goes to remedy (the carve-outs), not to liability.

**3. The content of "material adverse change in the business relationship."** The phrase is broader than "lost revenue" but narrower than "any decline." It reaches the health, continuity, and terms of the relationship — volumes, renewals, pricing, service terms, and movement toward losing the account. The companion sentence confirms this: it warrants that no Material Customer had threatened to cease doing business or to change its pricing or other terms adversely, so the rep covers the *trajectory* of deterioration, not just formal termination. A sustained, material reduction or termination of business by a top account is the paradigm case of an adverse change in the relationship; quarter-to-quarter "fluctuations of revenue growth and decline" within an intact relationship are not. Prong (ii) independently captures changes in material terms — e.g., pricing concessions granted to retain an account.

**4. Timing.** The rep speaks as of the effective date and covers changes between the Lookback Date and signing. Post-closing terminations by the top three customers are not themselves breaches; they are evidence from which a pre-signing change can be inferred — corroborated by the pre-signing internal presentation identifying the two largest accounts as "with decreasing revenues" and by the functionality gaps integration later exposed.

**5. Survival and remedy architecture.** The fifteen-month survival period operates as a contractual limitations period for representation claims, and the exclusive-remedy clause channels recovery into indemnification except "[a]ctions for fraud, intentional misrepresentation or intentional breach." The dispositive enforcement questions are whether the contract count was filed within the survival period and, if not, whether the intentional-breach carve-out saves it.

## Construction

Materiality is judged relationship by relationship, on facts existing during the lookback period. A "material adverse change in the business relationship" is a durable, substantive deterioration — loss or material reduction of the customer's business, threatened loss, or an adverse change in terms — as distinct from ordinary-course variability. Because the rep is flat and unqualified, the buyer need not show seller knowledge or fault to establish liability; it must, however, plead specific facts — which customers, the magnitude and duration of the change, and that the change predated the effective date. Terminations and material reductions of business by at least two of the ten Material Customers — the two largest, flagged internally as declining before signing — clear that threshold; bare allegations of aggregate revenue softness would not. The buyer is right that revenue loss is the natural *measure* of an adverse change in a customer relationship, but it must be loss reflecting a change in the relationship itself, not transient variability; the sellers' "fluctuations" argument fails against identified top-account deterioration and would succeed only against conclusory aggregate softness.

## Enforcement in this dispute

- **Ordinary breach count.** Enforce the fifteen-month survival period as written; negotiated survival periods are enforced as bargained-for outside dates (see, e.g., Chicago Bridge & Iron Co. N.V. v. Westinghouse Electric Co. LLC (Del. Ch. 2017)). If the Section 3.22 count was filed outside the period and rests only on strict-liability breach, it is time-barred and should be dismissed.
- **Intentional-breach carve-out.** Enforce the carve-out per its plain terms. "Actions for ... intentional breach" is a contract-based category; if the parties had meant only tort claims, the third prong would be surplusage. A buyer who pleads that the sellers *knew* of the adverse changes when the flat rep was made — supported by the internal "decreasing revenues" presentation — states an intentional-breach claim that falls within the carve-out and escapes the survival period. Delaware Chancery has enforced such carve-outs to take fraud/knowing-breach claims outside both the exclusive-remedy regime and the survival period (see, e.g., Prairie Capital III, L.P. v. Double E Holding Corp. (Del. Ch. 2020)); that is a risk the sellers accepted, and it is enforced as written. The carve-out is not self-executing, though: the buyer must plead and prove knowing breach (or reckless indifference), not merely breach.
- **Fraud counts.** Fraud and intentional misrepresentation are preserved by the carve-out in any event and stand on their own elements — knowing falsity or recklessness, justifiable reliance, damages (an intentional contract breach is not, without more, fraud; Winshall v. Viacom Int'l, Inc. (Del. Ch. 2012), aff'd, 76 A.3d 808 (Del. 2013)). The same pre-signing presentation supplies the scienter needed to survive dismissal.
- **Remedy.** Contract damages run through the exclusive-remedy/indemnification regime; because the earnout turns on revenue milestones, the customer attrition also depresses earnout payments, and the rep claim and earnout dispute interlock. Fraud remedies (rescission or tort damages) remain available under the carve-out.
- **Disposition.** Deny the motion to dismiss insofar as the claim is pleaded as intentional breach and as to the fraud counts; grant it as to any rep-breach theory resting only on strict liability and filed outside the survival period. If the action was in fact filed within fifteen months of closing, the time-bar fails entirely and the claim proceeds under the pleading standards above.

**Limits.** This analysis assumes the survival clause governs Section 3.22 with no separate fraud-survival period, that "Material Customer" is a defined revenue threshold (ten such customers implies one), and that no disclosure schedule excepts the known customer deterioration; each follows from the facts given and should be confirmed against the full agreement. The Material Vendor prong is not implicated by this dispute.
