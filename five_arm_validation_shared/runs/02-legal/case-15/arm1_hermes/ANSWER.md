# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-15

**The issues:** (1) whether an express condition — written consent "on or before" a stated deadline, with an automatic nullity clause — can be satisfied by "substantial performance" after a concededly missed date; and (2) whether the substantial-performance doctrine has any application to conditions precedent fixed by clear language, or is confined to covenant/breach analysis.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **Substantial performance has no application to an express condition with an agreed automatic-termination consequence — the JNOV was correct.** The parties' allocation of risk is the whole point of condition analysis: paragraph 4(c) made timely written consent a *condition precedent*, and 4(d) confirmed the parties would "not execute and exchange the Sublease unless and until" the conditions were "timely satisfied." Paragraph 4(c)'s failure clause is unambiguous — no written consent by February 25, and the Letter Agreement and sublease become "null and void and of no further force and effect," with neither party having "any rights against nor obligations to the other." The doctrine of substantial performance belongs to the breach of covenants (where a promisor's performance, though imperfect, is close enough to earn the bargain subject to damages); it is *inapposite* to conditions precedent, whose non-occurrence extinguishes duties by the parties' own design. Courts do not rewrite agreed risk allocations under the label of substantial performance — doing so here rewrites the parties' express nullity bargain into a duty to accept late performance, which is precisely what they declined.

2. **The equities don't change the analysis.** Waiver and estoppel were tried and rejected by the jury — correctly, because the Subtenant's conduct (declaring the agreement dead the next day and refusing an extension) is the opposite of inducement; there was no reliance to estop. Substantial performance cannot smuggle the same equity back in through a different door. A phone call on deadline day that consent "had been secured" is not "written consent delivered" — the condition required writing, and the writing arrived 23 days late. Where the contract specifies the *form* (written) and the *time* (a date), performance that meets neither is nonperformance of the condition.

3. **Policy note:** courts enforce condition deadlines precisely because they allow parties to plan; relaxing them whenever performance is "mostly" done would destroy the predictability the clause exists to buy. If the Sublandlord wanted tolerance, the instrument was the place to write it (a cure period, a notice-and-cure, a delivery "within a reasonable time after").

**Practical ruling:** reinstate the judgment notwithstanding the verdict — the missed condition operated as written, the Letter Agreement and sublease were null and void from March (deadline-day-plus-one), and the $1.2 million award cannot stand.

**Key issues flagged:** condition precedent vs covenant (substantial performance applies to the latter) · express nullity clauses allocate risk and are enforced as written · oral assurance on deadline day ≠ written consent · rejected waiver/estoppel cannot return via substantial performance.
