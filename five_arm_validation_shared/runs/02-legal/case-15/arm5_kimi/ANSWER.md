# Case 15 — Answer

## Issues presented

1. **Is paragraph 4(c) an express condition precedent or a mere promise?** The clause makes timely delivery of the prime landlord's *written* consent by February 25, 1987 a condition to the sublease ever coming into force, with an automatic "null and void" consequence and a mutual release if it is not met. Paragraph 4(d) reinforces this by barring execution of the Sublease "unless and until" the condition is "timely satisfied."

2. **What degree of compliance does the condition require?** The clause specifies both *form* (a writing, physically delivered to the Subtenant) and *time* (on or before the extended deadline). A telephone report from the Sublandlord's attorney on the deadline day satisfies neither.

3. **Does the doctrine of substantial performance apply?** Substantial performance is a doctrine about constructive conditions (implied exchange-of-performances conditions); it does not apply to an express condition precedent that the parties themselves wrote into their agreement. Where the parties unambiguously fixed the manner and date of satisfaction, the condition must be literally and strictly satisfied; a court may not rewrite it as "close enough."

4. **Waiver and equitable estoppel.** Whether the Subtenant, by conduct, waived the deadline/delivery requirement or should be estopped from insisting on it. These are fact questions — and the jury resolved both against the Sublandlord.

5. **Forfeiture versus freedom of contract.** Enforcing the clause as written produces a harsh result (a 23-days-late consent defeats a $1.2 million claim), but the "null and void" term was the parties' own bargained allocation of the risk that the consent might not arrive in time, and the Sublandlord controlled neither the prime landlord's timing nor the cure.

## How the clause should be construed and enforced

- The clause is unambiguous: it fixes an express condition precedent requiring **strict compliance** — written consent actually delivered by February 25, 1987. Construing it to accept an oral phone call, or a writing delivered 23 days late, would nullify the words "written consent," the delivery requirement, the deadline, and the automatic-termination and mutual-release language, leaving them surplusage.
- Because it is an express condition, the **substantial-performance charge was error**; that theory should never have gone to the jury (as the in limine ruling initially held). The jury's "yes" answer on substantial performance cannot support the verdict.
- With the jury's findings of **no waiver and no estoppel**, nothing displaces the condition. The condition concededly failed, so by the agreement's own terms the Letter Agreement and sublease became null and void, and neither party has rights or obligations against the other.
- **Enforcement:** the trial court's judgment notwithstanding the verdict was correct and should be affirmed; the appellate court's reinstatement of the $1.2 million verdict should be reversed. The Subtenant's declaration of invalidity on February 26 was effective, and the Sublandlord takes nothing.

Justification: an unambiguous express condition precedent with a fixed form and deadline must be enforced as written; substantial performance cannot substitute for literal compliance, and with no waiver or estoppel found, the automatic "null and void" consequence controls.
