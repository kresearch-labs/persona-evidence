**Bottom line:** Paragraph 4(c) is a condition precedent to the existence of the sublease bargain, with time of the essence. The written consent was concededly not delivered by February 25, 1987, so the Letter Agreement and the sublease became null and void by their own terms; neither waiver, nor equitable estoppel, nor substantial performance changes that result. The $1.2 million verdict has no contractual footing, and the trial court's judgment notwithstanding the verdict was correctly granted.

**I. The issues the clause presents**

1. **Characterization.** Is the written-consent requirement a covenant whose breach sounds in damages, or a condition precedent that determines whether the bargain ever comes into existence?
2. **Time of the essence.** Does the deadline of February 25, 1987 admit of informal extension or lateness tolerance?
3. **Form and manner of satisfaction.** Must the consent be in writing and physically delivered to the Subtenant, or does oral notice that consent "had been secured" suffice?
4. **Waiver.** By whom, of what, and by what conduct can the condition be waived?
5. **Equitable estoppel.** Did the Sublandlord's deadline-day telephone call induce reliance that bars the Subtenant from insisting on the writing?
6. **Substantial performance.** Can a doctrine built for defective performance of existing duties excuse the failure of a precondition that the contract itself says ends the deal?
7. **Effect of the self-executing nullity clause.** What survives — if anything — for damages theories once "null and void" and "no rights against nor obligations to the other" operate?

**II. Construction**

The clause's own text answers the characterization question. Paragraph 4(d) calls the 4(c) requirements "conditions" and forbids execution and exchange of the sublease "unless and until" they are "timely satisfied"; paragraph 4(c) prescribes the self-executing consequence of nonoccurrence — the Letter Agreement and sublease are "null and void and of no further force and effect," and neither party has "any rights against nor obligations to the other." That is the anatomy of a condition precedent, not a promise: it does not create a duty whose breach is compensated; it defines whether the agreement ever becomes effective at all.

Every word does work, and the construction leaves none surplusage: "written" fixes form; "deliver ... to the Subtenant" fixes manner; "on or before" a date certain fixes time; "timely" in 4(d) ties execution of the sublease to that deadline; "null and void" and "no rights nor obligations" fix the consequence; "unless and until" makes effectiveness contingent. The prior extension "in writing" to February 25 is the parties' own construction of their clause: they knew how to enlarge the time, did so in writing, and thereby made the extended date the essence. A party who fails to obtain a further written extension before the date cannot substitute telephonic accommodation for the mechanism the clause requires.

The reading's limits: it does not make the clause self-destructive — the condition provision survives long enough to declare the agreement dead, which is precisely the work it was written to do; it does not bar the parties from making a *new* agreement after February 25 (just as they once extended in writing before it); and it applies with equal force to the clause's other applications — consent obtained on time but only orally, or obtained in writing but delivered late, fails the condition, because the parties specified form, manner, and time and were entitled to have all three honored.

**III. Waiver**

Waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right, and conduct relied on to prove it must be unequivocally referable to waiver. Only the party for whose benefit the condition exists can waive it — here the Subtenant, in whose favor the consent-and-delivery requirement ran. The record shows the opposite of relinquishment: the Subtenant insisted on the writing throughout, and on the day after the deadline promptly declared the agreement invalid and refused an extension. Cooperation before the deadline, and a refusal to renegotiate after it, are not waiver; once the date passes, a party may insist on strict compliance. The jury's no-waiver finding is consistent with this and leaves the Sublandlord with nothing from its first theory.

**IV. Equitable estoppel**

Estoppel requires a clear and unambiguous representation of material fact, reasonable reliance, and a change of position. Counsel's statement that the consent "had been secured" did not represent that the written-delivery requirement was waived, and the Subtenant changed nothing in reliance — it acted the very next morning. Nor is there prejudice attributable to anything the Subtenant said or did: the Sublandlord had the extended deadline and simply did not obtain the writing in time. Estoppel cannot manufacture a contract where the agreed condition failed.

**V. Substantial performance**

The doctrine excuses defective performance of an existing, measurable duty (classically a builder's) where the other party has substantially received the benefit and the defect is not willful. It has no purchase here, for three reasons. First, the condition was a precondition to effectiveness: until timely written consent was delivered there was no contract to perform — the agreement either existed or it did not, and "substantially existing" is not a third state. Second, the parties themselves specified the consequence of lateness; charging substantial performance rewrites "on or before February 25" into "within a reasonable time," substituting the court's allocation of risk for the parties'. Third, what was to be "performed" was procurement of a third party's written consent by a date — binary, not susceptible of degrees. Whether the doctrine can apply at all is a question of law; where it cannot, a verdict resting on it cannot stand, even though the jury also found against the Sublandlord on waiver and estoppel — those findings merely defeated the Sublandlord's other affirmative theories.

**VI. Enforcement in this dispute**

Enforce the clause as written. The written consent was not received by February 25, 1987; the next day the Letter Agreement and sublease were, by their own terms, null and void, with neither party holding rights against or obligations to the other. The consent arriving 23 days late satisfied nothing — a lapsed condition cannot be satisfied retroactively, only re-agreed, and the Subtenant declined. The "no rights nor obligations" language likewise forecloses the Sublandlord's fallback damages theories premised on the agreement's continued existence, including breach of the 4(c) covenant and any promissory-estoppel recovery. The proper disposition is judgment for the Subtenant: the trial court's JNOV should be affirmed and the appellate court's reinstatement of the verdict reversed.
