# Case 17 — Answer

**Holding (one line):** Under the Federal Arbitration Act, an ambiguous arbitration agreement does not supply the necessary contractual basis for compelling class arbitration; the clause must be construed as authorizing individual (bilateral) arbitration only, and the Company's motion to compel individual arbitration should be granted.

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Whether silence or ambiguity about class procedures can constitute consent to class arbitration.** The agreement covers "all claims that may hereafter arise in connection with my employment" and substitutes arbitration "in lieu of any and all lawsuits or other civil legal proceedings," but never mentions class actions, class representatives, absent members, or certification. The issue is whether that capacious but silent language is a contractual basis for classwide arbitration.

2. **Whether a state-law canon of construction (contra proferentem — ambiguities construed against the drafter, applied "with peculiar force" to contracts of adhesion) may be used to fill that gap.** The intermediate court used the state ambiguity rule to adopt the Employee's reading. The issue is whether that state rule can supply consent to class arbitration under the FAA, or whether it is preempted.

3. **How the "in lieu of any and all lawsuits" phrase should be read.** The court treated it as "capacious enough to include class arbitration"; the question is whether a clause choosing arbitration as the forum for all employment disputes is an affirmative authorization of a fundamentally different, class-based procedure.

## How the clause should be construed

- **Class arbitration is fundamentally different from bilateral arbitration.** It sacrifices arbitration's principal advantages — informality, speed, and low cost — and introduces procedural formality, broader discovery, class-certification litigation, due-process problems for absent parties, and dramatically higher stakes. Because the differences are so great, **consent to class arbitration must be express and unambiguous**; a party may not be forced into it on the basis of silence or ambiguity. Imposing class arbitration where the contract is ambiguous does not facilitate arbitration — it contradicts the parties' intent.

- **The clause is at most ambiguous, and ambiguity is not consent.** The "all claims" and "in lieu of all lawsuits" language identifies arbitration as the forum for employment disputes; it says nothing about class procedures. Reading it to authorize class arbitration rests on inference, not on any textual grant.

- **The state contra proferentem rule cannot supply the missing consent.** State contract principles generally govern the validity and enforceability of arbitration agreements, but a rule that converts ambiguity into consent to class arbitration conflicts with the FAA's foundational principle that arbitration "is a matter of consent." Ordinary gap-filling defaults operate against a backdrop the parties could assume; here the default would change the very nature of the proceeding the parties agreed to. The FAA therefore precludes using the ambiguity rule to compel class arbitration.

## How the clause should be enforced in this dispute

The District Court properly compelled arbitration, but erred in authorizing classwide arbitration. Because the agreement is ambiguous and does not expressly authorize class procedures, there is no contractual basis for class arbitration. The judgment should be **reversed** insofar as it compelled classwide arbitration, and the case should proceed to **individual arbitration** of the Employee's own claims; the putative class claims may not be pursued in arbitration absent express consent to class treatment.
