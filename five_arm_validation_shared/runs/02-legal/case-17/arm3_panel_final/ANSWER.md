The clause presents two questions that must be kept separate: (1) whether the parties agreed to arbitrate the Employee's employment-related claims at all, and (2) whether they agreed to arbitrate them on a classwide basis. The agreement answers the first; it is silent on the second, and that silence is dispositive.

**Scope.** "All claims that may hereafter arise in connection with my employment" is a broad scope provision, and the data-breach claims — negligent disclosure of employee tax information — arise in connection with employment. "Arbitration shall be in lieu of any and all lawsuits or other civil legal proceedings relating to my employment" confirms the forum substitution: covered claims go to arbitration instead of court. But neither phrase addresses the collective dimension of the proceeding — the words "class," "collective," and "representative" appear nowhere in the agreement — namely, whether one arbitrator may aggregate the claims of some 1,300 employees, most of whom are absent from the case and have not been shown to have assented to anything. "In lieu of any and all lawsuits" says which forum replaces a lawsuit, not how many claimants that forum may bind; reading it to authorize class treatment gives the phrase work the parties never assigned it and treats the agreement's silence on class procedure as though it were an express authorization.

**The dispositive issue — consent to class arbitration.** Class arbitration is not ordinary arbitration enlarged. It sacrifices the attributes that make arbitration distinct: it is slower, costlier, more formal, more prone to one-sided error, and it binds absent employees who never agreed to arbitrate at all. Because arbitration under the Federal Arbitration Act is strictly a matter of consent, a party may be forced into class arbitration only if the contract says so. Silence is not consent (Stolt-Nielsen S.A. v. AnimalFeeds Int'l Corp., 559 U.S. 662 (2010)), and an ambiguous agreement cannot supply the necessary contractual basis either (Lamps Plus, Inc. v. Varela, 587 U.S. 176 (2019)). Whether a contract authorizes class arbitration is a question of arbitrability for the court absent a delegation clause (First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan, 514 U.S. 938 (1995)), and this agreement contains none.

**Why contra proferentem cannot fill the gap.** State law supplies background principles of interpretation, and ambiguities may ordinarily be construed against the drafter — with special force for contracts of adhesion. But that rule resolves doubt about what the parties said; it cannot manufacture assent to a fundamentally different procedure they never mentioned. Applied as the courts below applied it, the rule does not interpret the agreement — it answers the federal consent question by default rather than by bargain, imposing class arbitration on an unwilling party because the drafter failed to write "individual only." To the extent that use of the rule stands as an obstacle to the FAA's command that arbitration rest on mutual consent, it is displaced.

**Construction and enforcement.** The agreement should be construed as a broad bilateral arbitration clause: it covers the Employee's individual claims but does not authorize class arbitration. It should be enforced accordingly — compel the Employee to arbitrate his own claims, deny classwide arbitration, and leave the putative class claims, for which no contractual basis exists, to the court to dismiss or stay. The order compelling classwide arbitration should be reversed.

The anticipated reply — that adhesion contracts justify construing every doubt against the drafter — proves too much: a default rule of construction cannot supply the actual mutual assent to class treatment the FAA requires, and the Employee's reading of "in lieu of any and all lawsuits" would convert a forum-selection sentence into a class-procedure authorization no one wrote.
