# Answer

**Issue presented:** The clause — a broadly worded, employer-drafted arbitration agreement that never mentions class proceedings — presents the question whether, under the Federal Arbitration Act, an agreement that is merely *ambiguous* (or silent) on class arbitration supplies the necessary contractual basis to compel classwide, rather than individual (bilateral), arbitration.

**How it should be construed:** It cannot. Under *Lamps Plus, Inc. v. Varela*, 139 S. Ct. 1407 (2019), an ambiguous agreement does not provide a sufficient contractual basis for compelling class arbitration. Because arbitration is a matter of consent, not coercion, and class arbitration fundamentally transforms traditional individualized arbitration — sacrificing its speed, simplicity, low cost, and confidentiality while raising the due-process stakes for absent class members — courts may not infer consent to class arbitration from ambiguity; an affirmative contractual basis is required. The state-law contra proferentem rule (construing ambiguities against the drafter, even with "peculiar force" against an adhesion contract's drafter) cannot be deployed to supply that consent: as applied to impose class arbitration on an ambiguous clause, the rule is preempted by the FAA because it favors a mode of arbitration inconsistent with the FAA's consent-first foundation. The intermediate court's contrary construction — reading the "arbitration in lieu of any and all lawsuits" language as capacious enough to include class proceedings and construing it against the Company — was error.

**How it should be enforced:** The agreement must be enforced according to its terms as an agreement to *individual* arbitration only. The Company's motion to compel arbitration on an individual basis should be granted; the Employee's claims may not proceed on a classwide basis in arbitration (or in court), absent an affirmative contractual basis for class arbitration that this clause does not contain.

**Justification (one line):** Under the FAA, class arbitration requires an affirmative contractual basis, and an ambiguous adhesion arbitration clause construed against its drafter cannot supply the requisite consent — *Lamps Plus, Inc. v. Varela*, 139 S. Ct. 1407 (2019).
