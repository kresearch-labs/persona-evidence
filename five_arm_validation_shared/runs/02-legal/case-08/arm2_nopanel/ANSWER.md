The clause obligates the Contractor to defend and indemnify the Landlord only for claims traceable to the Contractor's own conduct — its performance, the conditions it created, or its breach or inadequate performance. It does not make the Contractor the insurer of the Landlord's own negligence: the Contractor must defend the Landlord against the customer's claims insofar as they arise from the Contractor's work and must indemnify the Landlord for the 40% share of fault allocated to the Contractor (plus attributable defense costs), but it owes nothing for the 30% share allocated to the Landlord itself.

**Issues the clause presents**

1. **Indemnity for the indemnitee's own negligence.** The dispositive issue is whether, without any express reference to the Indemnitee's negligence, the clause shifts the Landlord's own share of fault to the Contractor. Indemnity provisions are strictly construed, and an intent to indemnify against the indemnitee's own negligence must be expressed in clear and unequivocal terms, with ambiguities resolved against the drafter (here, the Landlord). The trial court's "broad form" rationale rests on an older line of precedent that no longer controls a 1994 agreement — most notably in Georgia, whose 1987 amendment to OCGA § 13-8-2(b)(2) voids indemnity for the indemnitee's own negligence unless the contract expressly provides for it — and, in any event, the modern clear-and-unequivocal rule points the same way.

2. **Scope of the enumerated triggers.** Every operative trigger is Contractor-side: "Contractor's performance," "the conditions created thereby," "Contractor's breach," "inadequate or improper performance ... by Contractor or its subcontractors, servants or employees," and "any claim brought by employees of Contractor." The parties demonstrably knew how to broaden the clause (parentheticals for equipment, employees' claims, and attorneys' fees) yet never mentioned the Indemnitee's negligence; that omission, read with contra proferentem and expressio unius, means the customer's claim is covered only to the extent it rests on conditions created by the Contractor's performance — which corresponds to the 40% fault the jury allocated to the Contractor.

3. **The independent duty to defend.** The defense obligation is separate from, and broader than, loss-shifting and is measured by the claim's allegations rather than the ultimate fault allocation. Because the customer's suit against the Landlord alleged injury from a floor condition arguably produced by the Contractor's cleaning, the Contractor owed the Landlord a defense at least until the claim was confined to the Landlord's own negligence.

4. **Public-policy limits.** Even express language would not reach the Landlord's gross negligence or willful misconduct; here only ordinary negligence was found, so no public-policy bar affects the Contractor's own share.

**Construction and enforcement**

The clause should be construed as a narrow-form, Contractor-triggered indemnity: the causal nexus required by "relating to, arising out of or existing by reason of" must run to the enumerated Contractor events, not to the Landlord's independent negligence. Enforced that way, the Contractor must (a) defend the Landlord in the customer's action insofar as the claim arises from the Contractor's performance or the conditions it created, and (b) indemnify the Landlord for the portion of the judgment (and attributable defense costs) corresponding to the Contractor's 40% share of fault. It is not obligated to indemnify the Landlord for the Landlord's own 30% share: the summary judgment should be affirmed as to the Contractor-side defense and indemnity obligations and reversed insofar as it shifted the Landlord's own negligence to the Contractor.
