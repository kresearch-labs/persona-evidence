**Issues presented.** (1) Whether this indemnity clause, which nowhere mentions the Landlord's negligence, must nonetheless be read to shift the Landlord's own share of fault (30%) to the Contractor — the dispositive issue, and a high-severity one because the trial court's summary judgment turned entirely on it. (2) Whether the "broad form" of indemnification, under which the trial court reasoned the clause protects the indemnitee even against its own negligence, remains good law — it does not: the broad-form/limited-form distinction of *Doloughty v. North American Rockwell Corp.*, 139 N.J. Super. 110 (App. Div. 1973), has been implicitly overruled by *Ramos v. Barton*, 103 N.J. 177 (1986), and *Mantilla v. NC America, Inc.*, 167 N.J. 262 (2001), so reliance on it was error (medium severity). (3) Scope of the clause as actually written: every operative trigger is contractor-anchored — "Contractor's performance," "the conditions created thereby" (including equipment use, misuse or failure), "Contractor's breach," and "inadequate or improper performance ... by Contractor or its subcontractors, servants or employees" — so the clause cannot be stretched to cover the indemnitee's own negligence (high severity). (4) Construction defaults: indemnity provisions are disfavored and strictly construed, and courts will not infer coverage for the indemnitee's own negligence absent explicit language (low severity). (5) Defense costs: because the clause also obligates the Contractor to "defend," a further issue is whether the Landlord may recover its defense costs for its own independent fault — under *Mantilla*, an indemnitee defending its own negligence cannot shift those costs absent explicit language (medium severity). A further drafting signal cuts the same way: the parties expressly enumerated one claim source ("including any claim brought by employees of Contractor"), showing they knew how to specify coverage and did not do so for the Landlord's negligence.

**Construction and enforcement.** The clause is a *limited-form* indemnity and is valid and enforceable as written. Under New Jersey's explicit-and-unequivocal-reference rule (*Ramos*, reaffirmed in *Mantilla*), an indemnity provision is not construed to cover the indemnitee's own negligence unless it explicitly and unequivocally references that negligence or fault; this clause does not, and its "relating to, arising out of or existing by reason of" language is broad-form phrasing that no longer carries the *Doloughty* presumption — indeed, even under the broad-form approach the clause would fail, because its exclusive focus is the Contractor's conduct. Enforced accordingly, the Contractor must defend and indemnify the Landlord against claims arising from the Contractor's performance, the conditions it created, its breach, or its inadequate performance — including the Contractor's 40% share of the plaintiff's judgment, any Landlord liability attributable to Contractor-created conditions (such as the spilled substance if the jury found it a condition of the Contractor's making), and claims by Contractor employees — but the clause does not indemnify the Landlord for the Landlord's own 30% negligence, and the Landlord cannot recover from the Contractor the defense costs of its own independent fault. The summary judgment should therefore be reversed insofar as it awarded the Landlord indemnity for the Landlord's own fault, with the clause enforced on remand according to its limited-form terms.

**One-line justification:** Because New Jersey requires an indemnity clause to explicitly and unequivocally reference the indemnitee's own negligence (*Ramos*, reaffirmed in *Mantilla*, displacing *Doloughty*'s broad-form presumption), and every trigger in this clause is the Contractor's own performance or breach, it is enforced as a limited-form indemnity that covers the Contractor's 40% share and Contractor-created conditions but leaves the Landlord's 30% share of fault — and the defense costs of that fault — with the Landlord.
