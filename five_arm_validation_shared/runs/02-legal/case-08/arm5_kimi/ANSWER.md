# Answer — Case 08

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Scope of the indemnity — does it cover the indemnitee's own negligence?** The central issue is whether this clause, which never mentions the Landlord's negligence, obligates the Contractor to indemnify the Landlord for the Landlord's own 30% share of fault, or only for liability arising from the Contractor's performance, the conditions it created, and its breach or inadequate performance.
2. **Whether "broad form" language alone shifts liability for the indemnitee's sole or concurrent negligence.** The trial court treated the clause as "broad form" indemnity protecting the Landlord even against its own negligence; the question is whether that label can substitute for the explicit expression of intent the law requires.
3. **Severability/enforcement to the extent of the indemnitor's fault.** A subsidiary issue is whether, if the clause does not reach the Landlord's own negligence, it still validly requires the Contractor to indemnify for the portion of liability attributable to the Contractor's own fault (40%).

## How the clause should be construed

Indemnification provisions are construed under ordinary contract principles, but a promise to indemnify an indemnitee against the consequences of the indemnitee's **own negligence** is so extraordinary that it is enforceable only when that intent is **clearly and unequivocally expressed** in the contract; such clauses are strictly construed against the indemnitee, and general "broad form" language — "any claim ... relating to, arising out of or existing by reason of" the Contractor's performance — is not enough by itself to shift liability for the indemnitee's own fault.

Read against that standard, this clause is fault- and performance-based: every enumerated trigger (Contractor's performance, conditions created by Contractor, use/misuse/failure of Contractor's equipment, Contractor's breach or inadequate performance) ties the indemnity to the **Contractor's conduct**. Nothing in it refers to the Landlord's negligence, and the inclusion of claims by the Contractor's own employees does not expand it to the Landlord's independent fault. The trial court therefore erred in granting summary judgment on the theory that "broad form" language alone covered the Landlord's negligence.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

- The Contractor must indemnify and defend the Landlord **only to the extent liability arises from the Contractor's performance or breach** — i.e., the 40% of fault allocated to the Contractor (and, on the defense side, the duty to defend applies to claims falling within the clause's coverage).
- The Contractor is **not** required to indemnify the Landlord for the **Landlord's own 30% share of negligence**, because the clause does not clearly and unequivocally express an intent to cover the indemnitee's own fault.
- The summary judgment for the Landlord should be **reversed in part**: indemnification is limited to the Contractor's proportionate fault, and the Landlord bears its own 30% (the plaintiff's 30% is, of course, her own).

**Justification (one line):** Because an indemnity covering the indemnitee's own negligence must be clearly and unequivocally expressed and is strictly construed, this performance-and-breach-based clause — silent as to the Landlord's negligence — is enforced only up to the Contractor's 40% fault, not the Landlord's 30%.
