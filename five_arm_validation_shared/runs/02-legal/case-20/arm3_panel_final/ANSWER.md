The clause presents four issues: (1) its character and validity; (2) the procedural mechanism for its enforcement — dismissal for "wrong" or "improper" venue under 28 U.S.C. §1406(a) and Rule 12(b)(3), transfer under 28 U.S.C. §1404(a), or forum non conveniens; (3) the burden and weight it carries within a §1404(a) motion; and (4) its scope — what it does not do and how it operates in its other applications. The governing precedent is Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas, 571 U.S. 49 (2013), which involved this very clause and materially identical facts, applying Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22 (1988).

**Construction.** The clause is a mandatory forum-selection clause: "shall be litigated" obligates the parties to sue in one of the two fora it selects — the Circuit Court for the City of Norfolk or the Eastern District of Virginia, Norfolk Division — rather than merely permitting suit there. The plaintiff may elect either, but both are in Norfolk. It is a venue-selection provision only: it does not consent to personal jurisdiction in the selected fora, and it does not choose governing law. Its validity is unchallenged, and rightly so — it was negotiated at arm's length between two sophisticated commercial parties, is not the product of fraud or overreaching, is reasonable, and contravenes no strong public policy. See M/S Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co., 407 U.S. 1 (1972); Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute, 499 U.S. 585 (1991). It should be enforced according to its terms.

**Issue 1: Mechanism.** Not by motion to dismiss. Venue in the Western District of Texas is proper: a substantial part of the events giving rise to the payment dispute — performance of the subcontract — occurred there. 28 U.S.C. §1391(b)(2). Section 1406(a) and Rule 12(b)(3) police only compliance with the general venue statutes; venue is "wrong" or "improper" only when the case is laid in a district that fails those statutes, which protect defendants against suit in a forum with no meaningful connection to the dispute and take no account of private forum agreements. A forum-selection clause does not render an otherwise-proper venue "wrong" or "improper," so the dismissal motions were correctly denied. Atlantic Marine, supra.

Because the clause points to another federal forum, §1404(a) is the exclusive mechanism for its enforcement. Stewart, supra; Atlantic Marine, supra. Proper venue in the transferor court is presupposed — and is satisfied here — but improper venue is not required: §1404(a) operates precisely where venue is proper yet inconvenient, and the Eastern District of Virginia is plainly a district where this action 'might have been brought,' as the clause itself confirms. (Had the clause selected only a state or foreign forum, enforcement would run through forum non conveniens; the federal-forum branch of this clause is what makes §1404(a) exclusive.)

**Issue 2: Burden and weight.** The District Court enforced the clause under the ordinary §1404(a) framework — movant's burden, clause as one factor among many, full public- and private-interest balancing — and that was the error. Because a forum-selection clause represents the parties' private agreement about the most convenient forum, it must be given controlling weight in all but the most exceptional cases. Three consequences follow.

First, the plaintiff's choice of forum merits no weight. The Subcontractor agreed to litigate in Norfolk; by filing in breach it forfeits its convenience-based preference, and it — not the Contractor — bears the burden of showing that public-interest factors decisively invalidate transfer. The District Court inverted that burden.

Second, private-interest factors are entitled to no weight. The parties already bargained over the litigants' convenience when they selected Norfolk; a breaching plaintiff cannot relitigate that bargain. Only in the most exceptional case can private interests defeat the clause.

Third, public-interest factors — docket congestion, the local interest in deciding local controversies, and the like — remain relevant but rarely defeat transfer, and familiarity with the governing law is not among them. The District Court's ground for keeping the case in Texas — that Texas federal judges know Texas law better — was error: on a §1404(a) transfer the governing law follows the case, so a Virginia court would apply the same substantive law a Texas court would. Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964); Ferens v. John Deere Co., 494 U.S. 516 (1990). The rationale also misreads the clause, which embodies the parties' judgment that Norfolk judges could fairly adjudicate their disputes.

**Anticipated counterarguments.** The Subcontractor's strongest points do not hold. That venue is proper in Texas is no shield — §1404(a) presupposes proper venue and asks only whether transfer serves convenience, a question the parties already answered by contract. That the clause lacks a consent-to-jurisdiction provision is immaterial: enforcement runs through §1404(a), which does not depend on consent-to-jurisdiction language in the clause, and no personal-jurisdiction objection is raised — the Contractor is a Virginia company, plainly amenable to suit in Virginia. And Stewart's treatment of the clause as a weighty but not controlling factor does not govern here: Atlantic Marine, applying Stewart to a clause of this kind, held that once the plaintiff's choice and the private-interest inputs drop out, the clause necessarily controls.

**Enforcement in this dispute.** The clause required the Subcontractor to sue in Norfolk; it sued in breach. The Contractor's alternative §1404(a) motion should have been granted, and the case transferred to the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia, Norfolk Division — which would merely give the Subcontractor one of the two fora it had promised to use. The denial of the motions to dismiss was correct; the denial of transfer, and the mandamus ruling resting on it, was not, and should be reversed.

**Limits.** (i) The clause fixes venue but confers no personal jurisdiction: a defendant haled into the Norfolk Circuit Court on it could still contest that court's jurisdiction, since the clause contains no consent-to-jurisdiction language. (ii) It selects no governing law; choice of law proceeds independently and is unaffected by transfer. (iii) Controlling weight presupposes validity — fraud in the inducement, serious inconvenience amounting to unreasonableness, or a contrary strong public policy would reopen the analysis, and none appears. (iv) The exclusive-§1404(a)/controlling-weight regime is clause-specific: it applies because this clause points to another federal forum; a clause selecting a state or foreign forum is enforced through forum non conveniens, with the factors balanced in the ordinary way. (v) Transfer may be denied only in the most exceptional case — where public-interest factors decisively show that holding the breaching plaintiff to its bargain would disserve the interests of justice — and nothing in this record approaches that threshold.
