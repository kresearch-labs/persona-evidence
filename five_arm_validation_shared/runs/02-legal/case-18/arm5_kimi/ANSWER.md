# Case 18 — Answer

## Issues Presented

1. **Scope of the "sole discretion" grant.** Section 8.06(a) expressly vests the Board with sole discretion over the *manner* of any sale (asset sale, merger, transfer of Membership Interests, or otherwise), subject to a single express condition: the buyer must be an independent, unaffiliated third party. The issue is whether any room remains for the implied covenant to regulate *how* the Board chooses among sale structures and processes.

2. **Whether the implied covenant can supply an affirmative market-check obligation.** The Member asks the court to imply a substantive term — a broad, open-market sale process with exposure to competing bids — that the agreement does not contain. The issue is whether the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing can be used to engraft such a term onto an agreement whose parties expressly addressed the sale issue.

3. **Effect of the fiduciary-duty elimination.** The agreement eliminated fiduciary duties "to the fullest extent permitted by law." Under 6 Del. C. § 18-1101, an LLC agreement may eliminate fiduciary duties entirely, but it *cannot* eliminate the implied contractual covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The issue is what that irreducible residual obligation requires when the agreement confers discretion.

## How the Clause Should Be Construed

- **The covenant survives but is narrow.** The elimination of fiduciary duties is effective, but the implied covenant remains as a mandatory backstop. It is, however, a "limited and extraordinary" gap-filling remedy — not a license to rewrite the contract or to impose duties the parties could have bargained for but did not (see *Nemec v. Shrader*, 991 A.2d 1120 (Del. 2010); *Dieckman v. Regency GP LP*, 145 A.3d 954 (Del. 2016)).

- **The agreement is not silent — it expressly addresses the sale.** The implied covenant operates only where the contract is truly silent or where a party is vested with discretion. Here the parties spoke directly to the sale question: they gave the Board sole discretion over the manner of sale and imposed one express limitation (an unaffiliated third-party buyer). Implying a market-check requirement would contradict the express grant of sole discretion and add a substantive term the parties omitted — precisely what the covenant forbids.

- **Discretion must still be exercised in good faith.** Where a contract confers discretion, the covenant requires that the discretion be exercised consistently with the parties' reasonable expectations — i.e., not arbitrarily, capriciously, in bad faith, or for an improper purpose (such as self-dealing or steering the company to an affiliated buyer). But it does not convert "sole discretion" into a duty to follow any particular sale process. The Member is therefore correct that the elimination of fiduciary duties does not vitiate the covenant — but wrong about what the covenant demands: it polices *bad-faith* exercises of discretion, not the Board's choice of a single-bidder negotiation.

## How It Should Be Enforced

The Court of Chancery's dismissal should be **affirmed**. The Board complied with the express terms of Section 8.06(a): it sold the company to an independent third-party buyer, the only condition the agreement imposed. The Member's open-market-process theory is an attempt to engraft a new term, not to fill a contractual gap, and the complaint pleads no facts suggesting the Board exercised its discretion in bad faith — no affiliation with the buyer, no self-dealing, no improper purpose. Absent such allegations, the implied covenant claim fails as a matter of law.

This construction and result track *Miller v. HCP & Co.*, 200 A.3d 1162 (Del. 2019), which affirmed dismissal of an identical implied-covenant challenge to a board's exercise of sole discretion over the manner of a sale under Section 8.06(a) of an LLC operating agreement.
