# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-18

**The issues:** (1) whether the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing can be used to impose a *specific process* (an open-market auction) on a sale whose *manner* the agreement vested in the Board's "sole discretion"; and (2) whether eliminating fiduciary duties "to the fullest extent permitted by law" (keeping only the contractual good-faith obligation) still leaves any constraint on the Board's sale conduct.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **The dismissal stands — the implied covenant cannot engraft an auction process onto an agreement that expressly allocated that decision to the Board's sole discretion.** Delaware law is settled: the implied covenant applies only to developments that the contract did not anticipate, and it cannot be invoked to add terms the parties negotiated — "a contract's express terms will control over any implied covenants." Section 8.06(a) squarely addresses the sale process ("the Board shall determine in its sole discretion the manner … whether by asset sale, merger, transfer of Membership Interests or otherwise"). Whether to shop the company or negotiate privately is quintessentially the "manner" of sale; a judicially mandated open-market process would be a new term, not an implied incident of the bargain. The Member's theory fails at this threshold — the covenant protects the *execution* of agreed allocations from arbitrary or bad-faith frustration, not the *wisdom* of discretionary choices.

2. **"Sole discretion" still means discretion exercised in good faith — but the standard is minimal here.** The Member is right that eliminating fiduciary duties doesn't extinguish the express contractual covenant the parties retained. But the covenant's constraint in a sole-discretion clause is narrow: the Board cannot exercise its discretion in a way that *defeats the contract's purpose*, act in bad faith (subjectively intending harm or improper motive), or use its discretion to extract benefits the contract assigns to others. An exclusive negotiation with an unaffiliated private-equity buyer, culminating in a sale of the entire company — the transaction the provision contemplates — does not violate the covenant even if a broader auction might have yielded more. Delaware courts repeatedly refuse to imply a market check where the agreement gives sole discretion over process (contrast fiduciary-out sales, where Revlon duties apply because the directors owe fiduciary duties — duties these parties negotiated away within Delaware's permissive limits).

3. **The contractual architecture is the answer.** These parties bargained for exactly this regime: fiduciary duties eliminated to the legal maximum, good-faith covenant substituted, sale manner in sole discretion. Delaware permits that bargain, and the covenant cannot be stretched back into a fiduciary duty the contract disclaims — that would rewrite the deal.

**Practical ruling:** affirm dismissal. The implied covenant is preserved in this agreement but does no work here: exclusive negotiation followed by an arm's-length sale to an unaffiliated buyer lies within the Board's express "sole discretion" over the manner of sale, and no bad-faith conduct was pleaded.

**Key issues flagged:** implied covenant cannot add process terms to an express sole-discretion grant · good-faith review of sole discretion is minimal (purpose-defeating or improper-motive conduct only) · contractual fiduciary elimination honored in Delaware · no implied market check absent fiduciary duties (no Revlon by contract).
