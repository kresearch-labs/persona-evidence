**Issues presented.** (1) Whether the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing can be used to engraft Revlon-type open-market sale obligations — a broad process exposing the company to competing bids — onto an operating agreement whose express terms already address how a sale of the company is conducted. (2) Whether a "sole discretion" provision displaces the implied covenant or merely narrows the conduct the covenant polices. (3) Whether the contractual elimination of fiduciary duties "to the fullest extent permitted by law" forecloses an implied-covenant claim that would, in substance, reintroduce those duties.

**Construction.** Section 8.06(a) is an express term: it vests in the Board's sole discretion the manner in which a sale of all membership units to an independent third party occurs, "whether by asset sale, merger, transfer of Membership Interests or otherwise." Choosing between negotiating exclusively with one buyer and running an open-market auction is quintessentially part of the "manner" of sale. Under Delaware law the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is a narrow gap-filler: it implies only terms the parties would have agreed to had they bargained over the point, and it cannot override the express bargain or add new terms the parties declined to write. Because the parties expressly addressed the sale issue, there is no gap for the covenant to fill, and it may not be used to imply a market-check requirement. That the agreement expressly cabined the discretion in one respect — the buyer must be an unaffiliated third party — confirms the parties knew how to limit the discretion and did so selectively; implying further process obligations would rewrite their allocation, not fill a gap.

"Sole discretion" does not, however, create a covenant-free zone. The covenant survives — here expressly, through the proviso obligating the Board to act in accordance with the implied contractual obligation of good faith and fair dealing — and it governs how the Board exercises the discretion granted: the Board may not act arbitrarily, in bad faith, or with a purpose foreign to the contract (for example, selling to an affiliate, taking secret benefits from a buyer, or acting to strip the Member of the fruits of the bargain). The covenant polices the exercise of the discretion; it does not supply a precondition to exercising it.

**Enforcement.** The elimination of fiduciary duties does not extinguish the claim, because the implied covenant is a contractual obligation that Delaware law does not allow an LLC agreement to eliminate (6 Del. C. § 18-1101) and that the parties here expressly preserved; and vesting sole discretion did not relieve the Board of its obligation to exercise that discretion consistently with the covenant. But the fiduciary waiver is decisive in the other direction: the covenant cannot be used to reintroduce the fiduciary-style, Revlon-type sale-process duties the parties eliminated. On the facts pleaded — exclusive negotiation culminating in a sale to an unaffiliated private-equity buyer, a manner of sale squarely within the Board's granted discretion, and no allegation of bad faith, self-dealing, fraud, kickbacks, or arbitrary conduct beyond the absence of a market check — the Member states no claim. A broad, open-market sale process is not an implied term of a contract whose express terms vested the manner of sale in the Board's sole discretion. The Court of Chancery's dismissal should be affirmed: the Board had to exercise its sole discretion in good faith and deal fairly, but it was not required to conduct an open-market sale process before selling to the buyer it chose.

**Bottom line:** the implied covenant survives the fiduciary waiver and acts as a backstop against bad-faith exercise of the Board's sole discretion, but it cannot be used to imply Revlon-type market-check obligations that the operating agreement's express terms allocate to the Board — so the dismissal is affirmed.
