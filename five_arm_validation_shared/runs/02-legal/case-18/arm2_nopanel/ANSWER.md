**Bottom line:** The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing survives the agreement's elimination of fiduciary duties, but it operates only as a gap-filler; because Section 8.06(a) expressly addresses the manner of sale and vests that decision in the Board's sole discretion — subject only to the requirement of an unaffiliated third-party buyer — the covenant cannot be used to imply a broad open-market sale process. The Board's discretion is enforceable so long as it is not exercised arbitrarily, unreasonably, or in bad faith; absent well-pleaded facts of bad faith, self-dealing, or conduct that defeats the Member's contractual expectations, the Member's claim fails.

**Issues presented**

1. **Does the implied covenant apply at all to an agreement that eliminates fiduciary duties?** Yes. Under 6 Del. C. § 18-1101(e), an LLC agreement may expand, restrict, or eliminate fiduciary duties, but it may not eliminate the implied covenant; § 18-1101(c) makes the covenant a term of every LLC agreement. The proviso here — preserving only the obligation of good faith and fair dealing — confirms the parties treated the covenant as the exclusive residual check on Board conduct. The Member is therefore right that the covenant applies, but wrong about what it requires.

2. **Can the covenant be used to engraft a market-check requirement onto a provision that expressly addresses the sale process?** No. The covenant is a gap-filler: it implies terms the parties would have agreed to had they thought of the matter, but it cannot contradict, override, or supplement express terms (Dunlap v. State Farm Fire & Cas. Co., 878 A.2d 434 (Del. 2005); Kuroda v. SPJS Holdings, L.L.C., 971 A.2d 872 (Del. Ch. 2009)). Section 8.06(a) expressly addresses how a sale may occur — asset sale, merger, transfer of units "or otherwise" — and commits that decision to the Board's sole discretion. A requirement of a broad, open-market auction would rewrite that allocation, not fill a gap. Nor does the covenant impose a fiduciary-style duty to maximize price or solicit competing bids.

3. **Does "sole discretion" mean unreviewable discretion?** No — but review is narrow. Discretion under a contract is exercised subject to the covenant, which bars arbitrary, unreasonable, or bad-faith action and action taken with a purpose foreign to the contract (e.g., to divest the counterparty of the fruits of the bargain). Courts do not second-guess the wisdom of a discretionary choice with hindsight; a bad outcome honestly reached is not a breach (Nemec v. Shrader, 991 A.2d 1120 (Del. 2010)). And since Ager v. Jane Street Group, LLC, 310 A.3d 1210 (Del. 2024), a covenant claim requires both a gap in the agreement and conduct that deprives the plaintiff of the agreement's benefits — bad faith alone, without a gap, is not enough.

4. **How does the fiduciary waiver affect the standard of review?** It channels all review into contract. The Member cannot recast the dispute as a fiduciary-duty claim or invoke entire-fairness scrutiny; the only enforceable standard is the covenant as incorporated by the proviso.

**Construction**

Section 8.06(a) should be read as a complete allocation of process control: the Board decides the manner of sale in its sole discretion, constrained only by (i) the express requirement that the buyer be an unaffiliated third party and (ii) the proviso incorporating the implied covenant. Read as a whole, the agreement makes the covenant the exclusive standard of Board conduct in a sale. The covenant's enforceable content here is therefore minimal: the Board must exercise its discretion honestly and for contractually legitimate ends — it may not sell to an affiliate, take secret benefits from the buyer, or act with the purpose of stripping the Member of the deal's fruits — but it is not obliged to run an auction, test the market, or obtain the best available price.

**Enforcement in this dispute**

The Member's theory fails as pleaded. It asks the court to imply a market-check term into an agreement whose parties expressly addressed the sale process, which Delaware law forbids. The Court of Chancery correctly dismissed. The Member's strongest point — that vesting sole discretion does not exempt the Board from the covenant — is correct as a matter of principle but unavailing on these facts: the complaint alleges only an exclusive negotiation, which is squarely within the Board's discretion, and identifies no gap and no bad-faith conduct depriving the Member of the contract's benefits. The claim should be dismissed, with leave to replead only if the Member can plead specific facts that the Board acted arbitrarily or in bad faith — for example, that the Board members were conflicted, received side benefits from the buyer, or negotiated for a purpose foreign to the agreement. The remedy for a member who wanted a market check is a bargain for one, not a covenant-imposed one.
