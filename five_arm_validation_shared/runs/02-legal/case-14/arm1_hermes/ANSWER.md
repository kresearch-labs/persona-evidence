# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-14

**The issues:** (1) whether "time being of the essence" in a contract where *neither party* tendered or signaled readiness by the settlement date lets one party declare the contract dead 18 days later while the other was equally unready; (2) whether the Sellers' post-deadline conduct — returning the deposit, then reselling at $11,500 — waived the time clause or estopped them; and (3) whether the Buyer's specific-performance claim requires proof that the *Buyer* was ready, willing, and able on the deadline.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **Specific performance requires the plaintiff's own readiness — that alone defeats the Buyer's claim as pleaded.** Specific performance is equitable and discretionary: the suitor must show it was ready, willing, and able to perform at the appointed time. The Buyer never tendered, never signaled readiness, and does not claim it was prepared to pay $9,200 within the sixty days. That failure alone justifies dismissing the bill — the chancellor's reason stands independently of anything the Sellers did.

2. **"Time is of the essence" cuts both ways, and the Sellers' delay does not waive it here.** A party asserting a time-of-the-essence clause must itself be ready to perform on the date; the Sellers were not, and their belated letter declared the contract dead only after both sides had sat on the deadline. But waiver/estoppel requires conduct by the *Sellers* inducing the Buyer to rely: a statement at signing that the Sellers did not know when their new house would be finished is, at most, an acknowledgment of uncertainty — not a representation that the sixty-day date would be extended or not enforced. Waiver of a time-of-the-essence clause generally requires clear, unequivocal conduct inconsistent with enforcement (e.g., accepting late performance, negotiating extensions). Returning the $500 *after* declaring termination — explicitly "so the Buyer would suffer no loss" — is the opposite: performance of the rescission, not inducement of delay. And estoppel needs detrimental reliance by the Buyer; none appears (the Buyer did nothing at all in reliance before the deadline).

3. **The equal-duty argument fails because the remedy is mutual, not reciprocal default.** If neither party was ready, either could have elected to keep the contract alive or end it; the Sellers' election, made promptly and followed by making the Buyer whole (deposit returned in full before resale), leaves the Buyer without equity. Specific performance also would be a poor fit: the Sellers had contracted with third parties at $11,500 (a $2,300 improvement), and money damages — here zero, since the deposit was returned — are adequate.

4. **The Buyers' better claim would have been the Sellers' own non-readiness defeats their time-of-the-essence election** — but that argument requires the Buyer to have been ready to settle itself; it was not, and cannot complain of a mutual failure it shares.

**Practical ruling:** affirm the dismissal — the Buyer, absent tenders or readiness within the essence period, cannot claim specific performance; the Sellers' declaration and refund were a lawful termination, and no waiver or estoppel arises from an uncertain construction-schedule comment.

**Key issues flagged:** equity demands plaintiff's readiness/willingness/ability · time-of-the-essence enforcement requires the electing party's own timely readiness · waiver needs unequivocal inconsistent conduct; anticipatory comments don't waive · adequate legal remedy defeats specific performance where the property has been resold.
