The clause is a closing provision that (i) fixes settlement "on or before" sixty days from signing, (ii) expressly makes time of the essence, (iii) conditions the Sellers' conveyance and delivery of possession on "payment as above provided of the unpaid purchase money," and (iv) contains a merger clause excluding every term or representation not written.

**Issues the clause presents**

1. Whether the sixty-day date, with time of the essence, is a binding deadline whose passage lets the Sellers terminate, or a mere target that the parties' mutual inaction — neither tendered within the period — waived.
2. Whether the Sellers' duties (possession, special-warranty deed) were concurrent conditions triggered only by the Buyer's payment, so the Sellers had no independent duty to tender performance on the date, or whether — as the Buyer argued — they owed an equal, independent duty to offer performance on the day fixed.
3. Whether the Seller's statement at signing, that he did not know when the Sellers' new house would be finished, waived the settlement date or estopped the Sellers, notwithstanding the merger clause and the absence of any pleaded reliance.
4. Whether the Buyer, which neither tendered the $8,700 balance nor signaled readiness within the period, can obtain specific performance, which requires proof of readiness, willingness, and ability to perform on the day fixed.
5. Whether the Sellers' post-date conduct — the attorney's termination letter eighteen days later, the refund of the $500, and the resale for $11,500 — effected termination under the clause or waived it.

**Construction**

Read as a whole, the clause sequences performance: the Buyer was to pay the balance in cash at settlement on or before day sixty; upon that payment the Sellers were to give possession of the entire premises and deliver the deed. The deed obligation is expressly "upon payment as above provided" — a concurrent condition, not an independent promise to tender a deed on the date. "Time being of the essence of this Agreement" binds both sides: the Buyer had to be ready to pay by day sixty; the Sellers had to be able to convey. The merger clause makes the written date the exclusive measure of the parties' obligations and excludes the oral statement; and in any event an expression of uncertainty about an unrelated future event (when the Sellers' house would be finished) is not the clear, unequivocal conduct needed to waive an express time-is-of-the-essence term, and estoppel fails without detrimental reliance — the Buyer did nothing for sixty days. The Buyer's "equal duty to offer performance" reading would strike "upon payment as above provided" from the clause; every word must be given effect, so the Sellers' performance fell due only upon tender of payment.

**Enforcement in this dispute**

Enforce the clause as written. Because time was of the essence, the Buyer's failure to tender or signal readiness by day sixty was a failure of its own performance that it cannot excuse by pointing to the Sellers' non-tender, which was not yet due absent payment. Specific performance therefore fails: it is an equitable, discretionary remedy, and the Buyer never showed it was ready, willing, and able to pay on the date fixed, so the chancellor properly dismissed the bill. The Sellers did not waive: they promptly — eighteen days — invoked the time-of-the-essence provision in writing, returned the deposit, and resold; those are acts of termination consistent with the clause, not conduct keeping the contract alive. Nor is there any forfeiture for equity to relieve: the deposit was returned and the resale for $11,500 more than made the Sellers whole, so the Buyer — who never tendered or signaled readiness — suffered no loss.

Disposition: the dismissal should be affirmed — under an express time-is-of-the-essence closing clause, a buyer seeking specific performance must show readiness, willingness, and ability to pay on the date fixed, and this Buyer neither tendered nor signaled readiness within the sixty days.
