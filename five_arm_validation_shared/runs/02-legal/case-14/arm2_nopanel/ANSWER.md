The clause presents four issues: (1) whether the sixty-day settlement date, made express by "time being of the essence," is a condition whose breach discharges the Sellers; (2) whether the Seller's signing-day statement — that he did not know when the Sellers' new house would be finished — waived that date or estopped the Sellers from insisting on it; (3) whether the Sellers' own failure to tender performance within the sixty days bars them from declaring the contract at an end; and (4) whether specific performance may be had by a Buyer who was not ready, willing, and able to settle on the date fixed.

Construction. The settlement provision is a valid and enforceable time-is-of-the-essence clause: the parties wrote the date into the contract ("on or before (60) Sixty days") and then added the express essence clause, so the date is material and non-settlement within it is a failure of a condition, not a mere delay inviting a demand for performance. Two features of the clause control the Buyer's counter-arguments. First, the deed covenant is expressly conditioned on payment — "upon payment as above provided of the unpaid purchase money, a deed ... shall be executed" — so the Sellers' obligation to convey never matured until the Buyer paid or at least manifested readiness to pay; nothing was due from the Sellers on the settlement date, and the premise of the Buyer's "equal duty to offer performance" argument fails, the clause itself making payment the act that had to come first. Second, the integration clause ("This Contract contains the final and entire Agreement ... neither they nor their Agents shall be bound by any terms, conditions or representations not herein written") excludes the signing-day remark from the bargain; that remark — an expression of uncertainty about when the Sellers' own new house would be finished — is not a promise that the Buyer might settle late. To the extent it is offered as a waiver rather than a term, waiver of an express time-of-the-essence provision must be clear and unequivocal and shown by conduct; an ambiguous collateral statement unaccompanied by any conduct inviting late settlement is not waiver. Nor is there estoppel: estoppel requires a representation, reliance, and detriment, and the Buyer neither changed position in reliance on the remark nor suffered detriment — it simply did nothing.

Enforcement. Because time was of the essence, the Buyer's failure to tender the price or signal readiness within the sixty days put it in default and discharged the Sellers. The Sellers' own silence during the period is no defense: their performance was by its terms dependent on payment, and a party in default cannot invoke the other side's non-performance of a concurrent condition to excuse its own. The Sellers' post-date conduct confirms rather than cures the default: eighteen days after the date their attorney declared the contract "of no further effect," the $500 was returned so the Buyer would "suffer no loss," and the property was promptly resold. The short delay in declaring termination at most waived a prompt declaration; it did not revive the Buyer's lapsed obligation, and the refund eliminated any forfeiture or liquidated-damages question (had the deposit been kept, its retention as earnest money would have needed justification; as returned, the Buyer has no loss to complain of, and the resale at $11,500 — a profit — shows the Sellers were not profiting at the Buyer's expense). Specific performance is equitable and presupposes a plaintiff who was ready, willing, and able to perform at the material time; the Buyer neither tendered nor signaled readiness within the period, and the chancellor's finding to that effect disposes of the bill. Equity follows the law of the contract and will not rewrite an expired essence date to rescue a party who slept on it.

Disposition. The clause should be construed as written — a sixty-day settlement date of the essence, with the Sellers' conveyance conditioned on payment. Enforced that way, the Buyer's non-settlement within the period terminated the contract; the waiver, estoppel, and "equal duty" arguments fail; the Sellers were entitled to declare the contract at an end and resell; and the dismissal of the bill for specific performance should be affirmed, the Buyer having been restored to its $500 deposit.
