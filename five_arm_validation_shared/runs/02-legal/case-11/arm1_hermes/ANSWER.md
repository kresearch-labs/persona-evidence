# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-11

**The issues:** (1) whether an anti-assignment clause reading "no payment under this annuity contract may be assigned in any manner by the payee" *defeats* an assignment or is merely a covenant whose breach sounds in damages; (2) how Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 322 and the modern anti-assignment case law classify such clauses; and (3) how the structured-settlement transfer statute interacts with privately bargained anti-assignment terms.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **The assignment stands.** The clause's operative words — "no payment … may be assigned" — are prohibitory in form but say nothing about the *legal consequence* of a violation: they neither state that an assignment made in violation "shall be void," "is invalid," nor that the obligor may treat it as an ineffective transfer. Under § 322(1) of the Restatement (Second) (and the modern majority position codified in UCC-style reforms and followed by courts construing common-law anti-assignment clauses), a term forbidding assignment operates as a **covenant not to assign, not as a limitation of the assignor's power**. Breach gives the obligor (the issuer) a damages claim; it does not undo the transfer as against the assignee, especially where the assignee neither knew of nor participated in any breach.

2. **The statutory framework reinforces the result.** The structured-settlement transfer statute's judicial-approval mechanism — best interests of the payee, fair and reasonable to all interested parties — exists precisely to police these transfers in the payee's protection; a court's approval after that hearing is the legislature's chosen safeguard. Nothing in the statute preserves issuers' anti-assignment clauses against approved transfers, and public policy in this market (factoring of structured-settlement streams) favors transferability. Reading the clause to nullify the court-approved transfer would also harm the payee the statute protects.

3. **The issuer's remedy is confined to its bargain.** If it suffered any loss from the breach of the covenant (e.g., administrative costs of redirecting payments), damages lie against the assignor — but it cannot block the payment redirection to the purchaser, and it remains obliged to make the payments as they come due to the entitled party.

4. **The "freely bargained clause" counterargument fails on drafting grounds:** had the issuer wanted assignments to be void, standard drafting says so ("any purported assignment shall be void"); the absence of consequence language is the tell that the clause was a covenant.

**Practical ruling:** the trial court's approval stands — the assignment is effective; the issuer's remedy is damages for breach of covenant, if any.

**Key issues flagged:** covenant-not-to-assign vs power-limiting clause (§ 322 test: does the clause invalidate the assignment?) · structured-settlement statutes as a public-policy overlay · consequence language ("void," "invalid") is decisive in drafting.
