# Case 11 — Answer

## Issues presented

The anti-assignment clause ("[n]o payment under this annuity contract may be assigned in any manner by the [payee]") presents three issues:

1. **Effect of the clause on the assignment's validity** — Does the clause render the claimant's transfer of payment rights void or ineffective, or does it merely give the annuity issuer a contract claim for breach?
2. **Interaction with the transfer statute** — Does the state statute requiring judicial approval of structured-settlement transfers (under a "best interests of the payee / fair and reasonable to all interested parties" standard) permit a court-approved transfer to proceed notwithstanding the clause, even absent express legislative intent to override anti-assignment provisions?
3. **The issuer's remedy** — If the transfer stands, what protection remains for the issuer that bargained for the clause?

## How the clause should be construed

Under settled case law and Section 322 of the Restatement (Second) of Contracts, a contractual term that merely *prohibits* assignment of a right is construed as a covenant not to assign — a promise whose breach gives the obligor a claim for damages — unless the clause goes further and expressly (a) limits the assignor's *power* to assign, or (b) states that any assignment shall be "void" or "ineffective." Courts distinguish between the *right* to assign (which such a clause restricts, sounding in damages) and the *power* to assign (which only explicit invalidating language can extinguish).

This clause contains no invalidating language. It says payments "may not be assigned," but it nowhere declares an assignment void, ineffective, or a nullity, and it does not purport to strip the payee of the power to assign. It must therefore be construed as a covenant not to assign, not as a provision that defeats the assignment itself.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

- The court-approved transfer **stands**. Because the clause does not invalidate assignments, the claimant's assignment to the purchaser is effective, and the trial court's approval under the statute — after finding the transfer in the payee's best interests and fair and reasonable to all interested parties — is not defeated by the clause. The statute's judicial-approval mechanism operates on the transfer regardless of whether the issuer consents.
- The issuer's protection is **limited to a damages claim** against the payee for breach of the covenant not to assign. In practice that claim is likely nominal: the issuer's obligations are unchanged — it owes the same payments on the same schedule, merely to the purchaser rather than the claimant — so it suffers little or no actual damage from the breach.
- The issuer's argument that its freely bargained clause is being "ignored" fails: the clause is enforced exactly as written. It was drafted only as a prohibition, and the parties could have — but did not — include language voiding assignments or limiting the payee's power to make them. The issuer received the remedy its drafting purchased, no more.

**Justification (one line):** Because the clause prohibits assignment without expressly voiding it or limiting the payee's power to assign, under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 322 it operates only as a covenant whose breach sounds in damages, so the statutorily approved transfer is effective and the issuer is remitted to a (likely nominal) damages claim.
