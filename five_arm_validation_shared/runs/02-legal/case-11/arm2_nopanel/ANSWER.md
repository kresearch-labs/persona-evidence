**Issues presented.**

1. What the clause "[n]o payment under this annuity contract may be assigned in any manner by the [payee]" actually does: is it (a) a mere covenant not to assign, breach of which gives the issuer a damages claim, or (b) a limitation on the payee's power to assign that renders an assignment ineffective?
2. Whether the assignment satisfies the general conditions of assignability — i.e., whether it materially changes the issuer's duty, materially increases its burden or risk, or materially impairs its chance of return performance or the value of performance to it.
3. What work the state structured-settlement protection act does: whether its judicial-approval requirement ("best interests of the payee... fair and reasonable to all interested parties") shows an intent that court-approved transfers override private anti-assignment terms, or is simply neutral, leaving ordinary assignment law to govern.
4. What the issuer's status as an "interested party" entitles it to in the statutory proceeding, and what remedy it has if the covenant is breached.

**Construction of the clause.**

The right to receive payments under an existing contract is freely assignable unless the assignment would materially change the obligor's duty, materially increase its burden or risk, or materially impair its chance of return performance or the value of performance to it. Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 317, 320, 322; accord UCC §§ 2-210, 9-404. Here none of those conditions is disturbed: the issuer owes the same payments, in the same amounts, on the same dates; only the payee's identity changes. The issuer's exposure is unchanged, and it gains a breach claim it would not otherwise have. So the transfer is of a kind the law favors.

The dispositive question is the clause's force. Anti-assignment language comes in three strengths: (i) a bare prohibition, which is a covenant not to assign; (ii) language limiting the assignor's power or declaring an assignment "void" or "of no effect"; and (iii) both. Only the second defeats an assignment. Section 322(2) states the rule: a contractual limitation on the assignor's right to assign is effective, but an assignment is not ineffective merely because it breaches the contract; the comment to § 322 confirms that invalidating effect requires express language such as "any assignment shall be void." This clause contains prohibition only — "may [not] be assigned in any manner" — with no voiding, invalidating, or power-limiting words. "In any manner" broadens the scope of the promise; it does not supply the missing invalidating language. The clause should therefore be construed strictly — and, to the extent ambiguous, against the issuer as its drafter, which could easily have added "any assignment shall be void" and did not. It is a covenant not to assign: it restricts the payee's right (exposing him to liability) but not his power, so the assignee takes an effective transfer subject to the covenant's consequences.

The statute does not alter this. The structured-settlement transfer act is consumer-protection legislation: it channels payees' sales of payment streams through a judicial "best interests / fair and reasonable" hearing to guard payees against improvident factoring. It presupposes that transfers can be effective — otherwise there would be nothing to approve — and its "all interested parties" language gives the issuer notice and a voice, not a veto. Nothing in it expresses an intent to enforce issuers' anti-assignment clauses by invalidation; equally, nothing in it overrides private bargains. Ordinary assignment law supplies the rule of decision, and under that law the covenant reading governs.

**Enforcement in this dispute.**

The clause is enforced — as a covenant. The claimant's assignment breached it, but the breach does not defeat the assignment: the trial court's approved transfer stands, and the issuer must make the payments to the purchaser as the approved assignee; it may not refuse performance or collaterally attack the approved transfer on covenant grounds. The issuer's protection is the remedy for breach — an action against its own contracting party, the claimant, for damages caused by the assignment. Because the issuer's duty, timing, and amount are unchanged, those damages may well be nominal; that is the consequence of the clause the issuer itself drafted. The issuer's fairness objections are heard, if at all, in the statutory proceeding as an interested party — they inform the "fair and reasonable" findings, but they do not convert the covenant into a power to block the transfer.

**Bottom line.** The assignment stands: the clause is a covenant not to assign rather than an invalidating restriction, so the court-approved transfer is effective and the issuer's remedy is a damages claim against the claimant for breach.
