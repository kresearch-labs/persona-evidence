# Case 02 — Answer

**Short answer.** The clause is an enforceable liquidated-damages provision, not a penalty, and the Sellers' prompt resale at a higher price was permissible mitigation — not a waiver — so the Sellers may retain the $17,750 deposit and the Buyers are not entitled to a refund.

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Liquidated damages or unenforceable penalty?** The clause fixes the remedy in advance (retention of the deposit). It is enforceable only if (i) at the time of contracting the Seller's actual loss on a buyer default was difficult to estimate, and (ii) the stipulated amount was a reasonable forecast of probable loss, not grossly disproportionate to it (the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356 test; the same two-part inquiry governs earnest-money deposits in most jurisdictions). A term whose real function is to compel performance by threat, or that bears no relation to possible loss, is a penalty and will not be enforced.
2. **Forfeiture and windfall.** Even a valid stipulation takes the Buyer's money without proof of loss, so the court must ask whether enforcing it in these circumstances produces a windfall — here, where the Sellers resold at $360,000, above the $355,000 contract price, and quantified no actual damages.
3. **Waiver, mitigation, and election of remedies.** Did the Sellers, by marketing the property and accepting a backup offer before closing, waive the clause or discharge the Buyers? Relatedly, does successful mitigation (a better-priced resale) defeat the stipulated remedy?
4. **Construction of the trigger and scope.** What counts as "fail[ure] to fulfill the Buyer's agreements herein," and does the express phrase "as liquidated damages" distinguish this deposit from an ordinary returnable escrow?

## How the clause should be construed

- **Plain meaning.** The trigger is any failure of the Buyer to perform; the consequence is that the Seller retains the deposit "as liquidated damages." That express label shows the parties intended a stipulated, in-lieu-of-damages remedy — not a down payment held subject to return if the deal failed.
- **Reasonableness judged at the time of contracting.** In August 1994 the Sellers' loss on a default was genuinely speculative: resale value in an uncertain market, carrying and financing costs, resale expenses, and the risk of a lower price. A 5% earnest-money deposit ($17,750 on $355,000) is the customary, moderate figure in residential purchase-and-sale agreements and is a reasonable forecast of such loss — not grossly disproportionate, hence not a penalty.
- **Unilateral form is not fatal.** The clause runs against the Buyer only, but that is standard in form agreements: it secures the Buyer's obligations without excusing the Seller's, and it does not make retention of a customary deposit punitive.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

- **The trigger occurred.** The Buyers essentially conceded they were not ready, willing, and able to close on September 1, 1994 — a repudiation squarely within "fail to fulfill the Buyer's agreements herein."
- **No waiver.** The August 9 authorization to market the property came from the Buyers' own attorney; the August 24 backup offer was expressly contingent on the Buyers' nonperformance, preserving the Sellers' rights against them; and closing with the third party on September 20 was consistent with treating the Buyers as in breach. Marketing with the Buyer's blessing and taking a backup contract is diligence, not an election that released the defaulting Buyers.
- **The higher resale price does not defeat the clause.** The law *requires* mitigation, so a seller who resells quickly and well should not forfeit the benefit of the parties' bargain. The clause exists precisely because damages on default are hard to prove — resale timing, market movement, and costs are speculative — and it was meant to spare both sides that litigation. Requiring the Sellers to prove zero or nominal actual damages before keeping the deposit would read "as liquidated damages" out of the agreement; hindsight that the resale happened to yield a gain does not retroactively convert a reasonable stipulation into a penalty.
- **Equity does not rescue a defaulting party** from a reasonable stipulated consequence absent unconscionability, excusable neglect, or Seller wrongdoing — none appears.

**Conclusion.** The clause should be construed as a valid liquidated-damages provision and enforced as written: the Sellers retain the $17,750 deposit, summary judgment for the Sellers is affirmed, and the Buyers take nothing.
