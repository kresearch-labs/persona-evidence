The clause presents four issues: (1) whether retaining the full $17,750 deposit is an enforceable liquidated-damages provision or an unenforceable penalty; (2) whether its trigger — "fail to fulfill the Buyer's agreements herein" — reaches the Buyers' conceded failure to close, and, conversely, whether its breadth to any breach however minor threatens its enforceability; (3) whether the Sellers' resale within weeks at $360,000 — five thousand dollars above the contract price — and their failure to quantify any actual loss require a refund or offset of the deposit; and (4) whether the Buyers' August 9 letter authorizing marketing, or the Sellers' acceptance of a backup offer expressly contingent on the Buyers' nonperformance, waived the clause.

Construction. The provision is a liquidated-damages clause, not a penalty. Under the settled two-part test (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356), enforceability is judged as of the time of contracting: the Sellers' loss on a buyer default — resale price, carrying costs, and market movement between breach and resale — was not readily ascertainable in advance, and a deposit of five percent of the purchase price, the exact sum acknowledged in the agreement, was a reasonable and proportionate forecast of that loss and the customary stake in a residential transaction. That the Sellers in fact resold at a profit does not retroactively convert the clause into a penalty; hindsight is not the measure. Read plainly, "fail to fulfill the Buyer's agreements herein" covers every unperformed Buyer obligation, and the failure to close on or before September 1 — conceded here — is squarely within it; the clause's reach over minor defaults raises a proportionality question only for trivial breaches, not for a total failure to purchase. The August 9 letter authorized marketing but expressly reaffirmed the Buyers' duty to close ("they would still close as agreed") and neither waived nor modified the deposit provision; accepting a backup offer contingent on the Buyers' nonperformance preserved the Buyers' chance to perform and was consistent with, not a waiver of, the clause.

Enforcement. The Sellers are entitled to retain the $17,750. A liquidated-damages clause that was reasonable when made is enforced as written upon breach; the promisee need not prove actual damages, so the absence of quantified loss and the above-price resale neither defeat the clause nor compel an offset. The Buyers' contrary argument — that retention without proven loss is a windfall — would convert liquidated damages into after-the-fact compensation, precisely what the clause by design avoids, and would perversely penalize the Sellers for mitigating promptly. Because the Buyers repudiated and the Sellers resold within weeks, enforcement works no disproportionate forfeiture.

Bottom line: summary judgment for the Sellers should be affirmed — the clause is a valid liquidated-damages provision that applies to the Buyers' conceded failure to close, so the Sellers keep the $17,750 deposit without proving actual damages or accounting for the resale profit.
