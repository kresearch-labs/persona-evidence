# Case 02 — Answer

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Enforceability vs. penalty.** Whether a clause letting the Seller retain the Buyer's deposit as "liquidated damages" upon the Buyer's default is a valid liquidated damages provision or an unenforceable penalty.
2. **Time of assessment ("single look" vs. "second look").** Whether enforceability is judged as of contract formation (a reasonable forecast of anticipated damages) or re-examined after the breach in light of the actual damages (if any) the Seller suffered.
3. **Effect of a quick, higher-priced resale.** Whether the fact that the Sellers resold within days for $5,000 *more* than the contract price — and quantified no actual damages — entitles the defaulting Buyers to a refund despite the clause.
4. **Burden of proof.** Who must show the clause is (or is not) a penalty.
5. **Construction/ambiguity.** Whether the clause's language covers this default and the retained deposit.

## How the clause should be construed

- The clause is **clear and unambiguous**: if the Buyer fails to fulfill his agreements, the Seller retains "all deposits" as liquidated damages. The Buyers concededly repudiated — they were not ready to close on September 1 — so by its plain terms the clause applies.
- A liquidated damages provision is valid if, **at the time of contracting**, (a) actual damages from a breach would be difficult to estimate, and (b) the agreed amount is a **reasonable forecast** of the damages expected to result from the breach. Both are satisfied here: damages from a buyer's default on a residential sale are uncertain at signing (carrying costs, market movement, resale timing and price are all unknown), and a deposit of **five percent of the purchase price ($17,750 on $355,000)** is customary in residential real estate and plainly proportionate — not grossly disproportionate to any anticipated loss.
- Enforceability is assessed under the **"single look" doctrine**: the clause stands or falls on the circumstances at contract formation, not on a retrospective comparison with actual damages. The court should **decline the "second look" approach**, under which a breaching party could escape the clause whenever the non-breaching party turns out to suffer little or no actual loss. Requiring proof of actual damages would nullify the very purpose of a liquidated damages clause — certainty and avoidance of damage litigation — and would shift to sellers risks the buyers agreed to bear.
- The **burden rests on the party challenging the clause — the Buyers** — to show it is an unenforceable penalty. They cannot carry it merely by pointing to the Sellers' prompt, higher-priced resale; a 5% deposit is not disproportionate, and the Sellers' good fortune in reselling does not convert a reasonable forecast into a penalty.
- The Buyers' August 9 letter authorizing the Sellers to market the property, and the Sellers' resale contract expressly contingent on the Buyers' nonperformance, show the Sellers acted properly to mitigate; mitigation does not waive or diminish their contractual right to retain the deposit.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

The clause should be **enforced as written**: the Sellers may **retain the entire $17,750 deposit** as liquidated damages, and the Buyers are entitled to **no refund**, notwithstanding the resale at $360,000 and the absence of quantified actual damages. Summary judgment for the Sellers should be **affirmed**.

**Justification (one line):** A liquidated damages clause fixing a customary 5% deposit is a reasonable forecast of hard-to-estimate damages judged at the time of contracting, and under the single-look rule the defaulting Buyers — who bear the burden of proving a penalty — cannot reclaim it merely because the Sellers resold quickly at a higher price.
