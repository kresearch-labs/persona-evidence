# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-02

**The issues:** (1) whether a liquidated-damages clause permitting retention of a 5% deposit is enforceable where the seller resold within days at a higher price and proved no actual loss; (2) whether the clause is a penalty rather than a reasonable liquidation of damages; and (3) whether the seller's duty to mitigate or the actual resale outcome limits the retained deposit.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **The clause is enforceable as liquidated damages, not a penalty — on these facts.** At contract formation, 5% of a residential purchase price ($17,750 on $355,000) is a reasonable, good-faith estimate of the seller's anticipated harm from a default: carrying costs, lost market time, price uncertainty, and the risk of a lower resale. The parties could not know in advance how the market would move; a fixed-percentage deposit is the conventional, uniformly accepted device in real-estate purchase agreements and carries a strong presumption of validity.

2. **The buyers' actual-loss argument fails on these undisputed facts.** The buyers conceded default (repudiation before closing). That the sellers resold days later at $360,000 — $5,000 *above* the contract price — does not convert the retained deposit into a penalty: the clause is a bargained-for risk allocation, and the sellers were entitled to the deposit by its terms without proving out-of-pocket loss. The buyers' windfall argument inverts the doctrine — liquidated damages do not require proof of loss; they require only that the amount was a reasonable forecast at formation and the breach was not willful, oppressive, or in bad faith.

3. **No mitigation failure.** The sellers marketed, accepted a backup offer contingent on the buyers' non-performance, and closed within three weeks; if anything, the prompt resale at a higher price confirms the sellers' good faith and that the deposit was not a windfall substitute for damages.

4. **The caveat the clause carries:** had the market collapsed, or had the sellers' resale been at a *lower* price with the deposit *plus* damages claimed (double recovery), or had the breach been trivial/technical, courts would trim the retention. None of that is this case.

**Practical ruling:** summary judgment for the sellers is affirmed in substance — the buyers repudiated, the clause is a reasonable liquidated-damages provision, and the seller's profitable resale does not require refunding the deposit.

**Key issues flagged:** liquidated damages vs penalty · reasonableness measured at formation, not at breach · no actual-loss requirement for a valid liquidation · seller's mitigation satisfied by prompt resale.
