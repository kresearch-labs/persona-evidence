# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-03

**The issues:** (1) whether the "reimbursement" clause — 1½× the prior year's fees for any former client served within 18 months — is an enforceable liquidated-damages mechanism or an invalid restrictive covenant (penalty) disguised as one; (2) whether labeling the employee a "fiduciary" by contract recital creates the confidentiality premise the clause rests on; and (3) whether the clause is severable so the remainder of the agreement survives.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **It is an invalid restrictive covenant in liquidated-damages clothing.** The clause's real effect is to make it financially prohibitive for the employee to serve *any* former Buffalo-office client for 18 months — a de facto non-compete/non-solicitation measured not by the firm's provable loss but by a fixed multiplier of historical fees. Restrictive covenants on professionals are tested for reasonableness (protectable interest, temporal/geographic scope, hardship to the employee, public benefit); this clause flunks the test because it (a) contains no requirement that the employee actually *solicit* clients or use confidential information — serving a client who independently seeks him out triggers the same payment; (b) taxes personal clients the employee brought to the firm through his own outside contacts; and (c) imposes liability in a fixed 1½×-fee multiple with no relationship to any actual damage.

2. **The recited "fiduciary relationship" cannot manufacture the premise.** A promotion-conditioned acknowledgment that disclosures give the employee an advantage does not establish that he in fact received trade secrets or unique client relationships; the firm's summary-judgment submission offered no evidence of solicitation or use of confidential information. A party cannot avoid restrictive-covenant scrutiny by labeling the obligation "liquidated damages" — courts look to substance, and a payment triggered by any client contact regardless of fault is a restraint.

3. **The clause is a penalty.** Damages equal to 1½ times a full year's fees per client, payable in five annual installments, bears no relation to the firm's provable loss in a client-following case (typically the lost profits on that client's work, if any). A stipulation grossly disproportionate to anticipated harm and payable irrespective of loss is a penalty — unenforceable as such.

4. **Blue-pencil the restraint, save the agreement.** The severability framing the appellate court used — invalidating the *entire* agreement — overshoots: the restrictive/reimbursement clause should be struck and the remainder of the Manager's Agreement (employment terms through promotion) stands, since the firm's legitimate protections (actual trade secrets, client lists held by the firm, standard non-solicitation lawfully drafted) remain available to it.

**Practical ruling:** summary judgment for the employee is correct — the reimbursement clause is an unenforceable restraint/penalty; the firm recovers nothing without proving solicitation and actual damages under ordinary principles.

**Key issues flagged:** liquidated damages vs restrictive covenant (substance over label) · fiduciary recital cannot create the protectable interest · penalty doctrine (disproportionate, loss-independent recovery) · severability saves the rest of the agreement.
