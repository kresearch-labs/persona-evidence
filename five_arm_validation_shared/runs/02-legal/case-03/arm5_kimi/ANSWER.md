# Answer — Case 03

**Issues presented.** The clause presents three issues: (1) whether a "reimbursement" provision requiring a departing employee to pay the firm 1½ times prior-year fees for serving former clients is a restrictive covenant subject to the common-law rule of reason, or merely an enforceable liquidated-damages mechanism; (2) if it is a restrictive covenant, whether it is reasonable — i.e., whether it protects a legitimate employer interest, imposes no undue hardship on the employee, and is not injurious to the public; and (3) if it is overbroad in part, whether it may be partially enforced rather than invalidated in its entirety.

**Construction.** The clause is a restrictive covenant and must be construed as such. Although drafted as a compensation or liquidated-damages provision, its purpose and effect are anticompetitive: it exacts a substantial financial penalty (1½ times annual fees, over five installments) if the employee serves any former client of the Buffalo office within 18 months of leaving. A restraint does not escape the rule of reason merely because it is framed as reimbursement rather than as an outright prohibition on competition. The label the parties chose is not controlling; the court looks to the clause's substance and practical effect.

**Application of the rule of reason.** The firm has a legitimate interest in protecting client relationships that the employee was able to develop and maintain through the firm's resources, name, and goodwill — that is the interest the recited "fiduciary" acknowledgment and the advantage conferred by the firm's disclosures were meant to secure. But the clause sweeps far beyond that interest: it reaches (a) personal clients the employee brought to the firm through his own outside contacts when his practice merged into it, and (b) clients he never serviced or for whom he was not the primary representative. As to those clients the firm has no legitimate protectible interest, and the restraint is therefore unreasonable and overbroad in part. Notably, the firm offered no evidence of solicitation or misuse of confidential information, so its claim rests entirely on the clause itself.

**Enforcement.** The overbreadth does not doom the entire agreement. The clause should be partially enforced: the offending applications are severable, and the covenant may be enforced to the extent it reaches clients of the firm whom the employee came to know and serve through the firm's auspices, while being denied enforcement as to his personal clients and clients he did not service. Total invalidation (as the courts below ordered) was error; so would be total enforcement.

**Disposition in this dispute.** Neither side is entitled to summary judgment. Triable issues of fact exist as to which of the 100 listed clients were the employee's personal clients, which clients he actually served after leaving, which accounts he primarily serviced while at the firm, and the resulting amount, if any, of recoverable fees. The order invalidating the entire agreement should be reversed, both summary-judgment motions denied, and the matter remitted for further proceedings consistent with partial enforcement.

Justification: This tracks the holding of BDO Seidman v. Hirshberg, 93 N.Y.2d 382 (1999), on whose facts this case is based — an accountant's client-based reimbursement clause is a restrictive covenant reviewed for reasonableness, overbroad as to the employee's personal clients, but partially enforceable as to firm-acquired clients, with factual disputes precluding summary judgment.
