# Case 03 — Answer

**The "SIXTH" paragraph is a restrictive covenant (a client-service covenant backed by a liquidated-damages formula), and under New York law it should be enforced in part, not invalidated in whole: it is valid and enforceable only as to former clients the employee serviced for the firm or brought in through firm-based contacts, and unenforceable as to personal clients he attracted through his own outside reputation and contacts.** (This is the fact pattern of *BDO Seidman, L.L.P. v. Hirshberg*, 93 N.Y.2d 382 (1999).)

## Issues presented

1. **Validity of the covenant** — Is the clause an enforceable restraint protecting the firm's legitimate interests, or an unreasonable restraint on the employee's ability to practice his profession?
2. **Liquidated damages or penalty** — Is the payment of 1½ times the client's prior year's fees a reasonable pre-estimate of the firm's loss, or a punitive forfeit?
3. **Effect of the fiduciary acknowledgment** — Does the employee's express acknowledgment that a fiduciary relationship arose from his receipt of firm disclosures extend the firm's protectable interest to *every* former client of the Buffalo office?
4. **Scope of protectable clients** — Which clients may the firm charge him for serving: all 100 on its discovery list, or only those with an employment-derived connection to him?
5. **Summary judgment** — On this record (the firm's list with no proof of solicitation; the employee's sworn denials and claims of personal clients), which side should prevail?

## Governing principles

- New York enforces employee covenants only to the extent necessary to protect legitimate interests: trade secrets/confidential information and the employer's trade connections (goodwill). *Purchasing Assocs. v. Weitz*, 13 N.Y.2d 267 (1963); *Karpinski v. Ingrasci*, 28 N.Y.2d 45 (1971); *Reed, Roberts Assocs. v. Strauman*, 40 N.Y.2d 303 (1976). Reasonableness is judged in the context of the particular employment.
- In the accounting setting, the firm's protectable interest is its goodwill with clients the employee **serviced** or helped develop **on the firm's behalf**. A covenant may not bar the employee from serving clients with whom he has no employment-derived connection; clients he brought in through his own extra-employment contacts and personal reputation remain his to serve — the firm has no legitimate interest in those relationships.
- Liquidated damages are enforceable when they bear a reasonable relation to anticipated loss and are not plainly disproportionate; a measure tied to a year's fees for a lost client is a conventional proxy for lost recurring business.
- An overbroad covenant should be partially enforced (its valid core blue-penciled) rather than voided outright, where the valid portion is separable.

## How the clause should be construed

- **Nature and trigger.** By its terms this is a *service* covenant, not a mere non-solicitation clause: liability is triggered by "serving" any former client within 18 months. Consequently the firm need not prove solicitation or misuse of confidential information to invoke it — but it must prove, client by client, the employment-based connection that makes a client "protected."
- **Duration and reach.** Eighteen months, limited to clients of the Buffalo office, is facially reasonable in time and reach.
- **Fiduciary acknowledgment.** The recital supports the firm's interest in protecting clients about whom the employee received firm disclosures, but it cannot enlarge the covenant to cover clients with which the employee had no connection. An acknowledgment of a fiduciary relationship does not convert every former client into a protected one.
- **Damages formula.** 1½ times one fiscal year's fees is not a per se penalty; it bears a reasonable relation to the value of the lost client relationship, and the five-installment schedule does not make it punitive. The formula, however, can be applied only to protected clients.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

- The lower courts' outright invalidation of the entire agreement was too broad; the covenant has a valid, separable core.
- The covenant should be declared enforceable **only** as to (i) clients the employee serviced while employed by the firm and (ii) clients generated through his solicitation on the firm's behalf (firm-based contacts). It is unenforceable as to clients he brought in through his own outside contacts and reputation, and as to clients he never dealt with.
- The employee's sworn averments — that some listed clients were personal clients he brought to the firm, that as to others he was not the primary servicing representative, and that he did not serve some at all — raise genuine fact issues requiring a client-by-client inquiry; on this record neither party was entitled to summary judgment.
- **Proper disposition:** reverse the invalidation of the agreement; enforce it in part; and remit for a hearing to identify the protected clients and to apply the 1½-times-fees liquidated damages only to those clients.

**Bottom line:** The clause presents restrictive-covenant and liquidated-damages validity questions; it should be construed as a goodwill-protection measure and enforced in part — against serving, within 18 months of departure, clients the employee serviced or developed for the firm, at 1½ times the prior year's fees — but not against his own personal clients.
