# Case 03 — Answer

**Character of the clause.** Paragraph SIXTH is in substance a restrictive covenant, not a mere debt instrument. Although the firm defends it as a lawful liquidated-damages mechanism, a provision that lets the Employee serve a former client only at a stipulated price — 1½ times the client's prior-year fees, in five annual installments — functions as a forfeiture imposed on competing, and so must be tested by the reasonableness standards governing covenants not to compete (Reed, Roberts Assocs. v. Strauman, 40 N.Y.2d 303; Columbia Ribbon & Carbon Mfg. v. A-1-A Corp., 42 N.Y.2d 496). The firm's anticipated reply — that the parties stipulated the amount precisely to avoid litigating damages, and that a bargained-for acknowledgment of a fiduciary relationship shows arm's-length agreement — does not change the analysis: the Employee's contractual acknowledgment cannot enlarge the categories of protectable interests the law recognizes, and a label of "liquidated damages" cannot exempt from reasonableness review a clause whose operation is to restrain client service.

**Issues presented.**

1. *Characterization.* Is the reimbursement obligation an invalid restraint on the Employee's practice or an enforceable liquidated-damages provision? Because it operates as a price on competing, it is a restrictive covenant subject to reasonableness review.

2. *Legitimate interests.* What may the firm protect? Only trade secrets, confidential client information, and client relationships the Employee developed or serviced on the firm's behalf. The recital of a "fiduciary relationship" premised on disclosures giving him "an advantage in attracting the firm's clients" supports protection only of relationships and information actually derived from the employment; it does not convert every former client of the Buffalo office into a protectable asset.

3. *Overbreadth.* As written, the clause reaches all former Buffalo-office clients without regard to the Employee's role. It is overbroad in two respects: (a) it covers clients he brought to the firm through his own outside contacts — personal relationships developed independently of the employment, whose goodwill is not the firm's to appropriate by contract; and (b) it covers clients he never serviced and about whom he acquired no confidential information, as to whom the firm has no legitimate interest requiring protection.

4. *Duration and measure.* The 18-month period is facially reasonable. The measure — 1½ times the last fiscal year's fees, payable over five years — approximates the value of the lost patronage and, as applied to covered clients, is not facially a penalty; the installments mitigate hardship.

5. *Remedy and procedure.* Does overbreadth require invalidating the entire agreement, or may the covenant be enforced in modified form? And was summary judgment proper? The Employee's averments — that some listed clients were personal clients he brought in, that as to others he was not the primary representative servicing the account, and that he denied serving some — raise triable issues as to which clients fall within the protected categories; summary judgment disposing of the whole agreement was therefore premature on the question of coverage.

**Construction and enforcement.**

The covenant should be construed narrowly and enforced partially. It is enforceable only to the extent it applies to clients (1) for whom the Employee had material contact while employed by the firm — i.e., whom he serviced as the firm's representative — or (2) about whom he acquired trade secret or confidential client information. It is unenforceable as to clients he brought to the firm through his own personal contacts developed outside the employment, and as to clients with whom he had no material contact and about whom he learned nothing confidential.

Because the defect is overbreadth rather than illegality of purpose, the court should not invalidate the agreement wholesale; the covenant is severable and may be judicially narrowed to its reasonable core. The proper disposition is to reverse the appellate court's wholesale invalidation, declare the covenant enforceable only as to the two protected categories, and remit for a client-by-client determination of which of the 100 listed clients are covered — the firm bearing the burden of showing material contact or trade-secret information as to each client it pursues. The absence of proof of actual solicitation or misuse of confidential information does not defeat the firm: as to clients within the protected categories, the covenant is enforceable by its terms, because the law permits protection of the client relationships the Employee developed or serviced on the firm's behalf, not merely of secrets. As to covered clients, an Employee who chooses to serve them within 18 months owes the stipulated compensation; as to uncovered clients, no amount of proof would justify enforcement.

This is, in clause type, posture, and holding, BDO Seidman v. Hirshberg, 93 N.Y.2d 382, 690 N.Y.S.2d 854, 712 N.E.2d 1220 (1999) — the same provision (an accounting firm's Manager's Agreement requiring reimbursement for serving former clients) reviewed on summary judgment and partially enforced rather than invalidated wholesale.
