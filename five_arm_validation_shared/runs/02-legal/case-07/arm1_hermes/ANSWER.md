# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-07

**The issues:** (1) whether a general change-of-terms clause ("We will notify you of any change in the terms, rates, or fees as required by law") authorizes a financial institution to unilaterally *insert an entirely new arbitration-and-class-waiver provision* into an existing account agreement years later; (2) whether silence plus continued account use constitutes assent to a new, substantive, rights-stripping term; and (3) whether the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing limits the amendment power.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **A change-of-terms clause permits changes to terms — not the insertion of a fundamentally new dispute-resolution regime.** The clause's plain language covers changes to existing "terms, rates, or fees." Arbitration and class-action waiver provisions are not "terms, rates, or fees" of the deposit relationship; they are a new contract about litigation — surrendering the right to a jury, to class relief, and to court process. Construing the clause to allow its unilateral insertion reads into it a power the parties did not write, in derogation of the principle that agreements to arbitrate arise from consent, and any ambiguity is construed against the drafter (the Credit Union).

2. **Silence cannot supply assent to a new arbitration agreement.** Arbitration is a matter of contract, and contract requires mutual assent. For *minor* term changes in ongoing service relationships (fee schedules, rates), notice-plus-continued-use works because the change operates within the original bargain's subject matter. A wholesale new arbitration provision is different in kind: forcing a member to arbitrate or lose the account's dispute framework is not a "change" to her checking terms but a de novo waiver of forum rights, which requires her signature or affirmative opt-in — not her failure to opt out. The opt-out mechanism proves the Credit Union knew consent was the issue; it cannot invert into deemed consent.

3. **Good faith limits the power's scope, as the Member argues.** Even where change-of-terms clauses are broad, courts police amendments that strip substantial accrued rights or restructure the parties' remedies in ways the original bargain never contemplated. Inserting binding arbitration years into a checking account, timed while a class action over overdraft fees was pending, is a use of the amendment power against the drafter's own customers' litigation interests — precisely where the implied covenant does limiting work.

4. **The governing-law clause is irrelevant to this question** — it addresses venue, not the amendment power.

**Practical ruling:** the Member is not bound; the change-of-terms provision does not authorize adding a new arbitration/class-waiver regime to an agreement that never had one, and her silence was not assent. The stay motion should be denied.

**Key issues flagged:** scope of unilateral change-of-terms powers · new arbitration provisions require genuine assent · opt-out ≠ opt-in for substantive rights-stripping amendments · implied covenant constrains amendment timed to pending litigation.
