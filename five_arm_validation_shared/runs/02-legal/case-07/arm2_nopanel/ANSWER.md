The clause presents a unilateral-amendment problem: a generally worded change-of-terms power, no stated mechanism by which changes become binding, and an attempt — six and a half years into the relationship, while overdraft-fee class litigation was pending — to insert a wholly new dispute-resolution regime (arbitration plus class waiver) and to treat the member's silence as consent.

**Issues the clause presents**

1. Scope of the reserved power. "May change the terms of this Agreement" is ambiguous about adding a brand-new term. Changing an existing term (a rate, a fee) is "changing"; inserting an entirely new arbitration provision that surrenders the court forum and class treatment is more naturally "adding." Any ambiguity is construed against the drafter (contra proferentem). The clause's other sentences show the drafters reserved specific powers by name when they meant to: the waiver sentence is a non-waiver clause (preserving the credit union's rights when it forbears from enforcing a term) and cannot be bootstrapped into a power to create new terms. "Except as prohibited by applicable law" imports external limits — state consumer-contract law, disclosure requirements, unconscionability, and the covenant of good faith and fair dealing.

2. Assent. Arbitration rests on consent (FAA § 2; First Options v. Kaplan; AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion). Silence is not acceptance unless the original bargain or the parties' course of dealing makes it so. The original agreement said nothing about continued use operating as acceptance of amendments; that rule appears for the first time in the credit union's own notice. The amending party cannot unilaterally supply the assent mechanism — the power to bind by silence must trace to the original agreement. Three emails and a cost-free opt-out show diligent notice, but notice is not assent.

3. Materiality. The more fundamental the change, the more assent is required. Arbitration plus a class waiver gives up the right to sue in court and to proceed collectively — precisely the remedies the member was then using. Courts divide on notice-and-continued-use amendments: some enforce them where the original clause broadly reserved amendment power (Seawright v. American General Financial Services); others refuse to impose arbitration on existing customers through a general change clause (Badie v. Bank of America). This clause — narrow verb, no reserved assent mechanism, no fresh consideration — sits closer to Badie.

4. Retroactivity. Even where amendment powers are enforced, they operate prospectively. The member's overdraft-fee claims accrued during the six and a half years before the amendment's effective date. Absent unmistakable language, a change-of-terms clause does not reach accrued disputes; using it to strip remedies from claims already accrued — and already the subject of pending class litigation — would let the credit union extinguish vested claims by notice. The covenant of good faith and fair dealing creates no substantive rights, but it polices the exercise of discretionary contractual power; the mid-litigation timing is evidence bearing on construction, not an independent basis to invalidate a lawful amendment.

5. Procedure. Whether a valid arbitration agreement exists is for the court, not an arbitrator, absent clear and unmistakable delegation; the credit union bears the burden of proving formation. The "Governing Law" clause is in substance a forum-selection clause (venue in the county where the credit union is located): it does not choose substantive law, does not delegate arbitrability, and cannot substitute for a missing agreement to arbitrate — though it may independently require her contract-based claims to be filed in that county.

**Construction**

The clause should be construed to authorize the credit union to modify existing terms prospectively, on notice, so far as law allows — not to unilaterally add a fundamentally new arbitration-and-class-waiver regime to an existing relationship, and not to make silence a substitute for assent. The notice sentence sets a floor for notification; it is not a source of power. The non-waiver sentence is confined to waiver. The savings clause keeps state-law limits in play.

**Enforcement in this dispute**

The motion to stay and compel should be denied as to this member. She never signed an arbitration agreement; the original agreement reserved no power to bind her by continued use; her silence and ordinary account usage do not manifest assent to surrender the judicial forum and class treatment; and in any event the amendment cannot be applied retroactively to claims that accrued before its effective date. At most, on the broader Seawright view, the amendment could govern disputes arising after the effective date for members who kept their accounts — it cannot govern her accrued overdraft-fee claims or be used, mid-litigation, to extinguish them. Her class action therefore proceeds in court (in the credit union's county under the forum clause), and the credit union remains free to offer arbitration going forward through a mechanism its agreement actually authorizes or by obtaining genuine assent.
