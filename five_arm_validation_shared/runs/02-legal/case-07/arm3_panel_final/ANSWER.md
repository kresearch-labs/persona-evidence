# Case 07 — Answer

**Bottom line.** The "Notice of Amendments" clause is a limited power to modify the *existing* terms of the account prospectively, on notice — not a standing authority to graft a wholly new arbitration-and-class-waiver regime onto a six-and-a-half-year-old agreement, and not a power that reaches claims already accrued. So construed, the Member never agreed to arbitrate: the motion to compel should be denied as to her overdraft-fee claims, with at most prospective application of the amendment to disputes arising after its effective date.

## I. Issues the clause presents

1. **Scope of the amendment power.** The power sentence says only that the Credit Union "may change the terms of this Agreement." Does "change" authorize *adding* a new arbitration provision and class-action waiver — a new dispute-resolution regime — or only modifying terms within the contemplation of the original bargain (rates, fees, service terms)? The clause never says "add," "insert," or "delete."
2. **Assent by silence and opt-out.** Can three emailed notices, an opt-out window, and continued account use bind a member to a provision surrendering forum, jury trial, and class relief? An arbitration agreement is a contract requiring mutual assent (FAA § 2), and silence is not acceptance absent circumstances showing assent (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 69). Notice itself is not contested — the Member got actual notice three times; the fight is over the Credit Union's *power* and her *assent*.
3. **The savings sentence.** "Except as prohibited by applicable law" subordinates the entire amendment power to external law — including any state rule requiring affirmative consent for arbitration, ordinary modification doctrine (assent, consideration, good faith), and unconscionability limits. It is the textual hook for importing those limits into this dispute.
4. **Good faith and timing.** The exercise of a unilateral amendment power is governed by the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (Restatement (Second) § 205; UCC § 1-304). The amendment was adopted for all members while overdraft-fee class litigation was pending, then invoked weeks later against a member whose claims had already accrued — facts inviting the inference that the mechanism is being used to strip accrued remedies rather than to govern the future relationship.
5. **Retroactivity.** Nothing in the clause (and, as described, nothing in the notice) says the amendment applies to claims already accrued. Amendments, like contracts, are construed to operate prospectively absent clear language.
6. **Illusoriness.** If the clause lets the Credit Union add or rewrite the arbitration term at will, its own "agreement" to arbitrate is illusory (Restatement (Second) § 77) unless the power is cabined — by the notice requirement, the savings sentence, and the duty of good faith.
7. **Who decides arbitrability.** The amendment as described contains no delegation clause, so the court — not an arbitrator — decides scope, assent, and retroactivity (First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan, 514 U.S. 938 (1995)).
8. **The venue clause.** The original Governing Law provision — any action "shall be brought in the county in which the credit union is located" — presupposes court litigation, a textual signal that the parties originally allocated disputes to the courts. If arbitration is enforced, the clause is displaced for arbitrable disputes; if not, it fixes the forum for the Member's suit.
9. **Unconscionability and preemption (secondary).** A take-it-or-leave-it amendment to an existing consumer relationship, coupled with a class waiver for small individual fee claims, raises the concerns of Discover Bank v. Superior Court, 36 Cal. 4th 333 (2005) — though its specific rule was preempted by AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011). Concepcion preempts state rules that single out and disfavor arbitration; generally applicable formation doctrines (assent, scope of an amendment power, prospective construction) survive it.

## II. Construction

**Plain language, resolved against the drafter.** The power sentence is "may change the terms of this Agreement"; the enumeration "terms, rates, or fees" appears only in the *notice* sentence, and the clause provides for *notice*, not *assent*. Read naturally, "change the terms" covers altering existing terms — the kind of adjustments the notice sentence itself contemplates — not importing a new arbitration relationship. Any ambiguity is resolved against the Credit Union as drafter (*contra proferentem*), and the express mention of notice and waiver without any consent mechanism suggests silence-based assent was no part of the bargain (*expressio unius*).

**The case law splits — and the split does not save the Credit Union.** The restrictive line, led by Badie v. Bank of America, 67 Cal. App. 4th 779 (1998), holds that a general change-of-terms clause does not let a bank add an arbitration provision to existing account agreements: such clauses reasonably reach changes within the contemplation of the original agreement, while a new arbitration relationship — surrendering forum, jury, and class relief — requires express assent. The permissive line, chiefly in the credit-card context where change-in-terms notices are established practice, enforces notice-and-opt-out amendments even for arbitration insertions. The Member prevails on her accrued claims under either line: on the restrictive reading there is no arbitration agreement at all; on the permissive reading the amendment operates prospectively only.

**Assent — and the wrinkle in the Member's own argument.** Continued use of the account fits Restatement § 69's "accepted benefits" pattern and comfortably supports constructive assent to ordinary term changes; it is a weak foundation for a rights-waiving regime the Member never saw when she opened the account, and the opt-out device shifts to her the burden of avoiding a contract she did not bargain for. But her silence argument has a tension she must confront: if the clause effects changes on notice alone, her silence is irrelevant and the amendment binds regardless. Her real position is therefore that (1) the power to "change the terms" does not extend to adding a dispute-resolution regime at all, and (2) even if it does, applicable law — reaching the clause through the savings sentence — requires affirmative consent before a member can be deemed to have agreed to arbitrate. The dispute narrows to those two points.

**Good faith and timing.** Even a valid amendment power is exercised subject to the implied covenant. Adopting an arbitration-and-class-waiver amendment while fee litigation was pending, then invoking it within weeks against accrued claims, supports at minimum a prospective-only construction — and in a restrictive-line jurisdiction reinforces the conclusion that the power was never meant to reach dispute-resolution rights.

**Testing the construction against the clause's other work.** The narrow reading does not disable the clause: the Credit Union may still change rates, fees, and service terms on the required notice, and may waive terms under the non-waiver sentence. It holds only that a general change-of-terms clause cannot be stretched, against its own wording, to import a new arbitration-and-class-waiver regime or to reach claims already accrued.

## III. Enforcement

- The motion to compel arbitration should be **denied as to the Member's accrued overdraft-fee claims**: (1) the clause, construed *contra proferentem*, does not clearly authorize inserting an arbitration agreement into the existing contract (the *Badie* line); and (2) even in a jurisdiction enforcing amendment-by-notice with opt-out, the amendment operates prospectively only, and her claims predate it.
- The **class waiver** cannot be applied to her pre-amendment claims for the same reason.
- If the governing state takes the permissive view, the amendment should be enforced only as to claims accruing after the effective date — and only if the amendment power is itself cabined, since an unbounded power to rewrite the arbitration term would render the arbitration agreement illusory.
- Her suit then proceeds under the **venue clause**: venue lies in the county where the Credit Union is located. Concepcion does not alter this result; it preempts state rules that discriminate against arbitration, not the generally applicable formation doctrines (scope of the amendment power, assent, prospective construction) on which the Member's defense rests.
- The **court decides** these arbitrability questions, because the amendment contains no clear delegation of them.
