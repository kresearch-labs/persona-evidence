The clause is a delegation clause: it gives the arbitrator "exclusive authority" over any dispute relating to the arbitration agreement's "interpretation, applicability, or enforceability." It presents four issues, and it should be construed broadly, per its plain terms, and enforced as written.

1. Who decides arbitrability. Under the Federal Arbitration Act (9 U.S.C. §§ 2, 4), parties may agree to arbitrate "gateway" questions of arbitrability, and they do so where the agreement is "clear and unmistakable" (First Options of Chicago v. Kaplan, 514 U.S. 938 (1995); Rent-A-Center, West v. Jackson, 561 U.S. 63 (2010)). This clause is express and exclusive ("exclusive authority," "any dispute"), and the handbook's incorporation of provider rules under which the arbitrator rules on his or her own jurisdiction — including objections to the agreement's validity — confirms the parties' intent to delegate. The court's role is therefore narrowed to deciding whether a valid agreement to delegate exists.

2. The specific unconscionability challenge. Under the severability doctrine (Prima Paint; Buckeye Check Cashing; Rent-A-Center), a challenge to the agreement as a whole goes to the arbitrator; only a challenge aimed specifically at the delegation clause itself stays in court. The Employee made that specific challenge — asserting the delegation clause was unconscionable both standing alone and in combination with other terms, while disclaiming any argument that all delegation clauses are per se unconscionable — so the trial court properly reached it, and it fails. The clause is standard, symmetrical delegation language made pursuant to the FAA; the take-it-or-leave-it handbook setting supplies at most modest procedural unconscionability, which is offset because the clause merely routes gateway questions to a neutral forum whose competence to rule on its own jurisdiction the parties incorporated. Her "in combination" argument is, in substance, an attack on other terms of the agreement — i.e., on the agreement as a whole — and belongs to the arbitrator. (Sandquist v. Lebo Automotive, Inc., 1 Cal.5th 233 (2016).)

3. Class arbitration. Whether the parties agreed to class arbitration is a question of contract interpretation and applicability — precisely what this clause delegates (Green Tree Financial v. Bazzle, 539 U.S. 444 (2003); Stolt-Nielsen v. AnimalFeeds, 559 U.S. 662 (2010); Sandquist). Nothing in the agreement reserves that question to the courts. And to the extent the agreement provides for no class arbitration, that term is enforceable: AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), abrogated California's Discover Bank rule, and Epic Systems v. Lewis confirms that class waivers in employment arbitration agreements are enforceable under the FAA — but the term's scope and application are for the arbitrator under the delegation clause.

4. Construction and enforcement. Construe the clause broadly — "any dispute relating to" interpretation, applicability, or enforceability covers gateway arbitrability, validity, unconscionability, scope, and the class-arbitration question — and enforce it as follows: the court rejects the specific attack on the delegation clause, grants the petition to compel arbitration, and leaves to the arbitrator (a) the unconscionability of the agreement as a whole (to be judged under the Armendariz framework) and (b) whether class treatment is contractually available. The Employee's writ should be denied: the trial court's order compelling arbitration on an enforceable delegation clause stands, with all remaining gateway questions committed to the arbitrator.

One line: The clause is a clear and enforceable FAA delegation clause — the court decides only the Employee's specific unconscionability attack on the clause itself (which fails), and the arbitrator decides everything else, including whether class arbitration is available.
