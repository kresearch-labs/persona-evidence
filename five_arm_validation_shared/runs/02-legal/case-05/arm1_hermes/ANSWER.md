# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-05

**The issues:** (1) whether the delegation clause — giving the arbitrator "exclusive authority" over questions of interpretation, applicability, and enforceability of the arbitration agreement — is itself unconscionable, which is a *court* question (who decides arbitrability), distinct from the employee's unconscionability challenge to the agreement as a whole (an *arbitrator* question); and (2) how the class-arbitration ban interacts with the delegation.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **The two questions must not be conflated.** Under the Prima Paint/Buckeye/Rent-A-Center line, a party challenging the arbitration agreement as a whole goes to arbitration; but a party challenging *specifically the delegation clause* presents that challenge to the court first. The employee correctly framed it: she attacked the delegation clause itself — that is for the trial court to decide on unconscionability grounds before ordering arbitration.

2. **The delegation clause is enforceable on these facts.** Challenges to delegation clauses fail when they rely on the same generic unconscionability arguments leveled at the agreement as a whole (the Rent-A-Center principle: a party must tailor the challenge to the delegation specifically). Here the delegation clause is a clean, self-contained sentence delegating "interpretation, applicability, or enforceability" questions; the handbook incorporates provider rules under which arbitrators rule on their own jurisdiction — the combination is the *normal*, commercially standard structure ( Clearent/JAG-style clauses are upheld routinely). Nothing about the clause itself (no fee-shifting surprise, no unilateralmodification right targeting it, no Arb-based cost barrier) makes delegation substantively or procedurally unconscionable.

3. **The class-arbitration ban does not undermine delegation.** Whether class arbitration is permitted is a "question of arbitration" the parties may delegate; the express ban, prominently stated, is enforceable (Stolt-Nielsen: parties may restrict class procedures). The employee's desire to proceed as a class does not render the delegation clause unconscionable.

4. **The correct procedural posture:** the trial court was right to grant the petition and leave the agreement-as-a-whole unconscionability argument to the arbitrator, because the delegation clause is severable and valid — but only after the court *itself* addressed the delegation-specific unconscionability claim and rejected it on the merits.

**Practical ruling:** deny the writ; the delegation clause is enforceable, so the arbitrator decides the employee's unconscionability attack on the rest of the agreement, and she arbitrates individually per the class ban.

**Key issues flagged:** severability of delegation clauses (challenge must target the delegation itself) · unconscionability of delegation is a court question · incorporated provider rules supporting arbitrator jurisdiction · class-arbitration waiver enforced.
