# Case 12 — Answer

## Issues the clause presents

Section 4.07 is a standard merger (integration/"entire agreement") clause, and it presents four issues:

1. **Integration/parol-evidence issue.** Does the clause establish the asset purchase agreement as a complete and final integration, so that the parol evidence rule bars proof of prior or contemporaneous oral representations (the sellers' pre-closing presentation about the probability and value of acquiring the contract rights) to vary or supplement its terms?
2. **Fraud-in-the-inducement exception.** Even if the writing is fully integrated, may a party that claims it was fraudulently induced to sign introduce extrinsic evidence of that fraud notwithstanding the merger clause?
3. **Scope-of-reliance issue.** Does the clause's "no restrictions, promises, representations, warranties, covenants or undertakings, other than those expressly set forth" language operate as an express disclaimer that makes the buyer's reliance on extra-contractual oral representations unjustifiable as a matter of law?
4. **Character-of-fraud issue.** Is the fraud exception limited, as the sellers contend, to fraud in procuring the signature (fraud in the factum/execution), or does it extend, as the buyer contends, to fraud in the inducement — misrepresentations about the subject matter that led the defrauded party to make the contract?

## How the clause should be construed

- The clause is a valid integration clause: by its terms it merges the parties' entire understanding into the writing and supersedes all prior agreements and understandings. It should be enforced according to its terms for ordinary contract purposes — prior negotiations and oral promises cannot be used to add to or contradict the obligations the agreement actually contains.
- But a merger clause is a shield for the *contract*, not a license to commit fraud. The parol evidence rule protects the integrity of the integrated writing; it was never designed to bar proof of an independent tort — fraudulent inducement — that goes to whether the defrauded party's assent was genuine. Extrinsic evidence of fraud in the inducement is therefore admissible despite full integration, because it is offered not to vary the writing but to avoid it (rescission) or to recover in tort (damages).
- The sellers' proposed limitation — that extrinsic proof reaches only fraud in procuring the signature — should be rejected. Fraud in the inducement is equally an attack on the reality of consent, and the overwhelming rule is that a general merger clause does not immunize a party from its own pre-contract misrepresentations.
- The no-other-representations language is a *general* merger/recital provision, not a specific, negotiated disclaimer of reliance on the particular representations at issue (the probability and value of the contract rights). A general "entire agreement" recital does not, as a matter of law, make reliance on specific oral misrepresentations unjustifiable; only an explicit, unmistakable disclaimer directed at the subject matter of the alleged fraud can do that. The sellers' presentation concerned the very assets being sold, and the clause nowhere disclaims representations about those assets' prospects or value.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

The clause should be enforced as an integration of the parties' contractual obligations, but it does **not** bar the buyer's fraud counterclaim. The buyer may offer extrinsic evidence of the sellers' alleged misrepresentations concerning the probability of acquiring, and the value of, the contract rights; whether those representations were made, were false, and were justifiably relied upon are fact questions for trial. The trial court's partial summary judgment — resting on the theory that the merger clause forecloses proof of the misrepresentations — should be reversed and the fraud counterclaim reinstated.

**Justification (one line):** A general merger clause integrates the bargain and triggers the parol evidence rule, but it neither bars extrinsic proof of fraud in the inducement nor, absent a specific disclaimer of reliance on the very representations at issue, makes such reliance unjustifiable as a matter of law.
