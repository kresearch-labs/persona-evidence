# Case 12 — Answer

**Bottom line:** Section 4.07 should be construed as a negotiated integration clause that also operates as a *specific disclaimer of extrinsic representations*, and it should be enforced to bar the buyer's proof of fraudulent inducement insofar as that proof rests on oral representations about the probability and value of the contract rights — the very subject matter of the sale — while preserving proof of fraud in the execution, wholly collateral promises of future performance, and any representations incorporated by reference or falling outside the contemplated transaction.

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Integration and its scope.** The clause does three things: it declares the writing (with its exhibits and referred-to documents) the entire agreement "in respect of the transactions contemplated"; it affirmatively disclaims any extrinsic "restrictions, promises, representations, warranties, covenants or undertakings"; and it supersedes prior agreements on the transaction. The first issue is the reach of each function — the integration and disclaimer are transaction-scoped, and the disclaimer expressly preserves representations "expressly set forth **or referred to** herein."
2. **The parol evidence rule's reach.** The rule bars extrinsic evidence to vary or contradict an integrated writing; it does not itself bar proof of invalidating causes such as fraud (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 214(e)). The buyer's appellate argument — that neither the clause nor the rule precludes a defrauded party's proof — is right as far as it goes, but it conflates *admissibility of fraud proof* with *justifiable reliance*. The dispositive question is the latter.
3. **Justifiable reliance under a specific disclaimer.** Because the clause disclaims representations specifically (not merely integrates), the question is whether a sophisticated buyer who negotiated for eight months, heard a presentation by the sellers' own employees, and had the opportunity to investigate can justifiably rely on oral statements about the deal's subject matter.
4. **Character of the misrepresentations.** Statements about the center's "capabilities" and the "likelihood and value" of acquiring contract rights mix existing-fact representations with predictions. The purchased intangibles expressly included "proposals under negotiation," so these statements concern the essence of what was sold — not collateral matter.
5. **Remedial consequences.** Whether partial summary judgment for the sellers was proper, and what, if anything, of the buyer's rescission-and-damages counterclaim survives.

## How the clause should be construed

The precedent line controls the construction. The older New York cases (e.g., *Adams v. Gillig*) admitted proof of fraudulent inducement notwithstanding an integration clause; *Danann Realty Corp. v. Harris*, 5 N.Y.2d 317 (1959), confined that principle to misrepresentations *collateral* to the writing — a promise "to do something other than that which is embodied in the contract" — and enforced a specific no-other-representations clause where the alleged misrepresentations concerned the very subject matter of the deal and the buyer had opportunity to inspect. *Mallad Construction Corp. v. County Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n*, 12 N.Y.2d 155 (1963), kept the collateral-promise carve-out alive (an oral promise of future performance survived the clause). *W.W.W. Associates, Inc. v. Giancontieri*, 77 N.Y.2d 157 (1990), treated the parol evidence rule as substantive law and held reliance on oral representations unreasonable as a matter of law where the clause disclaimed them. The modern applications (*Merrill Lynch Interfunding, Inc. v. Argenti*, 155 F.3d 113 (2d Cir. 1998); *Hutton v. Klabal*, 26 A.D.3d 447 (2d Dep't 2006)) state the operative limit both ways: a *general* merger clause alone does not preclude a fraud claim, but a clause that *specifically disclaims representations* can defeat justifiable reliance, at least between sophisticated parties.

Each of these cases arose in a different transactional setting — the sale of an apartment house (*Danann*), a land contract with rezoning assurances (*W.W.W.*), a loan with anti-reliance language (*Merrill Lynch*) — and that mismatch should be flagged, not papered over. What transfers is not the setting but the clause type and its rationale: a specific disclaimer, negotiated at arm's length, tells the buyer exactly what the deal is, and the law will not let the buyer re-trade the bargain with oral statements about matters the writing disclaims and the buyer could have verified. The rationale also has limits that the construction must respect: the clause cannot bar proof of fraud in the execution (its own validity depends on genuine assent); it cannot disclaim representations it incorporates by reference ("referred to herein"); it does not reach representations outside "the transactions contemplated"; and it is not an exculpation of fraud — it reallocates reliance rather than licensing a known lie. Read this way, the clause works across all of its applications: it bars supplementary consistent terms, bars contradictory terms, and bars reliance on disclaimed subject-matter representations, while leaving the narrow fraud carve-outs intact.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

Enforce the clause. The alleged misrepresentations — the probability and value of acquiring the contract rights — go to the heart of the purchased intangibles ("proposals under negotiation" and technology development), i.e., the subject matter of the contract. The buyer is a commercial purchaser that negotiated for roughly eight months, received the presentation through its own representatives, and signed an agreement containing Danann-style language: "There are no restrictions, promises, representations, warranties, covenants or undertakings, other than those expressly set forth or referred to herein." Under *Danann* and *W.W.W. Associates*, reliance on such oral representations is unjustifiable as a matter of law, and the partial summary judgment for the sellers should be affirmed to that extent — the buyer's rescission claim falls with it, since rescission rests on the same discredited reliance.

The buyer's best counterarguments, answered: (i) *fraud vitiates consent, so a party cannot contract out of its own fraud* — true, but New York implements that principle through justifiable reliance, not a per-se rule; a general integration clause does not bar a fraud claim (*Hutton*), yet this clause is not general — it specifically disclaims representations, which is precisely the *Danann*/*W.W.W.* situation; (ii) *the statements were predictions about future events, not integrated terms* — the clause disclaims "representations, promises … or undertakings" of every kind, and the predictions concerned the exact asset the buyer was buying; (iii) *the statements were made by seller employees in a presentation* — that establishes attributable misstatements by the sellers; it does not establish justifiable reliance against a specific disclaimer. What genuinely survives the clause is narrow: proof of fraud in the execution (misrepresentations about what the buyer was signing); proof of a wholly collateral promise of future performance outside the writing (*Mallad*), though the clause's express disclaimer of "promises … or undertakings" makes that difficult on these facts; and any representation in documents "referred to herein" or concerning matters outside the contemplated transaction. The buyer's proper channel for dissatisfaction with the contract rights' value is the express warranties and referred-to documents it bargained for — not parol re-litigation of the deal it signed.
