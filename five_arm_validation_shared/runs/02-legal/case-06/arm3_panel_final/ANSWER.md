# Case 06 — Automatic Renewal Clause in an Elevator Maintenance Contract

## Issues the clause presents

1. **The dispositive issue — the effect of an admittedly untimely termination notice.** The clause renews itself automatically for successive five-year terms unless a party gives written notice at least 90 days before a term ends; the Hotel Owner's notice was 58 days late. The clause never says time is of the essence, so the question is whether the 90-day requirement is a strict condition of the termination power or a non-essential term whose breach is excused absent prejudice — under the Pennsylvania rule the Hotel Owner invokes, untimely notice under an automatic renewal provision suffices unless the counterparty demonstrates prejudice from the delay. Because a miss here means a five-year lock-in, this issue decides the Service Company's largest claim.
2. **Disproportionate forfeiture.** Enforced strictly, a 58-day lapse produces a five-year obligation — a sanction out of all proportion to the delay, and one a court should not impose where the clause bears another reasonable reading.
3. **Notice formalities.** The clause specifies no method, address, or form of notice; the son's letter was written, unequivocal, received, and acted upon. Only timeliness is genuinely in dispute, and the owner's adoption of his son's letter raises no separate problem on this record.
4. **No mid-term exit.** Termination is permitted only at five-year boundaries, so the Hotel Owner's complaints about the work cannot justify exit — only setoff, cure costs, or material breach going to the essence. His withholding of the October–December 1990 payments must be measured against that limit.
5. **The side promise.** The alleged oral 20% discount "offered in connection with the five-year renewal" raises parol-evidence, integration, consideration, and statute-of-frauds questions — and sits awkwardly with the owner's primary position that no renewal ever took effect.
6. **Remedial structure.** If the renewal were enforced, the remedy would have to be settled: damages versus specific performance of a five-year service contract.

## Construction

The clause grants a power of termination, exercisable at the end of the initial extended term (December 31, 1990) or at the end of any later five-year period, "by giving the other party 90 days prior written notice."

First, the clause fixes a notice period but nowhere declares time to be of the essence. That is the precise gap the Pennsylvania rule fills: where an automatic renewal provision lacks time-is-of-the-essence language, untimely notice is effective unless the party resisting termination demonstrates prejudice from the delay. The rule maps onto this clause's material facts without strain — a long-term service contract, automatic five-year renewals, a fixed notice deadline, no essence language, and notice actually given before the renewal took effect. (The facts track Otis Elevator Co. v. George Washington Hotel Corp., 3d Cir. 1994.)

Second, doctrinally the 90-day notice is a condition precedent to exercise of the termination power, and the question is whether its breach should be excused. Enforcing the condition strictly, regardless of harm, serves the clause's purpose (giving the counterparty lead time to arrange replacement service or commit to continue) only incidentally; its principal effect here would be to convert a protective term into a forfeiture-generating trap: 58 days of lateness buys five more years of obligation. The avoidance-of-forfeiture factors of Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 229 — how far the condition was fulfilled (notice given, only its timing short), whether the forfeiture is disproportionate (five years locked in for 58 days), and whether the counterparty can be compensated (concrete prejudice) — point the same way as the Pennsylvania prejudice rule. That rule polices the deadline while preventing the windfall: late notice still risks dispute and litigation cost, and concrete prejudice remains compensable.

Third, the construction is symmetrical: the same prejudice inquiry would govern termination "at the end of any subsequent five year period" by either party, so it favors neither side. Contra proferentem does little work — the clause is clear on its face — but the operative silence (no essence language) is the drafter's own choice, and any residual ambiguity in a form provision is read against the Service Company.

## Enforcement in this dispute

**Renewal (dispositive).** The November 30, 1990 letter was written, unequivocal, and received 31 days before the extended term ended — early enough to accomplish everything the notice requirement exists to accomplish except to give the Service Company its full 90 days. The burden of showing prejudice from the 58-day delay is the Service Company's, and this record shows none: its response letter proves receipt and comprehension, not harm. The lost renewal itself is not "prejudice from the delay"; if it were, the rule would swallow itself, since every late notice by definition forfeits the renewal. Prejudice must be concrete — forgone replacement work for 1991–1995, crews scheduled or inventory dedicated in reliance, a decision to exit or reprice — and none appears. Nor do the Service Company's anticipated replies help it: the owner's knowledge of the deadline shows at most that the notice-giver sat on his rights, whereas the rule asks what the delay cost the recipient; and by suing to enforce the renewed term the Service Company concedes it would not have terminated in any event, leaving its grievance as the owner's exit alone — the very forfeiture the rule disfavors. The Hotel Owner's motion for summary judgment should therefore be granted: the notice was effective, the contract terminated December 31, 1990, and the claim for breach of the renewed term fails. If the Service Company comes forward with specific evidence of prejudice from the delay, a genuine issue of material fact defeats the motion and the clause is enforced as written — renewal through 1995 — subject to the limits below.

**The withheld October–December 1990 payments.** Those services were rendered under a contract then in force; proven defects do not permit unilateral nonpayment unless they amounted to a material breach going to the essence of the agreement. The Service Company recovers the three months' contract price; the Hotel Owner recoups the reasonable cost of curing proven defects (or the diminution in value), up to the amount owed.

**The 20% discount counterclaim.** The writing fixes the price and is integrated; an oral offer made "in connection with the five-year renewal" cannot vary it under the parol evidence rule. Treated instead as a separate promise about future renewal pricing, it required consideration (for example, inducement for the owner's forbearance from serving timely notice or for his acceptance of the renewal) and, because it could not be performed within a year, had to satisfy Pennsylvania's statute of frauds. The claim is also coherent only as an alternative position: a discount tied to the renewal presupposes the very renewal the owner denies. With the contract terminating December 31, 1990, the claim largely falls away; a bare oral offer contradicting the integrated writing, unsupported by consideration or a writing, should be rejected.

**If the renewal were enforced.** The remedy would be damages, not specific performance of a five-year service contract: the renewed term's contract price less substitute earnings and avoided costs, subject to the duty to mitigate, the cure-cost setoff above, and the discount only if proven with consideration and a writing.

**Disposition.** Construe the clause as a termination power whose notice requirement — a condition precedent which, absent time-is-of-the-essence language, is excused under the Pennsylvania prejudice rule and Restatement § 229 where untimely notice is received before the renewal takes effect and causes no demonstrated prejudice. Enforce it here by holding the November 30, 1990 notice effective and the contract terminated on December 31, 1990; awarding the Service Company the October–December 1990 contract price less proven cure costs; and dismissing the renewal-breach claim and — absent proof of a consideration-supported, written discount bargain — the counterclaim.
