The clause presents four issues: (1) whether the 90-day notice requirement is a condition precedent to termination, to be enforced as written, or a flexible notice provision; (2) whether the Hotel Owner's notice — given 31 days before the extended term ended and 58 days after the notice window closed — was effective to prevent the automatic five-year renewal; (3) who bears the burden of showing prejudice from the late notice, and whether the question can be resolved on summary judgment; and (4) the collateral claims — the withheld October–December 1990 payments and the alleged oral twenty-percent discount.

Construction. The clause gives each party an option: "Either party may terminate this agreement ... by giving the other party 90 days prior written notice." Read literally, timely notice is the prescribed method of exercising the option — a condition on the power to terminate. But the clause nowhere says that time is of the essence, nor that notice given after the window is a nullity. Pennsylvania (as the record states) construes such provisions flexibly: where a contract fixes a period for notice but does not make time of the essence, untimely notice is effective unless the party resisting termination demonstrates that the delay prejudiced it. The rationale is that the notice period exists to give the other party lead time to prepare for the change — here, to wind down the maintenance schedule and line up replacement work — not to spring a forfeiture; and a party to an automatic-renewal contract always knows the expiration date from the contract itself, so a shorter notice period rarely causes real harm.

Enforcement — the renewal claim. Applying that rule, the November 30 letter from the Hotel Owner's son (sent on the owner's behalf, and its authority is not disputed) was effective to terminate the contract as of December 31, 1990, despite being 58 days late, unless the Service Company shows prejudice from receiving 31 rather than 90 days' notice. The burden of showing prejudice rests on the Service Company, the party seeking to hold the owner to the renewed term. For an elevator maintenance contract, one month is prima facie adequate lead time: the company knew the December 31 expiration date from the outset, the notice stated the correct termination date, and nothing in the record suggests the missing 58 days would have changed the company's position. If the Service Company cannot point to concrete prejudice (for example, reliance on the renewal in staffing commitments or forgone opportunities to market the service), summary judgment should be entered for the Hotel Owner on the claim for the renewed five-year term: the contract expired December 31, 1990, and the Service Company may not recover the renewal term's price. If the Service Company does demonstrate prejudice, the clause is enforced as written — the contract is deemed renewed for five years, with damages measured by the renewal term's price, subject to the owner's mitigation arguments.

The October–December 1990 payments. These stand on different footing: those months fell within the existing (extended) term, the services were rendered, and the obligation to pay the monthly contract price does not depend on the renewal dispute. The owner's defense — problems with the Service Company's work — is a claim of defective performance. Under the substantial performance doctrine, the Service Company recovers the contract price for the three months less whatever damages the owner proves for the deficiencies; the owner may not simply withhold the full price absent a material breach.

The twenty-percent discount counterclaim. The alleged commitment was oral, made "in connection with the five-year renewal." It faces three obstacles: as an oral variation on a negotiated written contract it is likely barred by the parol evidence rule if the writing is integrated; it is contingent on a renewal which, as construed above, never took effect; and the owner, having terminated rather than renewed, cannot claim the benefit of a discount offered as an inducement to a renewal he rejected. The counterclaim should fail unless the owner can prove a separate, independently supported oral promise.

Bottom line: because the clause lacks a time-is-of-the-essence provision, it is construed as a flexible notice requirement rather than a strict condition, so the Hotel Owner's 58-days-late notice was effective absent demonstrated prejudice to the Service Company — the contract terminated December 31, 1990, the Service Company may recover only the October–December 1990 price less proven service deficiencies, and the oral discount counterclaim fails.
