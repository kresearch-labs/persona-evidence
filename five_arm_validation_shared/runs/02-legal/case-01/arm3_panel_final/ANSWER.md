The disputed provision is a refund-only limitation-of-liability (exculpatory-type) clause in paragraph 5 of the "Terms and Conditions of Agreement" printed on the reverse of a one-page standardized directory-advertising form. On cross-motions for summary judgment on undisputed facts, it presents these issues and must be construed and enforced as follows:

1. Notice/conspicuousness. The clause is set in roughly one-sixteenth-inch, light type on the reverse page — 21 dense paragraphs, 65 lines of copy in a six-by-seven-inch area — about one-third of the way down, with no separate heading, underlining, or highlighting. Measured against the UCC conspicuousness standards the court used as analogy (N.J.S.A. 12A:1-201(10), 12A:2-316(2)), it is inconspicuous, and the form as a whole "discourages the actual reading of it" — the print is too small to read without a magnifying glass. A reasonable subscriber therefore cannot be charged with notice of the limitation as a matter of law: it was not effectively communicated at the time the agreement was reached.

2. Procedural unconscionability (contract of adhesion). Under the two-prong unconscionability analysis adopted from Allen v. Michigan Bell Telephone Co. (guided by Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors and Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.), prong one is satisfied: the form was prepared by the telephone company and used in all subscriptions for yellow-pages advertising; the clause was not subject to deletion or modification, was never discussed, and the salesperson who presented it lacked authority to alter it — his function was merely "ministerial," to secure a signature on the dotted line. This was a "take it or leave it" offer by a party with a superior bargaining position — a contract of adhesion.

3. Substantive unconscionability. The clause attempts to relieve the company of all liability for its own negligence except a refund of the contract price. Because it shifts substantially what a reasonable subscriber would expect to be the publisher's risk-bearing obligation, and the advertiser had no opportunity to negotiate, enforcement would be unfair and unconscionable — prong two satisfied. The company's public-policy justifications fail: low advertising rates could be maintained by spreading the risk of its negligence among all advertisers, and it is unfair that one advertiser's potentially irreparable harm should be subordinated to a blanket limitation for the general class; the inevitability of occasional printing errors (Hamilton Employment Service) is a reason to devise methods of cost allocation, not to immunize the publisher; the objection that damages would be speculative is a trial question, not a ground for summary judgment; and the yellow pages were effectively the only classified directory freely distributed to all telephone subscribers in the area, so the "other media are available" argument is unpersuasive.

4. Public-duty/regulatory distinction. Under the principle enunciated in Tessler v. Sonitrol, exculpatory clauses are valid only where they do not adversely affect the public interest, the exculpated party is not under a public duty to perform (as a public utility or common carrier is), and the contract does not grow out of unequal bargaining power or is otherwise unconscionable. The regulatory-approval cases the company invoked (McTighe, Cole, Correll, Gas House) are distinguishable: in those the advertising rates and contracts were filed with and approved by a public-utilities regulator, whereas no agency regulates yellow-pages advertising rates here, leaving the advertiser to ordinary contract protections. Allen — which refused to enforce a virtually identical clause as contrary to public policy — is the persuasive line, consonant with Henningsen. Mayfair Fabrics is distinguishable because its exculpatory clause appeared in a typewritten rider that stood out from the printed form and effectively communicated itself; here the clause is so located as to easily escape attention.

5. Construction. Even apart from unconscionability, an exculpatory or limitation clause is construed strictly against the party relying on it (Carbone; Kuzmiak), and the refund-only language reaches only "errors, deviations or omissions ... in connection with any advertisement or listing covered by this agreement" — it cannot be stretched further.

Enforcement: The clause is unconscionable and unenforceable in this dispute. The telephone company cannot limit its liability to a refund of advertising charges. The photographer's motion for partial summary judgment as to liability is granted; the company's cross-motion for summary judgment is denied; and the issue of damages proceeds to trial.
