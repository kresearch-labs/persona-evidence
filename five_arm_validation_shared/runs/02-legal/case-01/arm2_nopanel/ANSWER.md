The clause is an exclusive-remedy (limitation-of-liability) provision in a standard-form, adhesion contract, and it presents four issues: (1) incorporation and assent — whether fine print on the reverse of a one-page form became part of the bargain; (2) procedural unconscionability — a take-it-or-leave-it standardized agreement that the salesperson had no authority to alter; (3) substantive unconscionability — whether confining recovery to a refund, where the company concedes its own printing errors, is so one-sided as to be unenforceable, including whether the exclusive remedy fails of its essential purpose; and (4) construction — the reach of "errors, deviations or omissions," whether the later telephone-requested changes (the certification line, the additional listing, the display ads) are "covered by this agreement," how the refund is measured by "charges already paid ... for the issue(s) in which the errors ... occur," and whether the exclusivity bars consequential damages such as lost business and harm to professional reputation.

On incorporation, the clause is part of the contract. One who signs a written agreement is charged with knowledge of its contents, including terms printed on its reverse under the caption "Terms and Conditions of Agreement"; the strict conspicuousness rules applied to warranty disclaimers have not generally been extended to remedy limitations. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 211, assent to a standardized form is effective even without reading particular terms if the party had reason to know of them — and a businessperson purchasing commercial advertising did. The very fact that the salesperson lacked authority to vary the form confirms that the printed terms, not any oral understanding, are the agreement.

On unconscionability, the photographer shows procedural unconscionability (an adhesion contract, no negotiation) but not the substantive unfairness needed to strike a term in a commercial setting. The clause does not exonerate the company from all liability; it substitutes a certain, prompt remedy — repayment of the charges — for uncertain litigation, the classic trade-off embedded in directory-advertising rates. The remedy is real, not illusory, and on these facts it does not fail of its essential purpose: the errors were isolated printing deviations in a single issue of each directory, the company conceded them, and a refund restores the photographer's out-of-pocket loss. Striking an agreed remedy requires gross imbalance or overreaching; an inconveniently small remedy for a conceded but minor error is not enough. (The common law governs this service contract, with UCC §§ 2-302 and 2-719 available only by analogy.)

On construction, the clause must be read strictly against the drafter but its plain terms cover what happened. A substituted tagline and a misspelled word are "errors" and "deviations" "in connection with" the advertisement and listings "covered by this agreement"; the changes requested by telephone modified that same advertisement and listing, so they fall within the clause. The refund is measured by the charges already paid for the affected advertisement or listing in the issue(s) in which the errors occurred — not the whole contract price and not more. Because the clause makes that refund the "sole and exclusive liability" and declares that "no other or further liability shall exist" once it is paid, consequential damages are barred.

On enforcement, the facts being undisputed and unconscionability being a question of law, the court should enforce the clause as written on the cross-motions: the photographer recovers a refund of the charges paid for the affected advertisement and listings for the issues in which the errors appeared, and nothing more; the unconscionability, inconspicuousness, and adhesion objections fail. The clause would be disregarded only if the refund remedy proved illusory in operation — for example, recurring uncorrected errors issue after issue leaving the advertiser with no meaningful remedy — which this record does not show.
