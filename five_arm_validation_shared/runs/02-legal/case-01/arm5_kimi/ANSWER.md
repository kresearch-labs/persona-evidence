# Case 01 — Answer

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Conspicuousness and fair disclosure.** The limitation was printed on the reverse of a one-page standardized form, buried in paragraph 5 of the "Terms and Conditions," with no showing that the photographer's attention was drawn to it. A clause that strips a customer of meaningful remedy must be conspicuous and fairly brought home to the adhering party.

2. **Adhesion and unequal bargaining power.** The agreement was a pre-printed form offered take-it-or-leave-it by a salesperson with no authority to vary its terms, by a utility that is effectively the only practical outlet for classified telephone directory advertising. That is the classic profile of a contract of adhesion.

3. **Unconscionability.** The clause raises both procedural unconscionability (hidden term, no negotiation, no meaningful choice) and substantive unconscionability (for errors entirely within the company's control, the customer's sole remedy is a refund of what he paid — a remedy that costs the company nothing and leaves the customer's actual business loss wholly uncompensated, however careless the company was).

4. **Scope of the clause.** Whether general language limiting liability for "errors, deviations or omissions" reaches the company's own negligent performance, and whether a refund-only remedy differs in kind from a total exculpation, which would more plainly offend public policy.

## How it should be construed and enforced

The clause is an exculpatory-type provision drafted by the company for its own benefit, so it must be **strictly construed against the drafter** and enforced, if at all, only to the extent its language plainly and unambiguously covers the specific printing errors at issue. Because the facts are undisputed, enforceability is a question of law for the court on the cross-motions, not for a jury.

On enforcement, the court should weigh the clause's one-sidedness against the considerations that have led many courts to sustain directory-advertising liability limits: the charge for the ad is small relative to the speculative lost profits claimed, the parties can be restored to the pre-contract position by a refund, and a limitation (as opposed to a total exclusion) of liability is less offensive to public policy. But where, as here, the term is inconspicuous, non-negotiable, and offered by a monopoly provider on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, the better construction is that the clause is **procedurally and substantively unconscionable as applied and therefore unenforceable**; at minimum, any ambiguity must be resolved against the company and in favor of full compensatory recovery. The photographer's motion for summary judgment on the enforceability issue should be granted, and the company's motion denied; the company remains liable for the proven harm from its conceded printing errors, not merely for a refund.

**Justification (one line):** An inconspicuous, non-negotiable refund-only limitation imposed by a monopoly utility through an adhesion form is strictly construed against its drafter and, on these undisputed facts, is unconscionable and unenforceable as a matter of law.
