# Case 04 — Answer

**Issues presented.** Paragraph 6 raises three issues: (1) a drafting defect — the clause recites that the employee "agree[s]" to a two-year post-employment restraint but omits prohibitory language ("I shall not" / "I will not") before subsections (a) and (b), raising the question whether the covenant is "meaningless" as written; (2) a construction issue — whether a court may supply the obviously missing prohibitory words as a matter of contract *interpretation* (reading the agreement as a whole to effectuate the parties' evident intent), or whether doing so requires a distinct claim for equitable *reformation* that the Company never pleaded; and (3) an enforceability issue — whether, once construed as a promise not to solicit, the two-year non-solicitation covenant is a reasonable restraint (duration, scope, and protection of a legitimate business interest in customer relationships and goodwill) and was breached.

**How it should be construed.** The clause should not be read literally into meaninglessness. The paragraph is captioned "Agreement Not to Solicit," states it is given "[a]s a condition to my hiring and continued employment," and sets out a detailed, graduated time scheme (six-month periods) and defined categories of protected employees, customers, and prospective customers. Read as a whole, the only sensible meaning is that the employee promised not to do the listed acts; the omitted words are a typographical/scrivener's slip. Supplying them is interpretation, not reformation: where the parties' intent is clear from the four corners of the agreement, a court may construe the contract to carry out that intent without a separate reformation claim. The trial court therefore erred in holding the paragraph unenforceable for want of promissory language.

**How it should be enforced.** Construed as a two-year covenant not to solicit, the restriction is reasonable and enforceable: it is limited in time (two years), limited in subject matter (soliciting Company employees and insurance business of customers/prospective customers the employee personally dealt with), and protects the legitimate interest in customer relationships the employee built on the Company's behalf over eighteen years. The employee's December 2006 sales presentation to the risk manager of the major account she had serviced — which moved roughly $500,000–$550,000 in annual fees to her new employer — fell squarely within the covenant and within the two-year window following her September 2005 resignation. The covenant should be enforced against her (injunctive relief and/or damages for the lost business), and the case resolved on that basis rather than on the drafting defect.

*Justification: the parties' intent to prohibit solicitation is unmistakable from the clause read as a whole, so the missing prohibitory words may be supplied by ordinary interpretation — no reformation claim is required — and the resulting two-year customer non-solicitation covenant is reasonable in scope and was breached by the employee's post-resignation sales presentation.*
