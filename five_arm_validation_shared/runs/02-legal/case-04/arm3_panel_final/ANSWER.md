Paragraph 6 is an enforceable covenant not to solicit: the promissory force missing from its subsections is supplied by the introductory words "I agree that," the paragraph's own heading, and the instrument read as a whole, and supplying the omitted negative is ordinary interpretation, not equitable reformation requiring a separate request.

The clause presents five issues: (1) whether paragraph 6, which states its prohibitions without "shall not" or "will not," is a binding covenant or, as the trial court held, "meaningless"; (2) whether a court may supply the omitted negative without a pleaded request for equitable reformation; (3) what term the successive six-month periods prescribe and whether that restraint is reasonable as applied; (4) whether the employee's December 2005 dinner and December 2006 sales presentation breached the covenant; and (5) what enforcement and damages the Company is entitled to.

Construction. The instrument must be read as a whole. The paragraph opens "As a condition to my hiring and continued employment by the Company, ... I agree that, during the period I am employed by the Company and for a period of six months following the termination of such employment, followed by a second six-month period ... followed by a fourth six-month period: (a) ... solicit for employment any person who is an employee of the Company ... (b) Call on or, directly or indirectly, solicit, divert, or take away insurance-related business from the Company ..." The phrase "I agree that" is promissory and governs each enumerated subsection; the operative verbs are the object of that agreement. The agreement's title ("Employment, Non-Solicitation, and Confidentiality Agreement") and the paragraph's heading ("Agreement Not to Solicit") state the parties' purpose on their face. The only literal reading without a negative — a promise to solicit the Company's employees and to divert its customers' insurance business — is absurd and contradicts the recited condition to which the promises are expressly tied. A construction that would obligate an employee, on pain of losing her job, to raid her employer is one no party could have intended, and it renders the paragraph meaningless in precisely the way the rules of construction forbid: every provision must be given effect, and a court may not adopt a reading that makes an entire bargained-for paragraph incoherent with the document's title and recitals.

Interpretation, not reformation. Supplying "not" before subsections (a) and (b) does not rewrite the bargain; it gives the parties' words their only sensible effect. Reformation is an equitable remedy that corrects a mistake in reducing a meeting of the minds to writing: it must be pleaded, and the intended terms must be proven clearly and convincingly. None of that machinery is needed here, because the intended terms appear on the face of the document — the heading, the title, the introductory promise, and subsection (b)'s own two-year lookback ("during the two-year period prior to my termination of employment"). The trial court conflated interpretation with reformation; the Company needed no equitable pleading to have the covenant read as the covenant it plainly is.

Duration and reasonableness. The paragraph enumerates successive six-month periods — a first, second, and fourth — aggregating the evident two-year term, and subsection (b)'s two-year lookback corroborates that the parties were operating on a two-year horizon. The covenant should be enforced for that stated term. The restraint is reasonable: it is keyed not to geography or the industry at large but to persons and accounts with a specific relationship to the employee — Company employees, and customers or two-year prospective customers "with whom I had direct contact." For a senior broker who personally serviced the account, that is a narrow, goodwill-based restriction of the kind enforced in the insurance-brokerage setting: it protects the employer's legitimate interest in customer relationships the employee cultivated, while leaving her free to work for the competitor in every other respect. A two-year tail is at the outer edge but defensible where its breadth is bounded by the employee's own direct contacts.

Breach. The customer was a Company customer with whom the employee had direct contact through the risk manager. "Call on" reaches more than formal pitches: the December 2005 dinner, and a fortiori the December 2006 sales presentation, were calls on the customer for insurance-related purposes, and the presentation was solicitation — as the trial court in fact found. The timing confirms it: the presentation came roughly fifteen months after the September 2005 resignation, within the two-year term. The resulting shift of $500,000 to $550,000 in annual fees to the new employer is the diversion the covenant was written to prevent.

Enforcement and remedy. The covenant should be enforced as written: the Company is entitled to an injunction for the remainder of the stated term and to damages measured by the business diverted, with the trial court's finding of solicitation undisturbed and its "meaningless" ruling reversed. The court should not enlarge the restraint beyond its text — enforcement is of the two-year, contact-based prohibition the parties wrote, no more. Consideration is no obstacle: the agreement was signed in 1997 as a condition of continuing employment, the employee continued in employment for years afterward, and her eighteen-year tenure and sophistication as a broker defeat any claim of surprise.
