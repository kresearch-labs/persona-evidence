The clause is enforceable as written, it runs for two years from termination, and the employee breached it; the Company is entitled to damages for the diverted account.

**1. The missing promissory language is a problem of construction, not of validity.** Paragraph 6 opens with an operative promise — "I agree that, during the period I am employed by the Company and for [the stated periods]" — and subsections (a) and (b) enumerate the acts covered by that agreement. Listing the restricted acts in the infinitive ("solicit," "Call on or ... solicit, divert, or take away," "provide") after an introductory "I agree that" is a familiar drafting form: the promissory force comes from the lead-in, which carries through the list. The paragraph identifies the promisor, the restricted period, the restricted conduct, and the protected relationships — every essential term is present. Declaring it "meaningless" because the words "shall not" do not precede each subsection reads the instrument against its evident purpose. Supplying the connective negation is ordinary interpretation of the writing as a whole, not the equitable remedy of reformation; reformation (rewriting to reflect a bargain different from the one actually struck) requires a pleading and proof of mistake, but interpretation — giving the written words their natural effect — is always available and was all the Company needed. The trial court therefore erred twice: in holding the paragraph meaningless for want of promissory language, and in treating the absence of a reformation request as a bar to enforcement. The parties' conduct confirms the reading — the employee signed the covenant as a condition of employment, and both sides litigated it as a restriction.

**2. The covenant runs for two years.** The awkward chain — six months following termination, "followed by a second six-month period ... followed by a fourth six-month period" — is coherent only as four consecutive six-month periods, i.e., a two-year tail that expired in September 2007. That reading gives effect to every phrase; the employee's contrary reading (only the first six months bind) would discard the later periods as surplusage. It is corroborated within the same paragraph by the drafters' own reference to "the two-year period prior to my termination" in subsection (b), which shows they were thinking in two-year terms. Even if residual ambiguity remained, it would be construed against the drafter (the Company) — but the text is not genuinely ambiguous, and the point decides the case: the operative breach occurred in December 2006, within two years but beyond any six-month reading.

**3. The employee breached subsection (b).** Subsection (a) covers soliciting the Company's *employees* for employment; her conduct is not of that kind. Subsection (b) forbids her, directly or indirectly, to "Call on or ... solicit, divert, or take away insurance-related business from the Company" or to provide insurance-related services to any customer of the Company. The December 2005 dinner, standing alone, was at most a social contact and not a "call on" in the trade sense. But the December 2006 sales presentation — which the trial court found to be solicitation, a finding well supported — was made to a current customer whose account she had serviced for the Company, within the restricted period, and it produced precisely the result the covenant guards against: the account, with $500,000 to $550,000 in annual fees, moved to her new employer. That is a diversion/taking away of insurance-related business and the provision of insurance-related services to a Company customer within the meaning of (b).

**4. The covenant is reasonable and should be enforced as construed.** It is ancillary to employment — a condition of hiring and continued employment, supported by consideration (she remained employed for years after signing). It is narrow in reach: it protects only the Company's relationships with existing customers and with prospective customers with whom she had direct contact, and it bars solicitation and diversion, not competition generally. Two years is within the range courts accept for customer non-solicitation covenants, and the Company's interest — the goodwill she built up over eighteen years at its expense — is legitimate. Nothing requires blue-penciling; the clause as properly construed is reasonable.

**5. Relief.** Because the restricted period expired in September 2007, prospective injunctive relief is largely spent; the operative remedy is damages for breach — the fees lost to the diversion accomplished by the December 2006 solicitation. The judgment should be reversed insofar as it declared paragraph 6 meaningless and unenforceable, and remanded for determination of breach and damages, with the trial court's finding that the presentation constituted solicitation left intact.
