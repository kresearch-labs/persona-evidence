# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-09

**The issues:** (1) whether pandemic conditions plus government-imposed operating restrictions (masks, distancing, amenity limits) made performance "illegal or impossible" within the clause's four corners; (2) whether "impossibility" means literal illegality/physical impossibility or includes commercial frustration of the event's quality; and (3) whether the termination notice satisfied the clause's ten-day condition.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **Force majeure is read narrowly and textually — and the organizer's argument fails the text.** The clause excuses performance only when circumstances beyond reasonable control make it "**illegal or impossible**" to provide or use the facilities. By May/July 2020 the law *permitted* the event: the resort was open, the government allowed gatherings with precautions, and the organizer itself planned to attend until a week out. "Illegal" — no; mandates presuppose legality of the underlying operation. "Impossible" — the standard is objective impracticability of performance, not loss of the event's luxury character: reduced amenities, masks, and spaced seating are burdens on the *experience*, not the *ability to provide or use the facilities*. The parties' own April 2020 amendment — renegotiating commitments *while the pandemic was already raging* and leaving the force-majeure clause untouched — is powerful evidence that both parties understood COVID as a negotiated risk, not a supervening excuse.

2. **The clause conditions termination on timely notice — met here, so that hurdle doesn't save the organizer from the merits.** The organizer gave written notice within ten days of its claimed basis. But satisfying the procedural condition does not establish the substantive excuse: the notice itself reveals the true objection — the "first-class guest and conference experience" was compromised, i.e., dissatisfaction with the modified product, which is not impossibility.

3. **The commercial-disappointment line matters.** Cases throughout 2020–2021 drew exactly this line: government-ordered *closure* of venues excuses; operating restrictions that leave a lawful, usable facility do not. The organizer could have invoked the clause when gathering bans made the original May date illegal — it instead amended and postponed, waiving the strongest force-majeure window.

4. **Frustration of purpose (if the court looks past the clause) fails too:** the supervening event didn't destroy the purpose of the contract (a seminar could be held); it degraded its amenities — a cost-of-doing-business risk.

**Practical ruling:** the force majeure clause does not excuse the organizer; the termination was a breach. The resort's counterclaim succeeds for the guaranteed room revenue/food-and-beverage shortfall (with a duty to mitigate credited), and the $1.2M deposit refund demand fails, with deposits applied per the agreement's cancellation schedule.

**Key issues flagged:** "illegal or impossible" read literally (restrictions ≠ impossibility) · pandemic amendments reset the force-majeure calculus · procedural notice compliance doesn't create a substantive excuse · commercial frustration vs destruction of purpose.
