# Case 09 — Answer

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Is the pandemic a covered force majeure event?** The clause lists "acts of God, war, acts of domestic terrorism, strikes or similar circumstances." A pandemic is not enumerated, so the question is whether COVID-19 and official responses to it fall within "acts of God" or the catch-all "similar circumstances" (construed under *ejusdem generis* as events of the same kind — external, unforeseeable, and beyond a party's reasonable control). Even if the pandemic qualifies as the triggering circumstance, that alone does not excuse performance.

2. **Was performance made "illegal or impossible"?** This is the dispositive issue. The clause sets a high bar: it does not excuse performance that has merely become more expensive, less profitable, less attractive, or different in quality from what was expected. The resort stood ready, willing, and able to host the event lawfully — with masks, distancing, and amenity limits — and no law made holding the event illegal. The organizer's real objection was that the event would no longer deliver the "first-class experience" it wanted. Diminished value or altered character of performance is not impossibility, and the clause does not incorporate the broader doctrines of impracticability or frustration of purpose.

3. **Was the notice condition precedent satisfied?** The clause conditions termination on written notice "within ten (10) days after learning of such basis." The organizer knew of the pandemic and the government's responses by March–April 2020 (it amended the agreement in April on that very basis), yet gave notice only a week before the July event — months late. A condition precedent to termination is strictly enforced; untimely notice defeats the claimed excuse.

4. **Effect of the April 2020 amendment.** With full knowledge of the pandemic, the parties postponed the event and reduced commitments while leaving the force majeure clause untouched. By electing to continue the contract rather than invoke the clause then, the organizer assumed the risk of pandemic conditions as of the rescheduled date and undercut any claim that those conditions were an unforeseen, excusing circumstance.

## How the clause should be construed

Force majeure clauses are construed narrowly and according to their plain terms; the party invoking the clause bears the burden of proving every element. Here that means: (a) the triggering event must be of the same general kind as those enumerated; (b) "illegal or impossible" means objectively illegal or impossible — not burdensome, undesirable, or degraded in quality; and (c) the ten-day written-notice requirement is an express condition precedent to any termination. The contract's express subjection to applicable health and safety codes cuts against the organizer: compliance with lawful pandemic measures was contemplated by the agreement itself, not an excuse from it.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

The clause should be enforced as written, with the result that it does **not** excuse the organizer:

- The resort's ability and readiness to perform in compliance with law defeats any claim of illegality or impossibility; the organizer's dissatisfaction with mask mandates and amenity limits goes to the quality of the experience, not the possibility of performance.
- Independently, the organizer's failure to give notice within ten days of learning of the pandemic basis (known since spring 2020) fails the clause's express condition precedent, so its termination was ineffective even if a covered event existed.
- The termination therefore was an anticipatory repudiation/breach by the organizer. The operator is entitled to judgment on its breach counterclaim — retaining or applying the deposits per the contract and recovering damages under the room-usage and food-and-beverage guarantees, subject to its duty to mitigate — and the organizer's claim for a declaration of excuse and a $1.2 million refund should be denied.

**Bottom line:** Because the resort remained able to perform lawfully and the organizer gave notice months after the ten-day deadline, the force majeure clause provides no excuse; the organizer's termination was a breach and the operator should recover on its counterclaim.
