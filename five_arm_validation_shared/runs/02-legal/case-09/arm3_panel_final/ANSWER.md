# Case 09 — Force Majeure in the Hotel-Event Agreement: Issues, Construction, Enforcement

## Issues the clause presents

1. **Trigger — is the pandemic a force majeure "circumstance"?** The clause excuses performance when "circumstances beyond its reasonable control, such as acts of God, war, acts of domestic terrorism, strikes or similar circumstances" occur. The list is illustrative ("such as"), and "similar circumstances" invites *ejusdem generis*: events of the same genus as those named — widespread, externally imposed, uncontrollable disruptions. A global pandemic and official responses to it fit that genus (epidemics have classically been treated as acts of God). The trigger is therefore likely satisfied; the dispute should not turn here.

2. **Causal standard — "illegal or impossible."** This is the dispositive issue. The clause does not excuse performance that is merely harder, costlier, or degraded; it requires circumstances that "make it illegal or impossible to provide or use the Hotel facilities." The resort imposed mask mandates, distancing, and amenity limits precisely so the event could proceed **in compliance with law**; nothing on this record made providing or using the facilities illegal, and the organizer could lawfully use them under the adjusted conditions. The organizer's real complaint — that a "first-class guest and conference event experience" was impossible — is a diminished-quality or frustration-of-purpose argument, not impossibility of performance. Force majeure clauses are construed narrowly and the invoking party bears the burden; these sophisticated parties wrote a hard standard ("illegal or impossible"), not "impracticable" or "commercially frustrated," and that bargain should be enforced as written.

3. **Pre-allocated compliance risk.** The agreement was "expressly subject to all applicable laws, including health and safety codes." The parties built regulatory-compliance burdens into the baseline of performance. Pandemic-era operating conditions of the kind imposed — masks, distancing, limited amenities — fall squarely within that assumption, which independently defeats the claim that compliance measures themselves made performance "impossible."

4. **The April 2020 amendment.** With the pandemic already known, the parties postponed the event, reduced commitments, and left the force majeure clause untouched. That conduct shows the parties identified the pandemic risk and allocated it by rescheduling with obligations intact — not by excusing them. The organizer's July obligations were assumed with full knowledge of pandemic conditions, so the amendment undercuts any claim that continuing pandemic burdens were outside the assumed risk, while confirming the clause remained available only on its written terms.

5. **The ten-day notice condition.** "The ability to terminate pursuant to this clause is conditioned upon delivery [of] written notice … within ten (10) days after learning of such basis." This is a condition precedent to termination under the clause. The notice's own stated basis — "the pandemic and official responses to it" — was known no later than April 2020, when the parties amended the agreement; measured from that learning date, notice given one week before the July event was far outside the ten-day window. If the organizer instead pins the "basis" to the resort's specific operating measures, timeliness turns on when those measures were announced — a date the record does not supply — but the April amendment (negotiated with the pandemic fully known) makes it hard to characterize the pandemic itself as a newly learned basis in July. The organizer bears the burden of proving timely notice; absent it, termination under the clause was ineffective.

6. **Remedial silence — the deposits.** The clause excuses performance "without liability"; it says nothing about refunding money already paid. Even a valid force majeure termination would not automatically convert deposits into refundable sums — the agreement's payment terms (80% room guarantee, $700,000 food-and-beverage minimum) govern the disposition of the deposits and any accrued obligations. Because the termination was ineffective, the operator may retain the deposits under those terms and pursue its counterclaim for the guaranteed amounts, subject to its duty to mitigate (e.g., reselling rooms).

## How the clause should be construed

Read the clause as a three-part allocation of risk: (i) a broad illustrative trigger (uncontrollable events of the acts-of-God genus, including a pandemic and government health orders); (ii) a strict causal requirement that the event render providing or using the facilities **illegal or impossible** — not impracticable, unprofitable, or inferior to what was hoped; and (iii) a mandatory ten-day notice condition on termination. The clause is mutual ("either party"), and its silence on refunds means the contract's payment provisions, not the force majeure clause, control deposit disposition. The reading is consistent across applications: a strike or order that genuinely bars use of the hotel triggers the clause; a party's dissatisfaction with legally compliant operating conditions does not.

## Enforcement in this dispute

The organizer should not prevail. The pandemic is a force majeure circumstance, but it did not make performance illegal or impossible: the resort stood ready to host in compliance with law, and the compliance measures invoked were exactly the health-and-safety burdens the agreement expressly assumed. The termination was also ineffective because the ten-day notice condition was not met on the basis the notice itself invoked, so the organizer's non-performance was itself a breach. The declaration should be refused; the operator's counterclaim should proceed for breach, with the operator entitled to retain the deposits under the payment terms and to recover the guarantee/food-and-beverage shortfall, subject to mitigation. (The case file specifies no governing law; this analysis applies general common-law force majeure principles.)

**Bottom line:** the clause is triggered by the pandemic but not satisfied by it — "illegal or impossible" was not shown, timely notice was not given, and the deposits stand or fall under the payment terms, not the force majeure clause.
