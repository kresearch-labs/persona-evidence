**Issues presented.** (1) Whether the COVID-19 pandemic and the official and venue-level responses to it fall within the clause's enumerated events ("acts of God, war, acts of domestic terrorism, strikes or similar circumstances"). (2) Whether, even if they do, those circumstances satisfied the clause's operative trigger — making it "illegal or impossible to provide or use the Hotel facilities." (3) Whether the organizer satisfied the condition precedent of written notice "within ten (10) days after learning of such basis." (4) How the risk of pandemic-era regulation was allocated, given the agreement's express subjection to "all applicable laws, including health and safety codes" and the April 2020 amendment. (5) The consequences: whether the organizer's termination was excused (deposits refundable) or was itself a breach supporting the operator's counterclaim.

**Construction.** Force majeure clauses are construed narrowly, and the party invoking the clause bears the burden; the clause is a limited allocation of risk for defined catastrophes, not a general escape hatch. The phrase "such as" makes the list illustrative, but "similar circumstances" should be read ejusdem generis — events of the same genus as acts of God, war, terrorism, and strikes, i.e., external catastrophic events. The pandemic and governmental responses readily qualify as an act of God or "similar circumstances," but that only gets the organizer to the threshold. The operative trigger is demanding: performance must be made "illegal or impossible" — not merely impracticable, unprofitable, degraded, or different from what the parties expected. The parties did not write "impracticable," "commercially unreasonable," or "frustration of purpose" into the clause, and a court will not supply them. The clause is mutual, and its remedy is conditioned: excusal without liability is available only upon strict compliance with the ten-day written-notice requirement, which operates as a condition precedent to valid termination. Finally, the agreement was "expressly subject to all applicable laws, including health and safety codes": legal compliance was built into the promised performance itself, so the existence of health mandates is part of the assumed baseline rather than a supervening illegality.

**Application and enforcement.** The organizer can plausibly characterize the pandemic and official responses as "similar circumstances" beyond its reasonable control, but it cannot satisfy the trigger. The resort stood ready to host the event in compliance with law; mask mandates, social-distancing setups, and limits on amenities made the event less attractive and less profitable, but they did not make it illegal to provide or impossible to use the hotel facilities. "Impossible" means the performance cannot be done, not that it cannot be done well; the organizer's own notice complains that the "first-class guest and conference event experience" was rendered impossible — a quality-of-experience objection (to the mask mandate in particular) sounding in impracticability or frustration of purpose, which the clause does not cover. The April 2020 amendment reinforces this: with full knowledge of the pandemic, the parties renegotiated the deal (postponement to July, reduced commitments) and deliberately left the force majeure clause untouched — an acknowledgment that the pandemic, by itself, was not a basis to terminate and an implied allocation of the pandemic risk as the clause stood. On notice, the organizer terminated only one week before the event: if the "basis" was the pandemic generally, the organizer learned of it no later than April 2020 and the ten-day clock had long expired; if the basis was the resort's specific measures, the organizer must show it learned of them within ten days of its notice. Either way, strict compliance with the condition precedent is doubtful, and the organizer bears that burden; late notice means no valid termination. Because the clause is not triggered and the notice condition fails, the organizer's termination was itself a breach: the operator properly refused the refund, may retain the deposits (over $1.2 million) under the agreement's cancellation and attrition terms, and may press its counterclaim for lost room revenue and the unmet $700,000 food-and-beverage minimum, subject to its duty to mitigate by reselling the rooms and event space. Only if the organizer could prove an actual legal prohibition making it unlawful to hold the event — not merely burdensome protocols — would the clause excuse its performance and require repayment of the deposits.

**Conclusion.** The clause should be construed narrowly and enforced as written: it excuses performance only when a listed or similar catastrophic circumstance makes providing or using the hotel facilities illegal or impossible, and only on strict compliance with the ten-day notice condition. The pandemic and the resort's health measures made the event different and less desirable, but not illegal or impossible, and the organizer's notice was at best tardy; the force majeure clause therefore does not excuse the organizer, its termination was a breach, and the operator may retain the deposits and pursue its counterclaim.
