The clause is a hybrid instrument bundling four distinct legal operations — a warranty disclaimer, an exclusive-remedy designation, a consequential-damages exclusion, and an attempted exculpation for the seller's own negligence — and each strand must be tested separately.

1. Conspicuousness of the warranty disclaimer. Under UCC § 2-316(2), language excluding the implied warranty of merchantability must mention merchantability — this clause does — and, in a writing, must be "conspicuous." Conspicuousness is measured by § 1-201(10): the term must be written or displayed so that "a reasonable person against whom it is to operate ought to have noticed it," with the Code's illustrations all involving something that visually separates the term from routine text (larger or contrasting type or color, capital-letter headings). A sentence buried in the body of the printed form, underlined but set in the same size, style, and color of type as every other provision, does not meet that standard: the underline is the sole distinguishing feature and does not bring the term home to the buyer before he surrenders the implied warranties. Conspicuousness is a question of law for the court, but on this record the court should have held the disclaimer inconspicuous; holding it conspicuous as a matter of law was error.

2. Exculpation for the seller's own negligence. The counterclaim sounds in tort — negligent installation that destroyed the crop — not in breach of warranty. A warranty disclaimer, even a valid one, eliminates only warranty theories; it does not immunize the seller from liability for its own negligence. The clause tries to reach negligence ("whether or not such loss is due to the negligence of the Seller"), but a provision releasing a party from liability for its own future negligence is strictly construed against the drafter and must be clear, explicit, and conspicuous. Buried boilerplate cannot carry that burden, so the disclaimer does not defeat the negligence counterclaim even apart from its inconspicuousness.

3. Consequential damages and exclusive remedy. Under § 2-719, commercial parties may limit or exclude consequential damages unless the limitation is unconscionable; the prima facie unconscionability rule of § 2-719(2) protects only consumers injured in person by consumer goods, so the buyer's commercial crop loss gets no such presumption. But the exclusion operates alongside an exclusive-remedy clause pointing to "the manufacturer's standard warranty, if any." If no manufacturer's warranty existed, the exclusive remedy is empty — a remedy that provides no relief fails of its essential purpose under § 2-719(2), reviving the Code's ordinary remedies — and a scheme that both strips the implied warranties and leaves the buyer remediless is vulnerable as unconscionable.

4. Economic-loss overlay. The buyer's loss was to other property (the crop), not merely to the barn itself, so the tort claim for negligent installation is not barred by the economic-loss rule; damage to property other than the product remains actionable in tort.

Enforcement in this dispute: the gas-price claim stands on its own — the stipulation eliminated only the barn's price, and the disclaimer does not touch the independent debt for gas, so summary judgment for the seller on that claim was proper (subject to recoupment of same-transaction damages, pursued through the counterclaim). The counterclaim is the reverse: the disclaimer was inconspicuous under §§ 2-316(2) and 1-201(10), so the implied warranties survived, and in any event the clause neither conspicuously nor clearly releases the seller from its own negligent installation. The jury's verdict for the buyer was supported by the evidence, and the trial court erred in setting it aside and entering judgment for the seller.

Bottom line: the clause presents conspicuousness (§§ 2-316(2), 1-201(10)), negligence-exculpation, consequential-damages/exclusive-remedy (§ 2-719), and economic-loss issues; construed against the drafter, the buried disclaimer is not conspicuous and does not bar the negligence counterclaim, so the buyer recovers on the counterclaim while the seller recovers the gas price.
