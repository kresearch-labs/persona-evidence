# Case 13 — Answer

## Issues presented

The quoted language is really two distinct clauses, and each must be analyzed separately:

1. **Disclaimer of implied warranties.** The statement that "there are no other warranties, express or implied, of merchantability or otherwise" is an attempted exclusion of the implied warranty of merchantability. Under UCC § 2-316(2), such an exclusion must mention merchantability and, if in writing, **must be conspicuous**. Whether a term is conspicuous is a question of law for the court under UCC § 1-201(10): a term is conspicuous only if it is "so written that a reasonable person against whom it is to operate ought to have noticed it," and language in the body of a form is conspicuous only if set off in larger or contrasting type or color.

2. **Exclusion of consequential damages and exculpation for negligence.** The second sentence attempts (a) to limit the Buyer's remedies to the manufacturer's standard warranty, (b) to bar consequential damages (lost profits, damage to goods processed by the product — here, the tobacco crop), and (c) to exculpate the Seller even for its **own negligence**. Under UCC § 2-719(3), a consequential-damages limitation for commercial loss is not prima facie unconscionable, but under § 2-719(2) an exclusive or limited remedy fails of its essential purpose — and the Code's remedies then apply — where circumstances cause the agreed remedy to leave the injured party without meaningful relief. Clauses exculpating a party from its own negligence are disfavored and strictly construed; they are enforced only if the intent to cover negligence is clearly and unequivocally expressed, and they cannot be smuggled in through inconspicuous boilerplate. Section 2-302 unconscionability is also implicated where an inconspicuous clause strips a buyer of all remedy for a product that never worked.

## How the clause should be construed

- **The warranty disclaimer is not conspicuous.** Mere underlining, in the same size, style, and color of type as the surrounding provisions, buried in the body of a printed form, does not make language conspicuous. Nothing about the presentation would cause a reasonable buyer to notice it. Because conspicuousness is a mandatory condition of an effective written disclaimer of the implied warranty of merchantability, the disclaimer **fails as a matter of law**, and the implied warranty of merchantability survives.
- **The consequential-damages/negligence exculpation should be strictly construed against the Seller.** Even if it could otherwise limit liability for commercial crop loss under § 2-719(3), it cannot shield the Seller where (a) it is inconspicuously embedded in the form, (b) the barn was wholly inoperative, so the "exclusive" warranty remedy failed of its essential purpose under § 2-719(2), and (c) the loss is charged to the Seller's own negligent installation — a liability a party cannot disclaim through general, inconspicuous language.

## How it should be enforced in this dispute

The trial court erred in concluding the disclaimer was conspicuous and in setting aside the jury's verdict for the Buyer on the counterclaim. The disclaimer is ineffective to bar the Buyer's claim for the destroyed tobacco crop; the jury verdict for the Buyer should be reinstated and judgment entered on it. The Seller's stipulated gas claim stands as resolved by the summary judgment, but the disclaimer gives the Seller no contractual defense to the counterclaim.

Justification: Under UCC §§ 1-201(10) and 2-316(2), a warranty disclaimer merely underlined and immersed in the form's body in type identical to the surrounding provisions is not "conspicuous," so the implied warranty of merchantability was never disclaimed, and the consequential-damages/negligence exculpation — strictly construed and undermined by the failure of the exclusive remedy's essential purpose (§ 2-719) — does not defeat the Buyer's counterclaim.
