The clause presents five issues: (1) whether the quoted provision — both its exclusion of implied warranties and its exculpation of the Seller's own negligence — must be "conspicuous" under UCC §§ 2-316(2) and 1-201(10); (2) whether, printed in the same size, style, and color of type as the rest of the form, merely underlined and immersed in the body of the contract, it meets that requirement; (3) how far the clause's own limiting words — "which extend beyond the above description of the property" — preserve warranty coverage; (4) whether the exclusion of consequential damages and the exclusive remedy (the manufacturer's standard warranty "if any") are enforceable under § 2-719; and (5) whether conspicuousness is a question for the court or the jury.

Conspicuousness. The disclaimer mentions "merchantability," so it clears the first requirement of § 2-316(2). But a written exclusion of an implied warranty — and, as invoked here against the counterclaim, the same provision's purport to relieve the Seller of liability for its own negligence — is effective only if "conspicuous": under § 1-201(10), "so written that a reasonable person against whom it is to operate ought to have noticed it," with language in the body of a form qualifying only "if it is in larger or other contrasting type or color," and whether a clause is conspicuous "is for decision by the court." The essential purpose of the conspicuousness requirement is to protect the buyer from surprise. Here no statutorily sanctioned method was used — no larger or contrasting type or color — and no other attention-calling device either: the clause is merely underlined, in type identical to the surrounding provisions, and buried in the body of the form. Underlining alone, in uniform type, does not "contrast"; nothing in the form's presentation would reasonably draw a buyer's attention to this provision, and the requirement exists precisely to prevent that kind of surprise. The Seller's contrary argument — that underlining is deliberate emphasis on a signed instrument — proves emphasis, not notice, and any residual ambiguity in the Seller's own printed form is construed against the drafter. The disclaimer is therefore not conspicuous.

Effect. A disclaimer that fails the conspicuousness requirement is ineffective as a matter of law under § 2-316(2). That disposes of the Seller's special plea of contractual defense: neither the implied-warranty disclaimer nor the exculpation of negligent installation can be enforced against the Buyer.

Scope preserved by the clause's own terms. Even if the disclaimer had been conspicuous, it disclaims only implied warranties "which extend beyond the above description of the property." Construed strictly against the Seller, that language preserves every implied obligation the description itself imports: a gas-fired tobacco curing barn that was inoperative from the day of delivery does not conform to its own description, so the implied warranty that the barn be merchantable as described survives the clause on its face; and because the clause never mentions fitness for purpose, no effective disclaimer of the warranty of fitness was attempted at all.

Consequential damages and negligence. The lost tobacco crop is literally "damage to goods ... processed in or by, the product," so a facially valid exclusion would reach the counterclaim. Three points tell against enforcement here: (a) the sentence purports to relieve the Seller of liability for its own future negligence ("whether or not such loss is due to the negligence of the Seller"), and exculpation of one's own negligence in a printed form is construed strictly and must be clearly brought home to the buyer; (b) the crop is property other than the product itself, and the loss is alleged to flow from negligent installation — an independent duty a printed sales-form limitation is not fairly read to cut off; and (c) § 2-719(3) enforces consequential-damages exclusions only "unless the limitation proves unconscionable."

Exclusive remedy. The exclusive remedy is the manufacturer's standard warranty "if any." Nothing in the record shows that any such warranty existed or was ever performed, and the barn never operated. An exclusive remedy that may not exist at all is illusory; one that is totally unperformed fails of its essential purpose under § 2-719(2), freeing the Buyer to resort to the Code's normal remedies (§§ 2-714, 2-715). An exclusion of consequential damages paired with an exclusive remedy that leaves the Buyer wholly remediless after a total failure of the goods is unconscionable as applied under § 2-719(3).

Procedural enforcement. Because § 1-201(10) makes conspicuousness a question for the court on the face of the writing, the judge rather than the jury decides it — but the court erred in concluding that this disclaimer was conspicuous. On the correct construction the clause is not conspicuous and is ineffective as a matter of law, so the Seller's special plea fails; the verdict for the Buyer on the counterclaim should not have been set aside. Judgment should be entered on the verdict for the Buyer, subject to recoupment against the Seller's gas claim, which the clause does not affect.

Bottom line: the disclaimer is not conspicuous — underlining in uniform type, buried in the body of the form, is neither larger nor contrasting type or color nor any other attention-calling device, and fails § 1-201(10)'s purpose of protecting the buyer from surprise — so it is ineffective as a matter of law under § 2-316(2), the Seller's special plea of contractual defense fails, and the Buyer's counterclaim for the destroyed crop should be restored to verdict and judgment.
