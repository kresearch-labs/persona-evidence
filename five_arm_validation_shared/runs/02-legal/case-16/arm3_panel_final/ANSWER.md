**Bottom line:** Paragraph 67(H) is a forum-channeling clause, not a forfeiture device. Construed to give every sentence effect, it waives only the tenant's free-standing declaratory judgment action and routes lease disputes into summary proceedings, while its fee-shifting sentence expressly contemplates — and prices — the tenant's injunctive applications. It cannot be enforced to defeat a timely Yellowstone injunction: a prospective waiver of Yellowstone relief is unenforceable as against public policy, and the clause's own text presupposes that such applications may be made. The Appellate Division, Second Department, construing this very rider language, so held in *159 MP Corp. v Redbridge Bedford, LLC*, 158 A.D.3d 659 (2d Dep't 2018), reversing the denial of a Yellowstone injunction sought one day before the cure period expired and the dismissal of the tenant's complaint.

## The issues the clause presents

**1. Scope of the waiver.** The first sentence waives only "a declaratory judgment action." The third sentence — "in the event injunctive relief is sought by Tenant and such relief shall be denied, the Owner shall be entitled to recover the costs of opposing such an application" — is inoperative unless the tenant *may* seek injunctive relief. The clause therefore distinguishes declaratory actions from injunction applications and permits the latter. The Landlord's position that the Tenants "have contractually waived the right to seek injunctive relief" reads words into the clause and reads the fee sentence out of it.

**2. Waivability of Yellowstone protection.** Even if the clause were read to reach injunctions, a lease provision purporting to waive the tenant's right to seek a Yellowstone injunction is unenforceable as against public policy. *Long Island Gynecological Services, P.C. v 1103 Dental Realty, LLC*, 85 A.D.3d 827 (2d Dep't 2011); *159 MP Corp. v Redbridge Bedford, LLC*, supra. The doctrine — which descends from *First National Stores Inc. v Yellowstone Shopping Center, Inc.*, 21 N.Y.2d 630 (1968) — exists to prevent forfeiture of the leasehold; the parties cannot contract out of it.

**3. Is suing a "breach of a substantial obligation" warranting "immediate termination"?** The termination trigger must be strictly construed, because forfeitures of commercial leases are disfavored. Applied as the Landlord urges — termination because the Tenants moved to prevent termination — it is circular: the act of seeking to avoid forfeiture becomes the forfeiture. The trigger fairly reaches a free-standing declaratory action filed in the teeth of the waiver; even then, the consequence the clause's own design supplies is the Landlord's chosen forum and its fees, not "immediate termination" of a 20-year tenancy without the cure machinery.

**4. Effect of "their disputes be adjudicated via summary proceedings."** This is the interpretive key: the clause's purpose is channeling. A summary proceeding under RPAPL article 7 is initiated by the landlord, so the clause presupposes that the tenant appears and defends there. Read as a whole, the waiver eliminates the tenant's parallel free-standing forum — not the tenant's defenses, and not the tenant's injunctive protection.

**5. Enforcement mechanics in this dispute.** (a) *Timeliness.* The Tenants moved one day before the cure period expired — timely, because the motion must be made before the cure period lapses and the lease terminates (*Jefpaul Garage Corp. v Presbyterian Hospital in the City of New York*, 61 N.Y.2d 442 (1984); *Yellowstone Picture Framing Corp. v Reid*, 61 N.Y.2d 495 (1984); after termination there is nothing left to toll, *Graubard Mollen Dannett & Horowitz v Moskovitz*, 65 N.Y.2d 850 (1985)). (b) *Predicate notice.* The "Ten (10) Day Notice" demanding cure within 15 days satisfies RPAPL § 232-c's ten-day minimum for nonresidential tenancies. (c) *Serious questions on the merits.* The Tenants raise at least three: waiver of the alleged breaches by years of rent acceptance with knowledge of the conditions (a recognized waiver absent a reservation of rights, *Jefpaul Garage*); whether the permits are the Landlord's responsibility under the leases; and whether the cited conditions — fire hazards, nuisances, noise, blocked inspections — are breaches at all. Desire and ability to cure are adequately shown for tolling purposes. (d) *Fees.* If the injunction is ultimately denied, the clause's fee sentence applies; and under Real Property Law § 234, fee covenants in leases are implied to be reciprocal, so the sentence is not the one-way risk-shifting it appears to be.

**6. The trial court's "disguised declaratory relief" rationale.** Treating every claim as declaratory in disguise nullifies the clause's express contemplation of injunction applications and renders the Yellowstone right illusory. It also answers a question the clause does not present: the clause allocates forums; it does not redefine the tenancy's substantial obligations.

## Construction

The reading that works across the clause's applications: (i) in a summary proceeding the Landlord brings, the Tenants defend without restriction; (ii) a free-standing declaratory action about a lease provision or a notice is waived and, if brought, breaches the paragraph; (iii) an injunction application is permitted, with the fee-shifting sentence supplying the Landlord's protection if relief is denied; and (iv) the termination trigger attaches to the waived conduct, not to protected injunctive applications. Each phrase does work; none is surplus; none defeats the others.

## Enforcement in this dispute

Grant the Yellowstone injunction tolling the cure period pending determination of the merits, and deny the Landlord's cross motion insofar as it seeks dismissal or termination for breach of ¶ 67(H): the waiver cannot support forfeiture, both because Yellowstone waivers are void as against public policy and because the Tenants' application was not a declaratory action within the waiver. The declaratory claim should not be dismissed outright — the declaration that the leases remain in force is inseparable from the injunction application, and the clause does not purport to waive defenses; at minimum the Tenants may litigate waiver-by-acceptance-of-rent and responsibility-for-permits in any summary proceeding the Landlord brings on proper predicate notices. The Landlord retains everything it bargained for: its chosen forum, a fast adjudication, and its fees if the injunction is ultimately denied. What it may not do is convert a forum choice into a forfeiture of a 20-year leasehold — the precise evil the Yellowstone doctrine exists to prevent.

Anticipated reply and answer: the Landlord will say the parties bargained for certainty and speed. Certainty and speed are fully served by the channeling reading; the only interest sacrificed is the forfeiture interest, which New York law subordinates to the preservation of commercial tenancies.
