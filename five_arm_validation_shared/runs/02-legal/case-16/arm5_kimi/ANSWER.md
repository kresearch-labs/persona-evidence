# Case 16 — Answer

## Issues Presented

Paragraph 67(H) is a **declaratory-judgment-waiver / forum-channeling clause**, not a limitation-of-liability or damages-cap clause (that clause-type distinction matters: the analysis below is specific to waivers of declaratory and injunctive relief in commercial leases). It presents five distinct issues:

1. **Enforceability of the prospective waiver of declaratory judgment actions.** Can a commercial tenant contractually waive, in advance, the right to sue for a declaration of its rights under the lease?
2. **Whether the waiver also bars a Yellowstone injunction** — i.e., whether a motion to toll the cure period is itself the waived "declaratory/injunctive relief" even though the tenant styled it as a motion rather than a DJ action.
3. **Public-policy tension with RPAPL 749(3)**, which voids lease provisions waiving the tenant's right to seek a stay of a summary proceeding — does enforcing this waiver nullify that statutory protection?
4. **The fee-shifting sentence**: if injunctive relief is "sought by Tenant and such relief shall be denied," the Owner recovers its costs and attorney's fees. Is that enforceable, and does it apply here?
5. **The termination-for-breach sentence**: any breach of ¶67(H) "shall constitute a breach of substantial obligations of the tenancy" and "grounds for the immediate termination of this Lease." Can the mere filing of the DJ action itself be made a forfeiture event, as the Landlord's cross-motion contends?

## How the Clause Should Be Construed

**The core waiver is enforceable.** New York enforces commercial-lease waivers of declaratory judgment actions between sophisticated parties under ordinary freedom-of-contract principles. The controlling authority is the First Department's decision in *159-161 West 16th St. LLC v. JG 16th St. Restaurant Corp.*, 143 A.D.3d 1 (1st Dept. 2016), which enforced a materially identical rider provision and held that such a waiver bars both the DJ action and the accompanying Yellowstone application. The precedent line behind it runs from *First National Stores v. Yellowstone Shopping Center*, 21 N.Y.2d 630 (1968) (creating the common-law Yellowstone remedy tolling a cure period), through the line of cases holding that a Yellowstone injunction is simply the procedural vehicle for what is, in substance, declaratory relief on the lease's validity — so a waiver of declaratory relief reaches the Yellowstone motion too. Citing only the newest case without that line would obscure *why* the waiver reaches a motion that is not literally captioned "declaratory judgment action": the clause's own final sentence supplies the reasoning — "it is the intention of the parties hereto that their disputes be adjudicated via summary proceedings." A Yellowstone application is the classic end-run around that bargained-for forum, so enforcing the waiver against it gives every word of the clause work and leaves none surplus.

**No public-policy violation.** RPAPL 749(3) voids waivers of the right to seek a *statutory stay* in the summary proceeding itself. A Yellowstone injunction is a *common-law, pre-proceeding* tolling device, not the RPAPL 749 stay; the tenant retains the statutory stay and every defense in the summary proceeding. The waiver therefore closes a collateral forum, not the courthouse: the Tenants may still litigate everything — their dispute of the violations, their claim that permits were the Landlord's responsibility, and their waiver-by-rent-acceptance defense — as defenses and counterclaims in the Landlord's summary proceeding. Because the clause channels rather than extinguishes the Tenants' rights, it does not offend public policy.

**Strict construction of the forfeiture and fee provisions.** Forfeiture clauses are construed strictly against the landlord. The fee-shifting sentence is clear and enforceable by its terms: the Tenants sought injunctive relief, it was denied, so the Landlord recovers its actual costs and attorney's fees of opposing the application. The termination sentence is more vulnerable: while the parties may agree that breach of the paragraph is a "substantial" breach, a lease *forfeiture* triggered solely by a good-faith (albeit contractually barred) resort to court sits uneasily with the policy favoring access to the courts, and a court should require the Landlord to establish the breach and materiality in the summary proceeding rather than treating termination as automatic.

## How It Should Be Enforced in This Dispute

- **Yellowstone motion: properly denied.** The motion was the functional equivalent of the waived declaratory relief; the Tenants contractually waived the right to seek it.
- **Complaint: properly dismissed.** Each claim was actual or thinly disguised declaratory relief within the waiver's plain text.
- **The Tenants' preserved defenses must actually be heard.** Dismissal of the DJ action is not adjudication of the merits. The Tenants' three substantive positions — (a) the alleged violations (permits, fire hazards, nuisances, blocked sprinkler inspections) did not occur or were not their breaches; (b) the permits were the Landlord's responsibility; (c) the Landlord waived objection by accepting rent for years with knowledge of the conditions — remain fully available as defenses in the summary proceeding, and the dispositive questions they raise (breach, responsibility for permits, waiver) must be decided there, not left unanswered by the dismissal.
- **Fee award: granted.** Under the clause's express terms, the Landlord is entitled to its costs and attorney's fees actually incurred in opposing the denied injunction.
- **Termination cross-claim: not automatic.** The Landlord may plead breach of ¶67(H) as a substantial breach, but termination of two 20-year leases is a forfeiture that must be proven and strictly justified in the summary proceeding; the filing of the DJ action alone, without more, should not mechanically forfeit the tenancies.

**Bottom line:** the clause is valid and enforceable as a forum-channeling waiver; it bars the DJ action and the Yellowstone injunction and supports the fee award, but it does not extinguish the Tenants' defenses — it relocates them to the summary proceeding the parties bargained for — and its termination-for-breach remedy should be enforced, if at all, only upon strict proof in that proceeding.
