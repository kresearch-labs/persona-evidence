# Case 16 — Answer

**Issues presented.** Paragraph 67(H) presents four issues. (1) Whether a commercial tenant may contract away the right to bring a declaratory judgment action concerning the lease or a notice sent under it — it may, as to the standalone action, since declaratory relief is a procedural choice parties may channel by contract. (2) Whether the clause also waives the tenant's right to seek a Yellowstone injunction tolling the cure period — the Landlord's theory below — which its own text refutes. (3) Whether the Tenants' timely protective suit is itself a "breach of substantial obligations of the tenancy" warranting "immediate termination" of two 20-year leases. (4) How the clause's statement that "disputes be adjudicated via summary proceedings" operates — as a forum/method-selection provision — and how it interacts with the Tenants' merits defenses (disputed violations, Landlord's responsibility for permits, waiver by acceptance of rent with knowledge of the conditions).

**Construction.** The clause must be read sentence by sentence, as a whole, and against forfeiture.

1. The waiver sentence is narrow on its face: it waives only the *declaratory judgment action* "with respect to any provision of this Lease or with respect to any notice sent pursuant to the provisions of this Lease." It nowhere mentions injunctive relief, and it does not purport to strip the tenant of defenses to a notice to cure.

2. The fee-shifting sentence confirms that injunctive relief remains available: it allocates fees "[i]n the event injunctive relief is sought by Tenant and such relief shall be denied." A party does not draft a fee-shifting provision for a remedy it has waived; the sentence presupposes — and permits — Yellowstone-type applications, at the tenant's risk as to fees if the application is denied.

3. The "summary proceedings" sentence is a forum- and method-selection clause: it channels the *merits* of lease disputes into the summary proceeding rather than plenary or declaratory suits. It governs where and how disputes are adjudicated, not whether the tenant may preserve the status quo pending that adjudication.

4. The termination sentence is a forfeiture provision. New York construes lease forfeiture provisions strictly and against the drafter, and the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing bars converting a tenant's timely, protective application into a terminal breach.

**Enforcement in this dispute.**

- *The declaratory claim.* Enforce the waiver literally. A claim for a declaration "that the leases remained in force," aimed at a notice to cure sent pursuant to the lease, is precisely the action ¶67(H) waives; it should be dismissed as an independent action — but without prejudice to asserting the identical contentions (the violations are disputed; the permits are the Landlord's responsibility; years of rent acceptance with knowledge of the conditions waived the defaults) as defenses in the summary proceeding. A waiver of a forum is not a waiver of substantive defenses.

- *The Yellowstone application.* Grant it. The Tenants hold leases with cure periods, moved before the cure period expired (one day before, on the 15-day demand), and alleged the ability and desire to cure or contest the defaults — the classic showing under *First Natl. Stores, Inc. v. Yellowstone Shopping Ctr., Inc.*, 21 N.Y.2d 634 (1968), *Yellowstone v. Point Pleasant Holdings, Inc.*, 43 N.Y.2d 800 (1977), and *Jefpaul Garage Corp. v. Presbyterian Hosp. in City of N.Y.*, 61 N.Y.2d 442 (1984) (timeliness is jurisdictional; equity will not revive an expired cure period). Nothing in ¶67(H) waives this relief: the clause never mentions injunctions, and its fee-shifting sentence would be surplusage on the Landlord's reading that the Tenants "contractually waived the right to seek injunctive relief." Nor does the summary-proceedings sentence defeat the toll: the clause's own premise is that the tenancy will survive to be adjudicated in a summary proceeding, and the injunction is what preserves that premise. The injunction should toll the cure period pending determination of the dispute; if the Tenants ultimately lose on the merits, the Landlord recovers its fees under the clause — the parties' negotiated allocation, not a bar to the application.

- *The "immediate termination" counter-theory.* Reject it. Treating the Tenants' timely protective suit as a terminal breach would convert a clause designed to channel disputes into a trap forfeiting two 20-year leases for seeking, one day before the cure deadline, the very relief the clause's fee-shifting sentence contemplates. Strict construction of forfeitures and the covenant of good faith preclude that result; the clause's own chosen remedy for a denied injunction — fees, not termination — shows the parties did not intend otherwise.

- *The merits defenses.* Disputed violations, the Landlord's permit responsibility, and waiver by rent acceptance go to the validity of the notices; ¶67(H) itself directs that they be litigated as defenses in the summary proceeding. They are preserved, not adjudicated, on this motion practice.

- *A notice point.* The notice was styled a "Ten (10) Day Notice" yet demanded cure within 15 days; the operative 15-day demand controlled and the day-14 application was timely, but the discrepancy illustrates that notices are construed as a whole and liberally, to secure cure rather than forfeiture.

**Bottom line.** ¶67(H) is enforceable as written: it bars the standalone declaratory action (dismissed without prejudice to the same defenses in the summary proceeding), it does not bar the Yellowstone injunction (which should issue, tolling the cure period, with fee exposure to the Tenants if they lose on the merits), and it does not support terminating the leases for bringing the protective action. The trial court's disposition — denying the injunction and dismissing the complaint as "actual or disguised declaratory relief" — misreads the clause, turning a forum-selection and fee-shifting provision into a waiver of the tenant's right to defend its tenancy, a result its text and the law against forfeitures do not require.

In one line: Paragraph 67(H) waives only the standalone declaratory action and channels disputes into summary proceedings; it leaves Yellowstone relief intact — the injunction should toll the cure period, the declaratory claim should be dismissed without prejudice to the defenses, and the leases should not be terminated for bringing the protective action.
