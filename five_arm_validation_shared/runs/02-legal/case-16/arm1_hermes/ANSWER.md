# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-16

**The issues:** (1) whether a lease rider provision waiving "the right to bring a declaratory judgment action" — and conditioning injunctive relief with fee-shifting if denied — is enforceable against commercial tenants seeking a Yellowstone injunction and declaratory relief in response to a cure notice; (2) whether such waivers contravene public policy or access-to-courts norms; and (3) whether the waiver's own text limits it (it targets declaratory actions and failed injunctions, not every affirmative claim).

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **The waiver is enforceable as written between sophisticated commercial parties, but it does not reach everything the Tenants filed.** Freedom of contract governs commercial leases of this kind; parties may bargain away particular procedural routes, including jury trials, forum selection, and specific remedies, so long as they leave a meaningful remedy. Declaratory-judgment jurisdiction is procedural (a remedy, not a right), and contractually channeling disputes into an agreed procedural path (summary proceedings) is classic and enforceable.

2. **Read the waiver by its words — it is narrow.** Paragraph 67(H) does two things: (a) waives declaratory-judgment actions "with respect to any provision of this Lease or any notice" — i.e., the Tenants may not ask a court to declare their rights under the lease; and (b) shifts fees if the Tenants seek injunctions that are denied. It does **not** waive breach claims, defenses to the cure notice, or the Yellowstone injunction itself. A Yellowstone injunction is provisional protective relief preserving the status quo pending the adjudication of a cure dispute — it is not a "declaratory judgment action," and the lease's text nowhere names it. The trial court's conversion of every Tenants' claim into "actual or disguised declaratory relief" reads the clause far beyond its language; construction against overbreadth applies because this is a waiver — waivers are strictly construed against the party invoking them.

3. **The Tenants' path was proper.** Filing a plenary action with a Yellowstone motion the day before the cure period ran — the classic, orthodox way to preserve a tenancy under threat of termination — is the *preservation* mechanism, not a disguised declaratory action. They may litigate the cure-notice merits (violation disputes, landlord-responsibility for permits, waiver by accepting rent) in the plenary action or as defenses in the summary proceeding; what they may not do under 67(H) is obtain a *declaration* of lease validity as the vehicle. The waiver clause also cannot be read to make the mere filing of any lawsuit a "breach of substantial obligations" warranting lease termination — that reading (which would forfeit the lease for filing a claim) is penal, unconscionable in effect, and construes ambiguity against the drafter.

4. **No public-policy bar, but no textual reach either.** New York law permits such waivers among commercial parties (no statute invalidates them); the clause fails not on policy but on scope.

**Practical ruling:** reverse — the Yellowstone motion and the plenary action are permissible; 67(H) bars only declaratory-judgment actions as the vehicle and fee-shifts denied injunctions. The Tenants' waiver-of-cure defenses and breach claims proceed; the Landlord's cross-motion to dismiss (and its lease-termination theory premised on the filing itself) fails.

**Key issues flagged:** enforceability of procedural waivers in commercial leases · Yellowstone injunctions are protective, not declaratory · waivers strictly construed against the drafter · a filing-is-breach reading is penal and fails.
