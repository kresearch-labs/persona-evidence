# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 02-legal case-19

**The issues:** (1) what the task orders' stated "total price corresponding to the price of fourteen classes" obligates — a ceiling with payment per class ordered, or a guaranteed minimum of fourteen classes; and (2) whether the termination-for-convenience clause of FAR 52.212-4, constructively invoked when the Government's needs fell short, caps the Contractor's recovery at unpleaded convenience-termination settlement costs.

**How it should be construed and enforced:**

1. **The contract requires payment for services actually ordered and performed — the ID/IQ structure defeats the guaranteed-fourteen reading.** In an indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity contract, by definition nothing orders the maximum until individual delivery/task issues it; the FAR-based task-order architecture the parties incorporated exists precisely so the Government orders what it needs, when it needs it. The task orders' "total price" line is the *not-to-exceed ceiling* for the lot — the arithmetic of fourteen classes times the per-class price — not a promise to buy fourteen. The Contractor's reading converts the "I" of ID/IQ into a requirements guarantee the contract never made, and would make the per-class price line meaningless (why price per class if the full number is owed regardless?). Payment per class actually taught, at the agreed per-class rate, is exactly what the incorporated terms command.

2. **The constructive-termination-for-convenience theory is sound and answers the damages question.** Where the Government, through its own conduct (using fewer than the maximum classes, substituting its own instructors), in effect terminates the unexercised balance of the maximum quantity without formal paperwork, the doctrine of constructive termination for convenience applies: the convenience clause is deemed invoked as to the unexercised portion. FAR 52.212-4(l) then limits the Contractor's recovery to costs incurred plus a proportionate profit on work performed up to termination, plus reasonable settlement costs — not anticipatory profits on the classes never ordered. The Government's partial performance (paying for each class actually taught) is consistent with this: it paid everything ordered and performed, and constructively terminated the remainder.

3. **The pleading wrinkle is curable, not fatal to the defense.** The Contractor sued for breach (total price). The government's constructive-termination position limits any recovery to convenience-termination settlement costs — a theory that changes the damages measure, not the liability framework. Courts in this posture allow the defense (and any amendment) because constructive invocation is a matter of the contract's own terms incorporated by reference; the Contractor had notice of the incorporated clause. On summary judgment, the Contractor's failure to plead entitlement to termination-settlement costs means no triable claim for the full stated price remains.

**Practical ruling:** affirm summary judgment for the Government. The Contractor is entitled to what it has been paid — the per-class price for classes actually ordered and taught — and any further recovery is limited to convenience-termination costs and profit on work performed, which it did not plead.

**Key issues flagged:** ID/IQ maximums are ceilings, not guarantees · task-order "total price" = ceiling arithmetic · constructive termination for convenience upon reduced needs · recovery capped at costs + profit on performed work + settlement costs.
