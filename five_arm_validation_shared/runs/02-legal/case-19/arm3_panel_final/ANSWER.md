The clause — FAR 52.212-4's termination-for-convenience provision ("the Government reserves the right to terminate this contract, or any part hereof, for its sole convenience"), incorporated by reference into an indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity (ID/IQ) contract and flowing down to every task order — presents four issues, and construed and applied correctly it defeats the Contractor's breach claim.

1. Incorporation and flow-down. The defense acquisition clause required all services to be ordered by task orders "subject to the terms and conditions of the underlying contract." Each task order is therefore governed by the contract's terms, including the termination-for-convenience clause; the Government need not have restated it in the task orders.

2. Meaning of the task orders' "total price." The dispute turns on whether the total price "corresponding to the price of fourteen classes" was a firm commitment to buy fourteen classes or a ceiling computed from the per-class price. In an ID/IQ contract the stated maximum is a ceiling on orders, not a guarantee of volume, and absent a stated minimum the Government is bound only for quantities actually ordered. The task orders' own structure — one lot, a price per class, and an aggregate — is most naturally read as a not-to-exceed total: the per-class price is the unit rate for payment, the total the price if all fourteen classes were ordered. The Government paid for every class actually taught at the agreed rate; that is performance of the task orders, not breach of them.

3. Constructive termination for convenience. A constructive termination arises where the Government, without formally invoking the clause, effectively ends performance — and the clause then caps recovery at the percentage of the price for work performed plus preparation and reasonable settlement costs, excluding anticipatory profit. Here, once it became clear the Government needed fewer than fourteen contractor-taught classes, its use of its own personnel and refusal to pay for untaught classes operated as a constructive invocation of the clause as to the unexercised balance. The Contractor sued for breach and pleaded no termination costs, so on this ground its recovery would be limited to convenience-termination settlement costs it never pleaded.

4. Limits on the clause. Termination for convenience cannot be used in bad faith or as a subterfuge to escape a firm commitment. That limit is not implicated: the contract committed the Government to no minimum quantity, so the clause operated exactly as written rather than as a device to convert a firm order into an optional one.

Construction and enforcement: the clause should be enforced per its plain terms — the Government may terminate "any part" of the contract for its sole convenience, with payment limited to work performed plus preparation and settlement costs. Applied here, the Contractor was entitled to the per-class price for each class taught (which it received), not the full stated price of the task orders, because no minimum quantity was ever ordered or guaranteed; and to the extent the Government constructively terminated the unneeded balance for its convenience, the Contractor's sole remedy is the clause's settlement-cost measure, which it did not plead. Summary judgment for the Government should be affirmed.

Bottom line: an ID/IQ maximum is a ceiling on orders rather than a promise of volume, and the incorporated FAR 52.212-4 clause caps any recovery for the unneeded balance at convenience-termination settlement costs — not the full task-order price.
