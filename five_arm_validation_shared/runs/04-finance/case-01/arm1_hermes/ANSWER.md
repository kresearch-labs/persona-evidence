# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 04-finance case-01 (Enron, as of Nov 8, 2001)

**Recommendation: SELL.**

**Rationale (as known on the date):**

1. **The restatement is not a one-quarter event — it destroys the basis for valuation.** Enron disclosed on November 8, 2001 that nearly *five years* of earnings would be recalculated, with a $1B write-off and a $1.2B equity reduction. When the reported history itself is in doubt, every valuation multiple built on it (and every model built on those multiples) is arbitrary. A holder cannot know what the earnings power is; that is not a "hold" condition, it is an information vacuum — and vacuums resolve badly for concentrated shareholders.

2. **The off-balance-sheet partnership web is the accounting equivalent of an iceberg report.** The Senate scrutiny and independent reviewers' "questionable items" findings mean the disclosed picture was materially incomplete during the very years the profits were booked. Until the true consolidated balance sheet is known, the equity may be worth materially less than even the restated numbers suggest.

3. **Liquidity and ratings are converging into a death spiral.** Junk downgrades trigger collateral calls under the partnership agreements and the trading contracts — for a trading business, the *franchise is the balance sheet*. Counterparties pull credit lines from a junk-rated trader; trading revenue collapses; the collapse forces more downgrades. At $19/share with $1.2B of equity already vaporized, the downside is plausibly total loss and the "still enormous trading franchise" is the first asset a downgrade impairs, not the last.

4. **Management vacuum at the worst possible moment** (Skilling's August resignation, uncertainty through the bench) removes the one thing that could credibly navigate a restructuring.

5. **The recovery case requires information that does not exist yet.** The "trade at $19 on an unknowable future" argument is a speculation, not an investment case for a client's capital — and the prudent recommendation on Nov 8 is to exit into what remains of the market for the stock before the junk downgrades land in full. (History's verdict — December 2 bankruptcy, shares at 67 cents — was consistent with what the November 8 disclosures already implied.)

**Bottom line:** sell; the restatement removes the informational floor, the ratings spiral removes the liquidity, and the trading franchise cannot survive the balance sheet's collapse. Re-entry (if any) belongs *after* the bankruptcy process clarifies what survives — a different decision with different information.
