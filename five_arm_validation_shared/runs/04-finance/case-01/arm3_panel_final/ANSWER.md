**Recommendation: Sell.**

As of November 8, 2001, the numbers a shareholder would normally rely on no longer exist. The restatement rewrites nearly five years of reported earnings and cuts shareholder equity by $1.2 billion — and those are only the losses Enron has chosen to disclose; the off-balance-sheet partnerships behind them remain unmeasured by anyone outside the company. Every pillar of the investment case has given way at once: the CEO resigned abruptly in August, the CFO was pushed out in October amid the partnership questions, Congress and regulators are examining what the filings actually disclosed, and the stock has lost roughly 80% from its peak. The one asset left — the energy-trading franchise — is the part most exposed, because a trading business lives on counterparty confidence: once lenders and counterparties doubt the balance sheet, credit lines, collateral calls and trading volumes can drain the company within weeks, faster than any energy-asset value can offset.

The recommendation hangs on one assumption: that the disclosed $1 billion write-off and $1.2 billion equity reduction cap the hidden liabilities. The restatement itself is evidence against that assumption, and any rescue — a sale or refinancing — would likely price the equity at a fraction of its November level. The risk is asymmetric: limited upside against a credible path to near-total loss, so the client should sell now and let the company prove its numbers before re-entry.

One-line justification: Sell — five years of reported earnings were just declared unreliable, the off-balance-sheet losses behind them are still unmeasured, and a confidence-driven run on the trading franchise can destroy the equity faster than its asset value can support it.
