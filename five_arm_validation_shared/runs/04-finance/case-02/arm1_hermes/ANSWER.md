# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 04-finance case-02 (WorldCom, as of June 24, 2002)

**Recommendation: SELL.**

**Rationale (as known on the date):**

1. **The math of the balance sheet is unforgiving and the stock no longer offers equity-like payoff.** ~$30B of debt against a company whose stock has lost >90% since December 2001 means the equity is an option — but an option on a *refinancing* the company cannot obtain. Surviving required: repaying/rolling the debt, new bank terms, and a demand recovery in corporate telecom. None was materializing; every one was deteriorating.

2. **The core business is in secular decline, not cyclical trouble.** Long-distance prices were falling through the floor and business demand was weak — meaning the cash flow that services $30B of debt is shrinking at exactly the moment refinancing terms are tightening. Overexposure relative to competitors compounds it: the industry strain hits the most-leveraged player hardest and first.

3. **Delisting risk is imminent and mechanical.** Below $1 for 30 consecutive trading days triggers Nasdaq delisting. A delisted, junk-adjacent telecom in a shrinking sector loses institutional holders, index funds, and the marginal buyer — liquidity vanishes precisely when the company must roll debt.

4. **Accounting scrutiny removes the last floor.** When management credibility is under question at a levered company, every prior financial statement becomes uncertain, and lenders price that uncertainty into every negotiation. The "averaging down under a dollar" temptation is averaging into an instrument whose payoff is now dominated by refinancing luck, not business quality.

5. **There is no visible catalyst** — no asset sales announced, no debt restructured, no demand inflection. "It's cheap" is not a reason to own; a dollar stock can go to a dime. (July 2002's $11B accounting fraud disclosure and Chapter 11 — the largest in U.S. history at the time — followed from exactly the stresses visible on June 24.)

**Bottom line:** sell. Total-loss risk dominates; any client who wants telecom exposure at the cycle's bottom should be advised toward a healthier balance-sheet competitor, not the most indebted name in the sector. Equity in a levered secular-decline business mid-audit is not a bargain — it's a lottery ticket with a toll.
