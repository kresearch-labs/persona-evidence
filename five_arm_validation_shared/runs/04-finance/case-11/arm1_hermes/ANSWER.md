# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 04-finance case-11 (Uber, as of June 4, 2019)

**Recommendation: HOLD (if held from the IPO — the position is a venture-style bet that has already been made); AVOID/DO NOT BUY for new client money at ~$70B valuation.**

**Rationale (as known on the date):**

1. **The losses are the valuation.** ~$3.1B quarterly revenue growing 67% with ~$1B net loss per quarter; ~$70B market cap on a business losing $1.8B+ a year. The bull case requires that rides, Eats, and freight eventually generate returns on capital in dense marketplaces — a plausible end-state. But as of this date management has offered **no timeline to profitability**, framing losses as perpetual investment. A company without a stated path to profit cannot be valued on earnings; it must be valued on revenue multiples, and $70B is a rich revenue multiple for a transportation business with thin per-ride economics.

2. **The unit economics are the unresolved question.** Drivers take the majority of each fare; competition (Lyft, regional players) persists in *every* market, meaning the take-rate cannot simply be raised without churn; and insurance, incentives, and regulatory costs are structural. The bull case's "improving efficiency as density rises" has been the pitch for a decade of losses — density has risen dramatically and the losses have too. At some point a marketplace must show operating leverage. It has not yet.

3. **Why hold, not sell, for existing holders:** the position was made at the IPO; the downside from here is a further de-rating, but the company has ~$70B of real network value, category leadership, a fast-growing Eats business, and enormous optionality (autonomy, freight, new mobility). Post-IPO lockup pressure will come (insiders can sell after 180 days — a known overhang), and a further slide is likely; but a *forced* sale at 6.8% below offer in the first weeks is the worst kind of price-sensitive discipline. Hold with an explicit thesis and a review date.

4. **Why avoid for new money:** at $70B, the buyer is paying for the *successful* version of the platform thesis before any proof of profitability appears. The three-year track record of recent mega-IPOs (all priced for perfection) and the coming lockup expiry argue for waiting for a materially better entry — ideally after the first demonstration that ride-hailing unit economics can bend toward profit in at least one mature market.

**Bottom line:** hold existing positions as sized-down venture bets with a thesis review at the lockup expiry and the next two quarterly prints (watch: Eats growth, ride-hailing take-rate discipline, any profitability timeline). New client money: wait. A great company is not automatically a great stock at $70B pre-profit — and the burden of proof now sits with Uber.
