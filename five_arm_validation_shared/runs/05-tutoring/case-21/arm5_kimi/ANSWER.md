# Case 21 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes Southern secession in 1860–61 was driven by an abstract constitutional principle — "states' rights" — and that slavery was not really the issue because most white Southerners did not own slaves. This confuses the *argument used to defend* secession (states' rights) with the *cause* of secession. The specific right the seceding states were defending was the right to preserve and expand slavery; "states' rights" was the legal language wrapped around that one issue, not a neutral principle the South applied consistently.

## 2. The correct concept, explained for the student

Ask: "states' rights to do *what*, exactly?" A principle by itself doesn't start a war — there is always a specific policy underneath it. In 1860–61 that policy was slavery.

The evidence is direct, and you can check it yourself:

- **The seceding states said so in writing.** South Carolina's secession declaration complains that Northern states were hostile to "the institution of slavery." Mississippi's says, "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery — the greatest material interest of the world." Georgia and Texas say the same. These are the states' own explanations of why they left.
- **Confederate leaders said so.** Vice President Alexander Stephens' "Cornerstone Speech" (1861) declared that the Confederacy's foundation rested on "the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery... is his natural and normal condition."
- **The South only liked states' rights when it protected slavery.** When Northern states used *their* states' rights to refuse to return escaped slaves, the South demanded federal power override them (the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850). So the principle was a tool, not the goal.

And the "most Southerners didn't own slaves" point doesn't remove slavery as the cause. Only about a quarter of Southern families held slaves, but the whole Southern economy and social order rested on slavery: non-slaveholders rented enslaved workers, hoped to buy slaves someday, sold crops and goods to plantations, and were told that slavery guaranteed even the poorest white man a higher status. People fight for systems they benefit from indirectly, not just ones they personally own a piece of.

**How to use this on a similar problem:** whenever you see a war or conflict explained by a vague principle ("rights," "honor," "tradition," "way of life"), ask *which specific interest or policy the principle was protecting*, and check what the people involved said and did at the time. For the Civil War, that test points squarely at slavery as the primary cause of secession.
