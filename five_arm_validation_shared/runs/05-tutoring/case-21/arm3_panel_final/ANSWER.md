# Case 21 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

The student's answer is built by four reasoning steps, and each one carries a specific flaw:

1. **"The Civil War was mainly about states' rights."** The student takes a slogan at face value without asking the question that unlocks it: *a state's right to do what?* "States' rights" names a legal mechanism, not a cause. A cause has content; a right is only ever a right *to something*.
2. **"The North wanted the federal government to control everything."** This frames 1860 as a generic power struggle. But in 1860 the North was not seeking to control "everything" — the one federal power actually in dispute was over slavery's expansion into the western territories, and Lincoln had just won on exactly that single-issue platform.
3. **"Slavery wasn't really the issue, since most Southerners didn't even own slaves."** This is a true statistic used to draw a false conclusion. It assumes only direct owners had a stake in slavery. In fact, non-slaveholding whites were tied into the slave economy (renting enslaved laborers, selling crops through it, aspiring to own), had a social and racial status stake in the system, and benefited politically from it (the Three-Fifths Clause inflated the South's power in Congress and the Electoral College). Meanwhile, the men who actually wrote and signed the secession ordinances were largely slaveholders acting to protect slave property.
4. **"The states' rights position means the South was defending a constitutional principle, not any particular policy."** This is the core error: it treats the principle as a cause that exists *separate from* the policy it was applied to. A principle nobody invokes except to defend a specific policy cannot be the primary cause of an action taken to defend that policy.

**The misconception in one sentence:** the student has detached "states' rights" from the thing it was a right *to* — treating an abstract constitutional principle as the cause of secession, when the principle was the legal wrapper around the actual cause: protecting and expanding slavery.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

Here is how I would explain it to this student:

**Start from what you already know.** You already know the South *said* "states' rights" — that part of your answer is right, and it's a real phrase from the period. The fix is not to throw it out; it's to ask the follow-up question the slogan is hiding: a state's right to do *what*? When you ask that, the answer collapses into slavery.

**Let the secessionists speak for themselves.** The strongest evidence isn't a modern textbook — it's what the states that seceded actually wrote at the moment they did it:

- South Carolina's "Declaration of the Immediate Causes" says the Union was being broken because northern states would not enforce the Fugitive Slave Act and because Lincoln was elected on a platform hostile to slaveholding.
- Mississippi's declaration opens: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery."
- Georgia's and Texas's declarations likewise list slavery — its protection, its expansion into new territories, and the return of fugitives — as the reasons for leaving.

If "states' rights in the abstract" were the cause, these documents would say so. They name slavery, specifically and repeatedly.

**Notice that the Confederacy itself restricted states' rights — about slavery.** The Confederate Constitution barred the Confederate Congress from ever passing any law "denying or impairing the right of property in negro slaves" (Art. I, Sec. 9(4)) and required every state, including its own, to deliver up fugitive slaves (Art. IV, Sec. 2). So the new nation that supposedly existed for states' rights tied its states' hands on exactly one subject: slavery. That tells you which one was the cause and which one was the language.

**Check the timing and the pattern.** The Nullification Crisis of 1828–33 was the loudest states'-rights confrontation in American history — tariffs, a state defying federal law — and nobody seceded over it. Secession came in 1860–61, immediately after an election decided entirely on stopping slavery's expansion. And the slave states that stayed in the Union (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri) claimed the same constitutional rights as the ones that left — what predicted secession was not how strongly a state believed in states' rights, but how deeply it was committed to slavery.

**So the correct concept is:** the primary cause of Southern secession was the protection and expansion of slavery. "States' rights" was the constitutional *argument used to defend* that cause — the wrapper, not the contents. Historians weigh causes by looking at the actors' own words at the time, the timing of the event, and who actually acted; on all three tests, slavery is the cause and states' rights is the rhetoric.

**A method you can reuse on a similar problem.** When a question asks for the *primary* cause of a historical action:

1. **Separate the principle from the policy.** Ask "a right/freedom/liberty to do *what*?" — the specific answer is usually the cause.
2. **Read the actors' own words from the time** (declarations, speeches, ordinances), not later slogans or summaries.
3. **Check the timing:** does the alleged cause line up with *when* the event happened?
4. **Check the pattern:** if the alleged cause were primary, who would have acted, and did they?

Try it on this one: *"What was the primary cause of Virginia's secession in April 1861 — resistance to federal coercion, or slavery?"* Step 1 asks: coercion over what? Step 2 sends you to Virginia's own ordinance of secession, which says the federal government had perverted its powers "to the oppression of the Southern slave-holding States." Step 3 checks the timing: Virginia left only after Fort Sumter and Lincoln's call for troops — which is exactly where the "coercion" slogan comes from. Step 4 checks the pattern: the Deep South had already seceded, over slavery, months before anyone was coerced. Same method, new question — and now you can tell a cause from a slogan.
