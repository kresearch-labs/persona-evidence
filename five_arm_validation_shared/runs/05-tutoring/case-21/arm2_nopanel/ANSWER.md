1. The misconception: the student treats "states' rights" as a self-contained principle — as if the South seceded for an abstract idea of self-government, with slavery as an unrelated side issue. This is the "Lost Cause" framing. Two errors sit behind it: (a) it never asks which right the states were actually claiming — the specific right in dispute was the right to own enslaved people and to take that "property" into the territories; and (b) it assumes that because most white Southerners owned no slaves, slavery couldn't be the central issue. In fact slavery structured the whole Southern economy, politics, and social order, so non-slaveholding whites still had a direct stake in it, and the planter class that did own slaves controlled the state governments that voted to secede.

2. Explaining it to the student: A "states' rights" argument is never just about rights in the abstract — you always have to ask, "a state's right to do what?" The seceding states answered that question in writing, and their own words settle it. Mississippi's secession declaration opens: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery — the greatest material interest of the world." South Carolina's declaration complains that Northern states were not returning escaped slaves, and Georgia's lists slavery in its first sentence. Alexander Stephens, the Confederacy's vice president, said in 1861 that slavery was the "cornerstone" of the new nation. If states' rights in general were the cause, you would expect these documents to defend many different rights — they don't; they defend slavery specifically.

The principle argument also fails a test you can apply yourself: did the South actually believe in limiting federal power? No — it demanded strong federal power whenever federal power protected slavery. It wanted the federal Fugitive Slave Act enforced over Northern states' objections, and it demanded a federal slave code for the western territories. Meanwhile, the Confederate Constitution took a states' right away: it forbade Confederate states from passing any law freeing slaves. So the South favored states' rights on slavery and federal power on slavery — the constant was slavery, not the principle.

Finally, "most Southerners didn't own slaves" doesn't prove slavery wasn't the issue. In 1860 about a quarter of white Southern families held slaves, but the region's wealth, its export economy (cotton), its political leadership, and poor whites' own social status all rested on the slave system — and secession was decided by conventions of that planter-led elite, not by a referendum on abstract constitutional theory.

So the correct concept: states' rights was the argument used to defend the institution of slavery, not a separate cause. One-line justification: the seceding states' own declarations of causes, the Confederate Constitution, and the Cornerstone Speech all name slavery as the specific right and central issue, which contradicts the "abstract principle" reading.
