# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-21 (causes of Southern secession: "states' rights" vs. slavery)

## 1. The misconception

The student has fallen for the **"states' rights, not slavery" myth**, built from two connected errors:

1. **Detaching the principle from the policy.** "States' rights" is never a cause all by itself — it is an *argument someone makes about a specific disputed policy*. The student treats it as a free-floating principle ("not any particular policy"), but the states that seceded left detailed written records naming the particular policy in dispute: slavery. Saying the South seceded over "states' rights" without slavery is like saying a divorce happened over "legal paperwork" without mentioning the marriage.

2. **The "most Southerners didn't own slaves" fallacy.** The student assumes that if most individuals didn't own slaves, slavery couldn't have motivated secession. That doesn't follow. Roughly one quarter of white Southern *families* held slaves — nearly half in South Carolina and Mississippi — and political power was concentrated in that class. More importantly, non-slaveholders still had huge stakes: jobs, credit, and cotton prices all ran through the slave economy, and the racial hierarchy gave poorer whites status and security regardless of wealth.

The student also misses an internal contradiction: the South embraced *federal* power whenever it protected slavery (the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 forced Northern citizens to help capture escapees; secessionists demanded a federal slave code for the territories) and objected when *Northern* states exercised their own states' rights (personal liberty laws). A group fighting purely for a principle wouldn't switch sides depending on whose interests were served.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Here's how historians settle questions like this: **go to the primary sources and ask what the people involved actually said at the time.** The seceding states didn't leave us guessing — each published an official "Declaration of the Causes of Secession." Read them, and one issue dominates:

- **Mississippi** opens with: *"Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery — the greatest material interest of the world."*
- **Texas** and **Georgia** center their complaints on Northern hostility to slavery and the push to keep slavery out of the western territories.
- **South Carolina** attacks Northern states for refusing to return escaped enslaved people.
- Once independent, the **Confederate Constitution** explicitly guaranteed property in slaves — hard to do if slavery "wasn't really the issue."

And the trigger fits: secession began immediately after **Lincoln's 1860 election**, won on a platform of blocking slavery's *expansion* into the territories. Southerners saw containment as the first step toward destruction.

Try this analogy: imagine your town threatens to break away from the county, and every official letter it publishes is about the **landfill** the county wants to site there. Years later, someone summarizes it as "the town stood up for local decision-making." Technically true — but it hides the actual disagreement. "States' rights" was the *mechanism* the South used; **slavery was the thing at stake.**

Finally, fix the "most didn't own slaves" logic: picture a town where a quarter of families own farms. Everyone else still depends on farm wages, bank loans, and crop prices — so when farm policy comes up, nearly the whole town votes to protect farming. Non-slaveholding whites similarly depended on the slave economy and on a racial order that promised them status no matter how poor they were. You don't have to own the asset to be invested in the system.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** A classmate argues: "The South seceded over tariffs, not slavery. The famous states'-rights fight over tariffs was the Nullification Crisis of 1832, and besides, most Southerners weren't planters." Evaluate this claim using evidence from 1860–61.

**Solution:** The claim fails on both timing and evidence. By 1860 tariffs were already low (Tariff of 1857), the Republican platform proposed no new ones, and the Nullification Crisis was nearly thirty years in the past. Meanwhile, the seven states that seceded before the war each issued an official declaration, and slavery dominates all of them: Mississippi identifies its position "with the institution of slavery"; Texas and Georgia name the exclusion of slavery from the territories as the core grievance; South Carolina condemns Northern states for not returning escaped enslaved people. The Confederacy then wrote explicit slavery protections into its constitution. When the actors' own contemporaneous documents repeat one issue over and over, that issue — not an abstract principle or a decades-old tariff dispute — is the primary cause.
