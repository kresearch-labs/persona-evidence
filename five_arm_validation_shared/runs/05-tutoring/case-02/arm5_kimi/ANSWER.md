# Case 02 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes that to "move" a term to the other side of the equation you keep the same operation — they saw "+3" and added 3 again — instead of applying the **inverse operation**. Solving an equation means undoing what was done to x, and undoing "+3" means **subtracting** 3, not adding it. (A related gap: they didn't keep the equation balanced — adding 3 only to the right side breaks the equality. The "do the same thing to both sides" rule they remembered from class was applied to the division step but not to the addition step.)

Correct solution: 2x + 3 = 11 → subtract 3 from both sides → 2x = 8 → divide both sides by 2 → **x = 4**. (Check: 2·4 + 3 = 11 ✓ — the student's x = 7 gives 2·7 + 3 = 17 ≠ 11.)

## 2. Explanation for the student

Think of the equation as a balance scale: "2x + 3" on the left weighs exactly the same as "11" on the right. Your job is to get x alone **without tipping the scale** — whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other.

Right now x has two things attached to it: it's multiplied by 2, and 3 is added. To peel those off, you **undo** each one with its opposite operation, in reverse order:

- The "+3" is undone by **subtracting 3**. Do it to both sides: 2x + 3 − 3 = 11 − 3, so 2x = 8.
- The "×2" is undone by **dividing by 2**. Do it to both sides: 2x ÷ 2 = 8 ÷ 2, so x = 4.

Two habits that protect you:
1. **Opposite, not same.** When you move or remove a term, flip its sign/operation: + becomes −, × becomes ÷. You added 3 because you saw "+3" — but removing it requires the opposite.
2. **Always check.** Plug your answer back into the original problem. Try yours: 2(7) + 3 = 17, not 11 — that tells you something went wrong. Try x = 4: 2(4) + 3 = 11 ✓.

Now you try one: solve 3x + 5 = 20. (Undo +5 first: 3x = 15; then undo ×3: x = 5. Check: 3·5 + 5 = 20 ✓.)
