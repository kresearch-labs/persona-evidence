1. The misconception: the student treats the sign they *see* as the operation to *do* — "it says + 3, so I add 3" — instead of using the **inverse (opposite) operation to undo** what was done to x. They are reading the equation as a left-to-right recipe to follow rather than a balance to keep while peeling operations off x in reverse order. Tellingly, they used an inverse correctly on the very next step (÷ 2 to undo × 2), so the inverse-operation idea is already half-built; it just wasn't applied to the + 3.

2. Explaining it to the student:

"Your last step was actually right — dividing by 2 is exactly how you undo 'times 2'. The slip is the first step, and here's the trick.

An equation is a record of what was done to x. In 2x + 3 = 11, x was multiplied by 2, *then* 3 was added, and the result was 11. To get x back, you retrace those steps backwards — and every backwards step uses the **opposite** operation: the opposite of + 3 is − 3, the opposite of × 2 is ÷ 2. If you see + 3 and add 3 again, you're walking the same direction twice, so the number just keeps growing. And whatever you do to one side, do to the other, so the equation stays balanced.

Undo in reverse order:
2x + 3 = 11
Subtract 3 from both sides → 2x = 8   (opposite of '+ 3')
Divide both sides by 2 → x = 4        (opposite of '× 2' — the part you already knew!)

Check it: 2(4) + 3 = 8 + 3 = 11. ✓

Now you try: **3x + 5 = 20.** Ask yourself: what two things happened to x? (× 3, then + 5.) Undo them backwards — subtract 5 first, then divide by 3. If you land on x = 5, check it: 3(5) + 5 = 20. ✓"
