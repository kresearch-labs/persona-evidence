# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-08 (3 ÷ 1/2)

## 1. The misconception

Two misconceptions are stacked here: (1) "dividing always makes smaller" — true only when dividing by numbers *greater than 1*; and (2) a mechanical slip — the student divided 3 by the *2* (the denominator), treating 1/2 as if it were just 2, instead of dividing by the actual quantity "one half." The deep misunderstanding: division asks **"how many of the divisor fit in the dividend?"** — and when the divisor is a fraction less than 1, the answer is *more* than the dividend, because each piece is smaller.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

"Forget the procedure for a second and answer the question in words: **how many halves are in 3?**

Take 3 candy bars. Cut each one into halves. Each bar gives you 2 halves, and you have 3 bars: 3 × 2 = **6 halves**. That's what 3 ÷ 1/2 means — 'how many 1/2-pieces fit in 3 whole things?' The answer is 6. Notice it's *bigger* than 3 — dividing by a number smaller than 1 always gives you a bigger answer, because you're counting how many small pieces fit.

Your reasoning — 'dividing always makes smaller' — is a rule that only works for divisors bigger than 1. Check it against small divisors: 6 ÷ 1 = 6 (same size). 6 ÷ ½ = 12 (bigger!). 6 ÷ ¼ = 24 (even bigger — each piece is tinier, so more of them fit).

The formal procedure you'll use in class — **multiply by the reciprocal** ('keep–change–flip'): 
3 ÷ 1/2 = 3 × 2/1 = **6**.

Why does flipping work? Because '÷ 1/2' asks how many halves fit, and there are 2 halves in every 1. So every 1 of the 3 contains 2 halves: 3 × 2 = 6. Dividing by a fraction and multiplying by its flip are the same question asked two ways.

And the check that would have caught it: **reverse the operation.** If 3 ÷ ½ = 1½, then 1½ × ½ should give back 3. But 1½ × ½ = ¾ — nope. If 3 ÷ ½ = 6, then 6 × ½ = 3 ✓. The check works."

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problems)

- *How many quarters (1/4) are in 2?* → 2 ÷ ¼ = 2 × 4 = **8 quarters** (each dollar holds 4 quarters; you have 2 'dollars' worth). 
- *4 ÷ 1/3?* → 4 × 3 = **12 thirds**. 
- *Now a tricky one: 1/2 ÷ 1/4 = ?* → keep-change-flip: ½ × 4/1 = **2** (two quarters fit in one half — check with real coins!).

**Rule to remember:** dividing by a number less than 1 makes the answer *bigger* — you're counting how many small pieces fit inside. And ÷ a fraction = × its flip.
