# Case 06 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student has correctly learned that similar figures scale by a factor, and has overgeneralized that rule from lengths to *every* quantity attached to the figure. The precise edge of the error is the difference between quantities **made of length** and quantities that are **comparisons of lengths**:

- Anything measured in length units scales with the figure: sides ×2 (the student did this part correctly), perimeter ×2, area ×2² = ×4.
- Anything that is a ratio of two lengths does **not** change, because both lengths get multiplied by the same factor and it cancels out: angles, steepness/slope, trig ratios.

An angle is a comparison — an amount of *turn* between two sides — not an amount of length. So "everything doubles" is true for the sides and false for the angles: angle measures are **invariant under similarity**. The student is treating an angle as stuff that grows with the figure, the way length does.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

You got the first half right: 3, 4, 5 → 6, 8, 10 is exactly how scaling works. Here's the piece to add.

**An angle isn't a length — it's a comparison of two lengths, and comparisons don't scale.**

You already know this from fractions: 3/4 = 6/8. Double the top and the bottom and the fraction doesn't move. Angles work the same way. Think of the 3-4-5 triangle's sharpest corner as a ramp: it rises 3 for every 4 it goes across. Now double the ramp — it rises 6 for every 8. Is the bigger ramp steeper? No: 6/8 = 3/4. Same steepness, same angle. The sides doubled; the *tilt* — the angle — didn't, because both numbers in the comparison doubled together.

You can see it another way: enlarge the triangle on a photocopier. Every side gets longer, but every side keeps pointing in the same direction it pointed before. A corner is just the gap between two directions — and neither direction changed, so the gap can't change. A right angle is a right angle whether its arms are 3 cm long or 3 m long.

That's literally what "similar" means: **same angles, different sizes.** The angles are the thing that stays the same; the sides are the thing that changes.

And the angle sum: every triangle's angles add to 180° — a tiny one, a huge one, any of them. You used that rule yourself when you said the small triangle's angles sum to 180°. The big triangle has the *same three angles* as the small one (about 37°, 53°, and 90°), so its angles also sum to **180°**. If doubling a triangle doubled its angle sum, a triangle's angles would depend on its size — but measure any triangle of any size and you always get 180°.

**A rule to carry to the next problem:** when a figure is scaled by a factor k, count how many lengths are inside the quantity:

| Quantity | Made of | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Side lengths, perimeter | 1 length | × k |
| Area | length × length | × k² |
| Angles | length ÷ length (a comparison) | unchanged |

**Try it:** A triangle has angles 40°, 60°, 80°. A similar triangle has sides 5 times as long. What is the largest angle of the larger triangle, and what do its angles add up to? *(Answer: 80° and 180° — the sides changed, the angles didn't.)*
