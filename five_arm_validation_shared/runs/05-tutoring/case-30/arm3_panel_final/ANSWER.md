# Case 30 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is treating **nominal income (dollars) as if it were real income (buying power)** — often called the *money illusion*. Their reasoning — "44,000/40,000 = 1.10, so she is 10% better off" — compares her salary only to its own old value and never checks it against the 10% rise in prices. "More dollars means more buying power" is only true when prices stay fixed; here prices rose by exactly the same 10% as her pay, so the two increases cancel out.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

**Your salary in dollars is not the same thing as what your salary can buy.** To find out whether Maria is better off, you have to compare two changes, not one:

- Her pay went up by a factor of 44,000 / 40,000 = **1.10** (a 10% raise).
- Prices went up by a factor of **1.10** (a 10% rise in the cost of the basket).

Buying power is always **salary ÷ price level**. In 2020 that was 40,000 ÷ 1. In 2025 it is 44,000 ÷ 1.10 = **40,000** — exactly what it was before. Every dollar is worth 10% less than it used to be, and she has 10% more dollars, so the two effects cancel: Maria can buy **the same amount** of goods, not 10% more.

Another way to see it: buying power grows by the **pay factor ÷ price factor** — here 1.10 ÷ 1.10 = 1.00, a 0% change.

A quick shortcut for problems like this: compare the two growth rates.

- Pay raise **bigger** than inflation → she can buy more.
- Pay raise **equal** to inflation → same buying power.
- Pay raise **smaller** than inflation → she can buy less.

Here 10% = 10%, so the answer is "the same."

**Try it yourself:** In 2020 Carlos earned $50,000 and by 2025 he earns $52,500, while prices rose 8%. Is he better off, worse off, or the same? (His raise is 52,500/50,000 = 1.05, i.e. 5%, which is less than 8% inflation — so he can buy less: 52,500 ÷ 1.08 ≈ $48,611 of 2020 buying power.)
