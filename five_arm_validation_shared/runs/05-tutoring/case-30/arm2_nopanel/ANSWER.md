# Case 30 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is confusing **nominal income** (the number of dollars earned) with **real income** (what those dollars actually buy — purchasing power). Their reasoning assumes "more dollars = more goods," which is only true if prices stay the same. Because prices rose 10% over the same period, the extra 10% in salary is exactly used up by higher prices, so the raise buys nothing extra. The student simply never compared the salary change to the price change.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

**Real income = nominal income ÷ price level.** A salary only means something relative to what things cost, so every time you compare money across years, you must divide by how much prices changed.

Here is how it works for Maria:

- Think of a "basket" of typical goods that cost $100 in 2020.
- In 2025 that same basket costs $110 (prices rose 10%).
- In 2020, Maria's $40,000 bought 40,000 ÷ 100 = **400 baskets**.
- In 2025, her $44,000 buys 44,000 ÷ 110 = **400 baskets**.

Same 400 baskets — so Maria can buy **the same amount** of goods, not 10% more. Her 10% raise was completely canceled by 10% inflation.

A quick shortcut: **% change in real income ≈ % change in nominal income − % change in prices.** For Maria: 10% − 10% = 0%, so no change in purchasing power.

**Try a similar one:** In 2020, Ana earned $50,000. By 2025 she earns $52,500, and prices rose 8% over the same period. Can Ana buy more, less, or the same amount of goods in 2025?

(Check yourself: her raise was 52,500/50,000 = 1.05, i.e. 5%, but prices rose 8%. Since 5% < 8%, her real income fell — she can buy *less*. Using baskets: the $100 basket now costs $108, and 52,500 ÷ 108 ≈ 486 baskets versus 50,000 ÷ 100 = 500 baskets in 2020.)
