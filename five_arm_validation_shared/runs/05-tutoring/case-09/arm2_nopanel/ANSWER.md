# Case 09 — Seasons

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

The student believes seasons are caused by **how far Earth is from the Sun** — "closer = hotter = summer, farther = colder = winter." This is the classic **distance misconception**. The hidden (and reasonable-sounding) idea behind it is: "Heat comes from the Sun, so nearer must mean warmer."

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

The real cause is Earth's **tilt**, not its distance. Here's how I'd explain it:

---

**Great guess — but here's a clue that distance can't be the answer:** Earth is actually *closest* to the Sun in **January**, when it's winter in the United States! If distance caused the seasons, January would be the hottest month. So something else must be going on.

**The real reason: Earth is tilted.** Earth spins like a top, but it's a leaning top — tilted about as much as a clock hand leaning a little past 3 o'clock. Earth keeps that same lean the whole year while it travels around the Sun.

Because of the lean, for half the year the **top half of Earth (where the U.S. is) leans *toward* the Sun**, and for the other half it leans *away*.

- **Leaning toward the Sun (July):** sunlight hits your part of Earth more *straight on*, like a flashlight pointed right at a wall — the light is strong and concentrated in one bright spot. Your days are also longer, so there's more time to warm up. That's **summer**.
- **Leaning away from the Sun (January):** sunlight hits your part of Earth at a *slant*, like a flashlight tipped sideways — the same light gets spread out over a bigger area, so each spot gets less. Days are shorter, too. That's **winter**.

**Try it:** In a dim room, shine a flashlight straight down at the floor — see the small, bright, hot circle? Now tip it sideways — the light spreads into a big dim oval. Same flashlight, same distance, but the tilt changes how strong the light feels. That's exactly what the tilt does to sunlight.

**Bonus check you can now solve:** In July, when it's summer here, it's *winter* in Australia. Why? Because when the top half of Earth leans toward the Sun, the bottom half leans *away* — opposite halves of a leaning ball always point opposite ways. If distance were the cause, the whole planet would have summer at the same time — and it doesn't!
