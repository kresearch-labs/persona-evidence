# Case 09 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes the seasons are caused by Earth's **distance from the Sun** — that we have summer in July because Earth is closest to the Sun then, and winter when Earth is farthest away. Their reasoning ("heat comes from the Sun, so closer must mean warmer") sounds logical, but it is wrong: Earth's orbit is nearly a circle, so the change in distance over the year is too small to cause seasons. In fact, Earth is near its **farthest** point from the Sun in early July! A quick test also disproves it: if distance caused summer, the whole planet would have summer at once — but when it is summer in the United States, it is winter in Australia.

## 2. The correct concept, explained for this student

The seasons are caused by the **tilt of Earth's axis**, not by distance from the Sun.

Earth spins on an imaginary line through its middle called its axis, and that axis is **leaning over** — tilted about 23.5 degrees, like a slightly tipped spinning top. As Earth travels around the Sun over the year, the axis keeps pointing the same direction in space. So for part of the year the **Northern Hemisphere** (where the United States is) is tilted **toward** the Sun, and for part of the year it is tilted **away**.

Why does tilting toward the Sun make it warmer? Two reasons:

1. **More direct sunlight.** Try this with a flashlight: shine it straight down at a table and the light makes a small, bright, concentrated spot. Now tilt the flashlight so the light hits at a slant — the same light spreads out into a bigger, dimmer patch. Sunlight works the same way. In July, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, so sunlight hits it more directly — a concentrated, stronger beam that heats the ground well. In winter the same sunlight arrives at a slant, spreads out, and delivers less heat.

2. **Longer days.** When your hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun, the Sun stays in the sky longer each day, so there is more time for the Sun to warm things up. Short winter days mean less heating time.

So: **summer in July happens because the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun then — giving direct, concentrated sunlight and long days — not because Earth is closer to the Sun.** Six months later, Earth has moved to the other side of its orbit, the Northern Hemisphere tilts away from the Sun, and we get winter (while the Southern Hemisphere, now tilted toward the Sun, gets its summer).

**How to solve a similar problem:** whenever you are asked why a place has summer or winter, ask "which way is that hemisphere tilted?" — toward the Sun means summer, away from the Sun means winter. Distance from the Sun is never the answer, because Earth's distance barely changes during the year.
