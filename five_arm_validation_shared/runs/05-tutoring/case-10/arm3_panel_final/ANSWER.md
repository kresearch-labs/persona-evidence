1. The misconception: the student believes mass and weight are the same quantity — that "weight" is just a synonym for mass (so both are measured in kilograms and both are fixed properties of a person that never change). It comes from everyday language, where the doctor's scale reports "weight" in kilograms, and from the fact that on Earth the two are proportional, so the difference never shows up in daily life.

2. Explaining it to the student:

Mass and weight are two different things, and the Moon problem is exactly the situation where the difference shows up.

- Mass is the amount of matter in you — how much "stuff" you're made of. It's measured in kilograms. Nothing about moving to the Moon removes any of your atoms, so your mass is still 60 kg there.
- Weight is a force: the pull of gravity on that mass. Because it's a force, it's measured in newtons, not kilograms, and it's calculated as:

  weight = mass × gravitational field strength  (W = m × g)

  On Earth g ≈ 9.8 N/kg, so the astronaut's Earth weight is 60 kg × 9.8 N/kg ≈ 588 N (about 600 N).
  On the Moon, gravity is 1/6 as strong, so g ≈ 1.6 N/kg, and her weight is 60 kg × 1.6 N/kg ≈ 98 N (about 100 N).

So the answer to the problem: mass on the Moon = 60 kg (unchanged); weight on the Moon ≈ 98 N (one-sixth of her Earth weight).

Why the doctor's scale says "kilograms": a bathroom or doctor's scale actually measures the force you press on it with (your weight in newtons), but it's calibrated for Earth's gravity — it divides that force by 9.8 to display the mass that would produce it. That's why the readout is in kilograms. Take the same scale to the Moon and it would read about 10 kg, even though her mass is still 60 kg — the scale is reporting a smaller force, not a smaller person.

Quick self-check you can do: your mass never changes when you change location; your weight only changes when the local gravity changes. If a problem gives you a new planet or moon, keep the mass in kg and recompute the weight with W = m × g using that place's g.
