# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-10 (mass vs. weight and how gravity changes weight)

## 1. The misconception

The student believes **mass and weight are the same thing** — just two words for one quantity — so both must stay fixed no matter where you are. Their reasoning shows *why* this feels true: in everyday life we say the doctor "measures your weight in kilograms," which blurs the two ideas together.

There are actually two linked errors here:

1. **Conceptual:** Treating "mass" and "weight" as synonyms rather than as two different physical quantities (one is *stuff*, the other is a *force*).
2. **Units:** Using kilograms for both. Kilograms measure mass; weight is a force and should be measured in **newtons (N)**. When the doctor says "your weight is 60 kg," that's really your *mass* — everyday language is sloppy here, physics isn't.

Because the student thinks mass = weight, they conclude nothing changes on the Moon, and they miss the whole point of the question: that weight *depends on local gravity* while mass does not.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Here's the distinction that makes every version of this problem easy:

- **Mass** = the amount of matter in you. It's a property of *you*, not of where you are. Measured in **kilograms (kg)**. Your mass is the same on Earth, the Moon, or floating in deep space.
- **Weight** = the gravitational *force* pulling on that mass. It depends on both your mass **and** the local strength of gravity. Measured in **newtons (N)**.

They're connected by one formula:

$$W = m \times g$$

where *g* is the acceleration due to gravity. On Earth, g ≈ 9.8 m/s²; on the Moon, g ≈ 1.6 m/s² (about 1/6 of Earth's).

**Apply it to the astronaut:**

- **Mass on the Moon:** still **60 kg**. She's made of the same atoms wherever she goes. ✅ (The student got this part right!)
- **Weight on Earth:** W = 60 kg × 9.8 m/s² ≈ **588 N**
- **Weight on the Moon:** W = 60 kg × 1.6 m/s² ≈ **96 N** — only about 1/6 of her Earth weight ❌ (This is where the student went wrong: weight is *not* 60 kg, and it definitely isn't unchanged.)

**A concrete way to picture it:** imagine stepping on a bathroom scale, which works by measuring how hard gravity squeezes its spring. Take that scale to the Moon and the spring gets squeezed only 1/6 as hard, so the scale reads far less — even though *you* (your mass) haven't changed. A useful memory trick: your **mass** travels with you; your **weight** is the handshake between your mass and whatever planet you're standing on. Different planet, weaker handshake.

So the rule to remember: **mass never changes with location; weight = m × g always does.**

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg on Earth. Mars's gravity is about 3.7 m/s² (roughly 0.38 times Earth's). What are the astronaut's mass and weight on Mars? (Use g_Earth = 9.8 m/s².)

**Solution:**
- **Mass on Mars:** 80 kg — mass never changes with location.
- **Weight on Earth:** W = 80 × 9.8 = 784 N
- **Weight on Mars:** W = 80 × 3.7 = **296 N** (about 0.38 of her Earth weight)

Notice the pattern: the number of kilograms stays put, but the number of newtons follows the local gravity. If you can state the mass in kg *and* compute W = m × g with the local value of g, you've mastered this problem type.
