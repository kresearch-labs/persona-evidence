# Case 10 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes **mass and weight are the same thing** — that "weight" is just another word for mass, so neither can ever change. This is reinforced by everyday language: doctors and bathroom scales report "weight" in kilograms, so the student concludes a kilogram is a kilogram anywhere. In physics, though, mass and weight are different quantities with different units, and only mass is location-independent.

## 2. The correct concept

**Mass** is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in kilograms and does not depend on where you are. The astronaut's mass is 60 kg on Earth, on the Moon, and floating in deep space.

**Weight** is the *force* of gravity pulling on that mass. It is measured in newtons (N) and is calculated as:

**W = m × g**  (weight = mass × gravitational field strength)

- On Earth, g ≈ 9.8 N/kg, so her Earth weight is 60 × 9.8 ≈ **588 N**.
- On the Moon, g is about 1/6 as strong (≈ 1.6 N/kg), so her Moon weight is 60 × 1.6 ≈ **98 N** — exactly 1/6 of her Earth weight.

So the correct answer to the problem: **mass on the Moon = 60 kg (unchanged); weight on the Moon ≈ 98 N (about one-sixth of her ~588 N Earth weight).**

Why the doctor's scale is misleading: a scale actually responds to the gravitational force on you, but it is *calibrated* for Earth's gravity and labeled in kilograms, so on Earth it conveniently reads your mass. Take that same scale to the Moon and it would read about 10 kg — not because your mass changed, but because the scale is really measuring force and converting with Earth's g. A balance (comparing against known masses) would read 60 kg on any world.

**Check yourself:** if a 12 kg rock is taken to the Moon, its mass is still 12 kg, and its weight is 12 × 1.6 ≈ 19 N (one-sixth of its ~118 N Earth weight). Mass never changes with location; weight scales with the local gravity.
