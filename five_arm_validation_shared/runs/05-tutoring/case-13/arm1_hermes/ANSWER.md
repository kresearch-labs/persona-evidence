# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-13 (evaporation vs. boiling)

## 1. The misconception

The student believes that **a liquid can only turn into a gas if it's heated to its boiling point** — in other words, that boiling and evaporation are the same thing, or that 100°C is a magic "gate" water must pass through to become a gas. This leads them to conclude that since the towel was never heated to 100°C, the water couldn't have escaped, so it "must still be there somehow."

The flaw in this reasoning is treating temperature as something *all* the molecules in a liquid share equally. In reality, temperature is just the **average** speed of the molecules — at any moment, some molecules are moving much faster than others, and those fast ones at the surface can escape into the air long before the whole liquid reaches 100°C.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Here's the key idea: **evaporation and boiling are two different things.**

- **Boiling** happens at 100°C (for water). That's when bubbles of water vapor form *throughout* the whole liquid — you see them rising and popping everywhere.
- **Evaporation** happens at *any* temperature, even 20°C or colder. It only involves molecules at the **surface** of the liquid.

Think of the water molecules like a crowd of people walking around a mall. The "temperature" is the *average* walking speed — but not everyone walks at exactly the average! Some people are sprinting. If a sprinter happens to be near the exit (the surface of the water), they can walk right out. No rule says the *whole crowd* has to reach sprinting speed before anyone leaves.

So in the wet towel:
1. Water molecules are constantly jostling and bumping into each other.
2. A few of the fastest ones near the surface have enough energy to break free and fly off into the air as **water vapor** (an invisible gas).
3. Over hours, millions upon millions of these molecules escape, one by one — until the towel is dry.

You've seen this happen lots of times: a puddle from rain disappears on a day far cooler than 100°C. Your wet hair dries without a hair dryer. A drop of hand sanitizer evaporates almost instantly at room temperature — and feels *cold* while doing it, because the fastest (highest-energy) molecules are the ones escaping, leaving the slower, cooler ones behind!

Bonus point for the towel: the escaping molecules don't come back easily, especially in moving air, so the water steadily transfers from the towel to the air. That's why hanging a towel out to dry works — no heating required.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** A small dish of water is left on a windowsill at 22°C. After a few days, the dish is empty, and the water is gone — yet the water was never boiled. Explain where the water went, and why this can happen well below 100°C.

**Solution:** The water evaporated. Even at 22°C, the molecules in the water are moving, and some are moving much faster than average. The fastest molecules at the surface have enough energy to escape into the air as water vapor. Over several days, more and more molecules escape until none are left in the dish. Boiling at 100°C isn't needed for a liquid to become a gas — boiling is just the special case where vapor forms as bubbles *throughout* the liquid. Evaporation at the surface happens at any temperature.
