# Case 13 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes a liquid can only become a gas if it is heated all the way to its **boiling point** (100°C for water). In other words, they are treating "boiling" as the only way liquid water turns into vapor, so a towel at room temperature "can't" dry. This confuses **boiling** (a fast process happening throughout the liquid, at 100°C at normal pressure) with **evaporation** (a slow process happening only at the surface, at *any* temperature).

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

Water doesn't need to boil to become a gas — it is evaporating all the time, at any temperature, even in your room at 20°C.

Here's why: water is made of tiny particles (molecules) that are always moving. They don't all move at the same speed — some are fast, some are slow. The fastest ones sit near the surface, and if one is moving fast enough, it can break free from the other water molecules and fly off into the air as water vapor. That's evaporation. It happens one molecule at a time, at the surface only, which is why it's slow — but it never stops.

Boiling is different: at 100°C, *so many* molecules have enough energy that vapor forms everywhere inside the liquid, not just at the surface — that's the bubbling. Boiling is just evaporation happening extremely fast and throughout the whole liquid.

So overnight, the towel's water molecules kept escaping into the air one by one. The room's air (and the towel's surroundings) supplied the small amount of energy each escaping molecule needed — no heating to 100°C required. By morning, all the water had left the towel as vapor mixed into the air, and the towel was dry.

**Check yourself with a similar problem:** a puddle on the sidewalk disappears on a 15°C day without ever boiling. Where did the water go? (Answer: it evaporated — surface molecules gained enough energy from the warm surroundings to escape into the air, even far below 100°C.)
