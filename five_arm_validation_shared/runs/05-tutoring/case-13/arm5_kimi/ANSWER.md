# Case 13 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes that a liquid can only become a gas by being heated to its boiling point (100°C for water) — that boiling is the *only* way water turns into vapor. This confuses **boiling** with **evaporation**. Because of this, the student concludes that water at 20°C cannot possibly have turned into a gas, so it "must still be in the towel."

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Water can turn into a gas at **any** temperature — it doesn't need to boil. Here's why:

Water molecules are always moving and jiggling, and they don't all move at the same speed. At the surface of the water, some molecules happen to be moving fast enough to break free from the liquid and escape into the air as an invisible gas called **water vapor**. This slow, quiet process is called **evaporation**, and it happens at any temperature — even from a puddle on a cold day, or from your wet towel at 20°C.

**Boiling** is different: it's the fast, bubbling version that happens throughout the whole liquid, and only at the boiling point (100°C for water). Boiling is one *special, rapid* way water becomes a gas — but evaporation is happening all the time at ordinary temperatures, without any bubbles or heating.

So what happened to your towel? Overnight, water molecules at the towel's surface kept escaping into the room's air, one by one, until the water was gone. The water didn't stay in the towel and it didn't need to be boiled — it **evaporated** and is now water vapor mixed into the air in the room.

You can see the same idea everywhere: puddles drying up after rain, sweat drying off your skin, and dishes drying in a rack — none of them ever reach 100°C, yet the water disappears. Evaporation happens faster when it's warmer, when air is moving (like a breeze), and when the water is spread out over a large surface — which is exactly why a towel hung open on a line dries overnight.
