# Case 13 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

**"A liquid can only turn into a gas at its boiling point (100°C for water)."** The student has merged two different changes of state into one. Their chain of reasoning: the water is gone → for water to become a gas it must have boiled → boiling needs 100°C → the room was only 20°C → so the water can't have left. The hidden false step is the first one: boiling is not the only way a liquid becomes a gas.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

Water becomes a gas in two different ways, and boiling is only the fast, dramatic one:

- **Evaporation** happens at *any* temperature — even 20°C, even in a fridge. A liquid is made of molecules that are all moving, but not at the same speed; some are always faster than the rest. At the water's surface, the fastest molecules have enough energy to break free of the liquid and fly off into the air as water vapour. That is water as a gas, with no boiling at all.
- **Boiling** is what happens at 100°C: the molecules are so energetic that vapour forms bubbles *throughout* the liquid, not just at the surface. That's why it's fast and you can see it.

So 100°C is not "the temperature water needs to become a gas" — it's the temperature at which evaporation goes wild and happens everywhere at once. Below that, evaporation still happens, just slowly, one surface molecule at a time.

Overnight, the water molecules in the wet towel kept escaping from the surface into the room air, a few at a time. The energy they needed came from the towel and the air themselves — anything at 20°C already contains heat energy, so nothing had to be heated to 100°C. After hours, every molecule had escaped, and the towel was dry. (You can even see evidence of this: a damp towel feels slightly cool, because the escaping molecules carry energy away with them.)

**Rule for similar problems:** if a liquid disappears without being heated to boiling — a puddle, wet hair, a towel, sweat — it *evaporated*: a slow change from liquid to gas that happens at the surface at any temperature below the boiling point.
