# Case 16 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes that **acceleration means only a change in speed** ("speeding up"), so constant speed implies zero acceleration. In physics, acceleration is the rate of change of **velocity**, and velocity is a vector — it has both a magnitude (speed) *and* a direction. Changing direction at constant speed is still acceleration. The student is using the everyday meaning of "acceleration" (pressing the gas pedal) instead of the physics meaning (any change in the velocity vector).

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Your speedometer tells you the car's *speed*, but it can't tell you the car's *velocity*. Velocity includes direction, and on a circular track the direction is changing every instant — north, then east, then south, then west. Since velocity is changing, the car **is** accelerating, even though the needle never moves.

You can feel this yourself: when a car turns, your body is pushed toward the outside of the curve. That push is real evidence of acceleration — if the car truly had zero acceleration, it would move in a perfectly straight line (Newton's first law), and you would feel nothing.

The problem even tells you where the acceleration comes from: friction between the tires and the road. That friction is a **centripetal force** pulling the car toward the center of the circle, producing a **centripetal acceleration** of

$$a = \frac{v^2}{r}$$

directed toward the center of the track — perpendicular to the car's motion. It doesn't make the car faster; it keeps bending the car's path into a circle.

**How to check yourself on a similar problem:** don't ask "is the speed changing?" — ask "is the *velocity* changing, in magnitude **or** direction?" If either one changes, the object is accelerating, and there must be a net force causing it. So: a satellite in a circular orbit at constant speed? Accelerating. A car braking in a straight line? Accelerating. A car cruising straight at constant speed? *That* is the only one with zero acceleration.
