# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-16 (uniform circular motion & centripetal acceleration)

## 1. The misconception

The student believes **acceleration only means "speeding up."** They're treating acceleration as a change in *speed* (a number on the speedometer), when in physics acceleration means a change in *velocity* — and velocity includes both **how fast** you're going **and which direction** you're going.

Because the car's speedometer reads a steady 60 km/h, the student concludes nothing about the motion is changing. But the car's *direction* is changing every instant as it moves around the circle — and a change in direction is just as much an acceleration as a change in speed. This is one of the most common misconceptions in introductory physics: "constant speed = no acceleration."

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Here's the key idea: **velocity is not the same thing as speed.**

- **Speed** is just a number: 60 km/h.
- **Velocity** is a number *plus* a direction: 60 km/h *heading north*, or 60 km/h *heading east*.

**Acceleration** is any change in velocity over time. That gives us three ways to accelerate:
1. Speed up,
2. Slow down,
3. **Change direction.**

Your car on the circular track is doing #3 constantly. At one moment it's heading north at 60 km/h; a few seconds later it's heading northeast at 60 km/h; later still, east at 60 km/h. The *number* never changed, but the *velocity* did — because the direction did. So **yes, the car is accelerating**, even though the speedometer needle never moves.

Here's a way to *feel* it: think about riding in a car that turns a corner at a steady speed. You get pressed against the door, right? Your body is trying to keep going straight (that's inertia), and the car has to push you sideways into the turn. That sideways push exists precisely because your motion is changing direction — you're accelerating. If there were truly no acceleration, the ride would feel exactly like driving straight.

There's also a neat clue hidden in the original question itself. It says the car is *"held on the circle by friction."* Remember Newton's second law: **F = ma**. A net force always produces acceleration. Friction from the road is pushing the car toward the center of the circle (this is called the **centripetal force**), so the car must have an acceleration pointing toward the center too — called **centripetal acceleration**. No acceleration would mean no net force, and without that inward friction the car would skid off in a straight line, tangent to the circle.

So the full picture: constant speed ✔, changing direction ✔ → changing velocity → **accelerating**, with the acceleration pointed at the center of the circle.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** A cyclist coasts around a circular bend at a perfectly constant 15 km/h. (a) Is the cyclist accelerating? (b) If so, what direction does the acceleration point, and what force provides it?

**Solution:**
(a) **Yes.** Even though the speed is constant, the cyclist's direction is changing continuously around the bend, so her velocity is changing — and a change in velocity is acceleration.
(b) The acceleration points **toward the center of the circular path** (centripetal acceleration). The force providing it is **friction between the tires and the road**, pushing the bike inward. (If the road were perfectly frictionless — say, covered in ice — the bike couldn't turn and would slide straight ahead.)
