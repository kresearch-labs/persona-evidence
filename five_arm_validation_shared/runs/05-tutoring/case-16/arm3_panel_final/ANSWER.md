# Case 16 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

The student believes **"acceleration = speeding up."** They have collapsed acceleration into one component only — the change in the *magnitude* of velocity — and dropped the other half entirely: **direction**. Two specific errors follow from it:

- They treat the speedometer reading as if it measured the whole of the car's velocity ("the speedometer reads a constant 60 km/h, so the acceleration is zero"). The speedometer only reports speed; it says nothing about the direction of motion, which is changing every instant on the circle.
- Their supporting claim "nothing about its motion is changing" is false: on a circular track the *direction* of motion changes continuously, and that is a change in motion.

So the root misconception is: **constant speed ⇒ no acceleration**, i.e. thinking of acceleration as a scalar change in speed rather than a vector change in velocity.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

You actually got one thing exactly right: the car's **speed** really is constant — the speedometer proves it. The missing piece is that speed is only half of what physics calls **velocity**. Velocity means *how fast* **and** *which way*. Acceleration means *any change in velocity* — speeding up, slowing down, **or turning**.

Here's the test that makes it click. Imagine the friction suddenly vanished (ice on the track). What would the car do? It would keep moving in a straight line, tangent to the circle — Newton's first law. It would *not* keep following the curve. So something must be actively bending its path inward: that something is friction gripping the tires and pushing the car **toward the center of the circle**. A push that changes your direction of motion *is* an acceleration, even if your speed never changes. That inward acceleration is called **centripetal acceleration**, and its size is a = v²/r — it points at the center at every instant, which is exactly why it changes your *direction* but not your *speed*. That's why the speedometer stays at 60 while the car accelerates the whole time.

So the answer to the original question is: **yes, the car is accelerating** — constantly, toward the center of the circle, and friction is the force producing it.

Quick self-check you can carry to any similar problem:

> **Is the direction of motion changing? Then the object is accelerating — even at perfectly constant speed.**

Try it: a cyclist takes a curve at a steady 15 km/h — accelerating? Yes, toward the inside of the curve; friction between tires and road supplies it. A ball swung on a string in a horizontal circle at steady speed — accelerating? Yes, toward your hand; the string's tension supplies it. Constant speed never cancels acceleration when the path bends.
