# Case 24 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes "less" is an all-purpose word meaning "a smaller amount" that can go in front of *any* noun. They are judging by size ("mistakes are small things") instead of by whether the noun can be counted. They haven't yet learned that English splits these words by noun type: **fewer** for things you can count one by one, **less** for amounts you measure as a whole.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

Here's how I'd explain it to this student:

**Ask yourself one question: "Can I count it?"**

- If you can put a number in front of the noun and count the items — *one mistake, two mistakes, three mistakes* — use **fewer**.
  - fewer mistakes, fewer cookies, fewer chairs, fewer people
- If it's one big amount that you *measure* instead of count — like time, water, money, or sugar — use **less**.
  - less water, less sugar, less homework, less time

**Now check the two blanks in your sentence:**

- "Mistakes" can be counted (you could number them: mistake 1, mistake 2...), so it needs **fewer**: "I made **fewer** mistakes on my essay."
- "Twenty minutes" is a single amount of time — you're not counting minutes one by one, you're naming one lump of time. Amounts like this use **less**: "it took me **less** than twenty minutes." (Same pattern: less than $10, less than a mile.)

So you actually got the second one right — the rule was already working for you there!

**Quick test to remember:** *fewer* + things you can count (usually plural nouns) · *less* + amounts you measure (things with no plural).

**Try this one yourself:** "There are ___ students in class today, and we have ___ time for lunch than yesterday."
(Answers: **fewer** students — you can count them; **less** time — it's one measured amount.)
