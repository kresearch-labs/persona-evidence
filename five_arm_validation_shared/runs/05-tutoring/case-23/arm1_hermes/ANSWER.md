# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-23 (affect vs. effect — commonly confused words)

## 1. The misconception

The student has **reversed the two words** — they believe *effect* is the action (verb) word and *affect* is the naming (noun) word. In reality, it's almost always the other way around: **affect** is the verb ("to influence something") and **effect** is the noun ("the result"). Because the student got the roles backwards, they swapped both answers in the sentence.

A second layer of the misconception is the belief that *"the two spellings are basically interchangeable anyway."* They aren't — they are different words with different jobs in a sentence, which is exactly why English keeps both spellings. The student is also guessing from **sound** (the words are pronounced similarly) instead of from **meaning and grammar**, which is an unreliable strategy for homophone-type pairs.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Here's the rule that will solve almost every affect/effect problem:

- **Affect = Action word (verb).** It means *to change or influence something.*
  - Trick: **A**ffect = **A**ction.
  - Example: "The loud music **affected** my concentration." (The music did something — it changed my ability to focus.)
- **Effect = End result (noun).** It names *the thing that happens because of something else.*
  - Trick: **E**ffect = **E**nd result.
  - Example: "The loud music had a bad **effect** on my concentration." (The *result* was poor focus.)

**A quick test:** If you can put the word "an" in front of it, you want *effect*. You can say "an effect," but "an affect" almost never sounds right.

Now let's fix the original sentence using these tools:

> "The cold weather had a bad ___ on my health..."

After the words "a bad," you need a **naming word** (you'd say "a bad *end result*"), so it's **effect**: "...had a bad **effect** on my health."

> "...and it really ___ how I felt all week."

Here the word needs to be an **action** — something the weather *did* to your feelings — so it's **affected**: "...it really **affected** how I felt all week."

So the corrected sentence is: *"The cold weather had a bad **effect** on my health, and it really **affected** how I felt all week."*

(Rare bonus fact: once in a while *effect* shows up as a verb meaning "to make happen," as in "to effect change" — but that's uncommon. Stick with **A = Action, E = End result** and you'll be right nearly every time.)

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

Fill in the blanks with *affect* or *effect*:

"Eating breakfast can ______ how well you focus at school, and teachers have noticed that ______ in their morning classes."

**Solution:** First blank = **affect** (it's the action — breakfast *does something*: it influences your focus). Second blank = **effect** (it's the end result being noticed — you could say "that *result*"). Completed sentence: *"Eating breakfast can **affect** how well you focus at school, and teachers have noticed that **effect** in their morning classes."* Remember: ask yourself "Is this word doing an action (**A**) or naming an end result (**E**)?"
