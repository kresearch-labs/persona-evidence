# Case 29 — Answer

## 1. The misconception
The student is confusing **correlation with causation**: they assume that because two variables rise and fall together, one must be causing the other. In reality, an observed association between ice cream sales and shark attacks does not mean buying ice cream causes shark attacks (or vice versa). The likely explanation is a **lurking (confounding) variable** — hot summer weather — which drives *both*: more people buy ice cream in hot months, and more people swim in the ocean in hot months, so more shark encounters occur.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student
"Correlation does not imply causation. When two variables move together, there are several possibilities: (a) X causes Y, (b) Y causes X, (c) a third variable causes both, or (d) coincidence. Your data only shows the two move together — it can't tell you which story is true. Here, a third factor, summer heat, increases both ice cream sales and beach swimming, which is why shark attacks rise. Cutting ice cream sales wouldn't keep anyone out of the water, so it wouldn't reduce shark attacks. To claim causation, you need more than an observed association — typically a controlled experiment that holds other factors constant. So whenever you see two things correlated, ask: 'What else could explain both?'"

**Justification:** The student's error is the classic correlation–causation fallacy; the association is produced by a lurking variable (summer weather/ocean swimming), so intervening on ice cream sales would have no effect on shark attacks.
