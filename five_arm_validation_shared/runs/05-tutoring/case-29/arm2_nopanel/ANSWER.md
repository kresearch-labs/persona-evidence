# Case 29 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is confusing **correlation with causation**. They see that two variables move together (ice cream sales and shark attacks) and conclude that one must be *causing* the other — and then pick a direction of cause and effect that the data never showed. The reasoning "the two variables move together, so one must be driving the other" is the exact error: correlation alone tells you the variables are related, not *why* they are related, and not which one (if either) influences the other.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Here's the thing your data can't tell you on its own: **when two variables rise and fall together, that's called a correlation — but correlation is not causation.** A correlation just says the two variables are *associated*. It never tells you which one causes which, or whether either one causes anything at all.

There are three possible explanations for any correlation:

1. **A causes B** — changing A really does change B.
2. **B causes A** — the arrow points the other way.
3. **A third variable (a confounding variable) causes both** — A and B move together only because something else is driving them both.

In the ice cream / shark attack case, the third explanation is the right one: **hot weather (summer)**. When it's hot, more people buy ice cream *and* more people go swimming in the ocean, which puts more people near sharks. Ice cream and shark attacks are both *effects* of the same cause, so they rise and fall together — but neither one influences the other. If everyone stopped buying ice cream tomorrow, people would still swim in warm weather, and shark attacks wouldn't change.

**How to handle a similar problem:** when you see two variables moving together, ask "could something else be causing both of these?" before claiming that one causes the other. Only a controlled experiment (or much stronger evidence than a correlation) lets you conclude causation.

**Quick check:** Months with more drownings also have more popsicle sales. Does banning popsicles prevent drownings? No — summer swimming is the confounding variable behind both. Same logic as the ice cream case.
