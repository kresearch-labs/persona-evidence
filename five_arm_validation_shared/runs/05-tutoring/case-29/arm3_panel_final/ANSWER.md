# Case 29 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

**Correlation implies causation.** The student correctly notices that ice cream sales and shark attacks move together (a correlation), but then takes the extra, unjustified step of concluding that one variable *drives* the other — and even picks a direction ("less ice cream → fewer attacks") without checking alternatives. Bundled inside this are two specific errors:

- Treating "the two variables move together" as proof that changing one will change the other.
- Never considering a **third variable (confounder)** that could be causing *both* variables to rise and fall together.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

You're right that the two variables move together — that observation is solid, and it's called a **correlation**. The problem is the leap to "so one must be driving the other." Moving together only tells you the variables are *related*; it doesn't tell you which one (if either) causes the other. Whenever two variables correlate, there are four possible explanations:

1. **A causes B** — ice cream sales really do drive shark attacks.
2. **B causes A** — shark attacks drive ice cream sales (unlikely here, but you should ask).
3. **A third variable C causes both** — something else makes both numbers rise and fall together.
4. **Coincidence** — the pattern is chance, especially in small samples.

In this case the missing piece is explanation 3: **hot, sunny weather**. Hot weather makes people buy more ice cream *and* it sends more people into the ocean to swim — and more swimmers means more opportunities for shark encounters. Ice cream sales and shark attacks rise and fall together only because both follow the temperature. Ice cream itself has no effect on sharks.

Here's a test you can run on any "X causes Y" claim built from correlational data: **ask what would happen if you changed X while everything else stayed the same.** Suppose a town closed all its ice cream parlors tomorrow during a hot July weekend. Would shark attacks drop? No — the beaches would still be packed, because the weather (the real cause) hasn't changed. When "change X alone" fails to change Y, the causal claim collapses.

**How to solve a similar problem:** when a study shows two variables moving together, (1) name the correlation, (2) brainstorm a plausible third variable that could cause *both*, (3) run the "what if we changed X alone?" test. Remember: only a **controlled experiment** — where the researcher actively changes one variable while holding everything else constant — can establish causation. Observational data showing things "moving together" can suggest a cause worth testing, but it can never prove one.
