# Case 22 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student has overgeneralized one rule: "an apostrophe means something belongs to someone." Since "Maria's book" shows possession with an apostrophe, they concluded that "you're" must be the possessive form of "you" — so it must be the right word before a noun. In fact, the apostrophe in "you're" does the opposite job: it marks a *contraction* (a missing letter), not possession.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

There are two different jobs an apostrophe can do, and you saw only the first one:

- **Job 1 — showing something belongs to someone:** "Maria's book" = the book belongs to Maria. The apostrophe + s means "belongs to."
- **Job 2 — squishing two words together (a contraction):** "you're" is a squished-together version of **you are**. The apostrophe is just a placeholder for the missing letter **a**. Read your sentence out loud: "You **are** going to love you **are** new phone" — the second one doesn't make sense, so it can't be "you're."

Here's the tricky part: **the possessive of "you" is "your" — with no apostrophe at all.** It's a possessive pronoun, and possessive pronouns never take apostrophes: *my, your, his, her, its, our, their*. (Compare: "its" = belongs to it, vs. "it's" = it is — same pattern as your/you're.)

**A quick test you can use every time:** if you can replace the word with "you are" and the sentence still makes sense, write "you're." If you can't, and the word shows something belongs to you, write "your."

So the correct sentence is: "**You're** going to love **your** new phone."
