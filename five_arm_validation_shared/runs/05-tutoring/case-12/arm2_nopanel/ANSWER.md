1. Misconception: The student treats the two forces of a Newton's-third-law pair as if they act on the same object, so they "cancel" and give zero net force. In reality an action–reaction pair always acts on two different objects, so its two forces can never cancel each other. A second misconception hides in "zero net force means no motion": zero net force means no *change* in motion (no acceleration), not no motion — an object with zero net force keeps whatever velocity it already has.

2. Correct concept: The two forces act on different things, so each object responds only to the forces applied to *it*. The truck pushes the car forward — that force acts on the car. The car pushes back on the truck — that force acts on the truck. The car accelerates because the forces on the *car* do not balance: the forward push from the truck is larger than the backward friction on the car, so the net force on the car is forward and F = ma gives an acceleration. The truck feels the car's backward push, but its engine makes the wheels grip the road, and the road pushes the truck forward; if that forward force from the road is at least as large as the car's backward push plus the truck's own friction, the truck keeps its speed or speeds up too. (The same-size force also gives the lighter car a larger acceleration than the heavy truck, since a = F/m.) To solve a similar problem: (1) pick one object; (2) draw a free-body diagram showing only the forces acting on that object — never both halves of an action–reaction pair on the same diagram; (3) add the forces as vectors to find the net force; (4) apply F = ma.
