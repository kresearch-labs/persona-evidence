## 1. The misconception

The student is treating the two third-law forces as if they act on the **same object**, so they can be added together into one net force. In the student's own reasoning — "Equal and opposite forces add to zero" — the truck's push on the car and the car's push on the truck are being summed as if they were two forces on one body. But an action–reaction pair always acts on **two different objects**, and forces can only cancel when they act on the *same* object.

A second, related error sits in the second half of the reasoning: "zero net force means no motion." That is the Aristotelian idea that motion needs a continuous net force. In fact zero net force means constant velocity (including staying at rest); motion itself needs no force — only *changes* in motion do.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Look closely at your own sentence: "the truck pushes the car" — pushes on **what**? On the car. "The car pushes the truck" — pushes on **what**? On the truck. Those are two different objects. The two forces never act at the same place, so they can never cancel each other — a force on the car can no more cancel a force on the truck than a push on your chair can cancel a push on you.

The reliable method is to pick **one object at a time** and add up only the forces acting on *that* object:

- **Forces on the car:** the truck's push forward, and friction/drag backward. If the push is bigger than the drag, the net force on the car is forward, so by Newton's second law (F = ma) the car accelerates. The car's backward push on the truck does not act on the car, so it plays no role in the car's net force.
- **Forces on the truck:** the car's push backward, plus the friction from the road on the truck's tires pushing forward. The truck accelerates because the road's forward push on it is larger than the car's backward push. That is where the "equal and opposite" reaction gets dealt with — the truck "pays" for pushing the car by needing the ground to grip its tires.

So Newton's third law doesn't prevent the car from accelerating; it only says the truck feels an equal backward push. Each object obeys the second law on its own separate set of forces.

**Try a similar one:** A horse pulls a cart forward, and the cart pulls back on the horse with an equal and opposite force. Why does the cart move? Solve it the same way: the cart feels only the horse's pull (forward) and friction (backward); when the pull exceeds friction, the net force on the cart is forward and it accelerates. The cart's pull on the horse is a force on the *horse* — it can only be balanced by the ground's push on the horse's hooves, never by anything acting on the cart.
