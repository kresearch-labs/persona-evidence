# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-12 (Newton's third law: action–reaction pairs)

## 1. The misconception

The student believes that **equal-and-opposite forces cancel each other out** — that because the truck pushes the car forward and the car pushes the truck backward with the same strength, these two forces "add to zero," giving zero net force and therefore no motion.

This misconception has two parts:

1. **Treating an action–reaction pair as if both forces act on the same object.** In reality, the two forces of a Newton's-third-law pair always act on *different* objects: the truck's push acts *on the car*, and the car's push acts *on the truck*. Forces can only cancel when they act on the *same* object. Since the two forces here act on different bodies, they can never cancel each other.

2. **Confusing "zero net force" with "no motion."** Zero net force means *no change in motion* (no acceleration), not necessarily no movement. An object with zero net force keeps doing whatever it was doing — sitting still *or* moving at constant velocity (Newton's first law).

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Here's the key rule: **forces only cancel when they act on the same object.**

When you analyze whether something accelerates, you must pick ONE object and look at all the forces acting *on it* — ignore forces that object exerts on anything else.

Let's do that for the car:
- Force on the car from the truck: forward, say 2000 N
- Friction/drag on the car: backward, say 1500 N
- **Net force on the car = 500 N forward → the car accelerates!**

Now do it for the truck:
- Force on the truck from the car: backward, 2000 N
- Force on the truck from the road (friction driving the wheels): forward, say 2500 N
- **Net force on the truck = 500 N forward → the truck also accelerates!**

Notice the car's push on the truck never appears in the car's force list, and vice versa. That's why they can't cancel — they're "billed" to different objects.

A concrete example: stand on a skateboard and push against a wall. You push the wall forward; the wall pushes you backward with an equal force. Those forces don't cancel — the wall doesn't budge (it's attached to the Earth), but *you* roll backward! If the forces truly canceled, you'd never move. Another everyday proof: every time you walk, you push the ground backward and the ground pushes you forward — equal and opposite, yet you accelerate down the hallway.

So Newton's third law doesn't prevent acceleration — it's actually *why* things accelerate: every push comes with a partner push on the other object, and each object responds to the force *it* receives according to F = ma.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** Two astronauts float in deep space, touching. Astronaut A (mass 60 kg) pushes astronaut B (mass 90 kg) with a force of 120 N.
(a) What force does B exert on A?
(b) Do these forces cancel? Find each astronaut's acceleration.

**Solution:**
(a) By Newton's third law, B pushes A with **120 N in the opposite direction**.
(b) No — the forces act on *different* astronauts, so they can't cancel.
- For A: a = F/m = 120 N ÷ 60 kg = **2 m/s²** (away from B)
- For B: a = F/m = 120 N ÷ 90 kg ≈ **1.3 m/s²** (away from A)

Both astronauts accelerate in opposite directions despite the forces being exactly equal and opposite — proof that action–reaction pairs don't cancel.
