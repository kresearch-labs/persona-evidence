# Case 19 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

The student treats the fall of the Roman Republic as a single, openly declared event: Caesar is killed (44 BC), Augustus immediately "takes over," announces himself emperor/king, and formally abolishes the Republic and the Senate. The underlying misconception is that a regime change must be a sudden, publicly proclaimed act — and that "emperor" is just another word for "king," so Augustus must have announced a new monarchy. In reality, the Republic's end was a gradual, disguised process: Augustus accumulated power over years while carefully preserving Republican titles and institutions, never calling himself king or emperor.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Your dates and your picture of what Augustus actually did need fixing — here's how it really went:

- **44 BC — Caesar's assassination did NOT end the Republic.** It triggered ~13 more years of civil war. Caesar's heir Octavian (later called Augustus) first shared power in the Second Triumvirate, then defeated his rivals — finally beating Mark Antony and Cleopatra at the Battle of Actium in **31 BC**.
- **27 BC — the traditional date the Republic "ends" and the Empire begins.** That's when Octavian, now sole ruler, took the honorific "Augustus." So he became the first emperor in **27 BC, not 44 BC** — thirteen years after Caesar's death, not "right after."
- **Augustus never declared himself emperor or king, and never abolished the Republic.** Romans hated the word "king" (rex) — it was partly why Caesar was killed. Augustus understood this, so he did the opposite of what you described: he claimed to be *restoring* the Republic. He kept the Senate, consuls, and elections running, and called himself simply **princeps** — "first citizen."
- **His real method was accumulating offices, not abolishing them.** He held tribunician power, supreme military command (imperium) over the key provinces and armies, and the office of pontifex maximus — all at once, for life. Each piece looked legal and Republican; added together, they gave one man total control. Historians call this system the **Principate**: monarchy in practice, republic in appearance.
- **The Senate kept meeting for centuries** — but real power (especially over the army) sat with Augustus and his successors.

**How to check yourself on similar questions:** when a government changes type, ask whether the change was announced or disguised, sudden or gradual. In Rome's case the key test is: did Augustus *say* he was ending the Republic? No — he said he was saving it. The Republic ended not with a proclamation but with one man quietly holding all the real power while the old institutions carried on as a façade.

*Justification: this diagnosis follows directly from the student's own stated steps — the 44 BC date, "declared himself emperor," "abolished the Republic," and the "emperor is basically a king" reasoning — each of which contradicts the historical record (Augustus took power in 27 BC after Actium, ruled as princeps, and preserved Republican institutions).*
