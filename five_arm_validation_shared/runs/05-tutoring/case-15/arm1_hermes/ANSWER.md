# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-15 (density and floating)

## 1. The misconception

The student believes **floating depends on an object's absolute weight**: "light things float, heavy things sink." In this view, there's some weight threshold — under it, you float; over it, you sink.

This fails immediately against everyday evidence: a giant 200-kilogram log floats, while a tiny 5-gram coin sinks. If weight decided floating, that would be impossible. The real deciding factor is **density** — how tightly packed the stuff inside an object is compared to water — not how many grams it weighs. The student also misses that the ice cube isn't being compared to "water in general"; it's competing against the *specific amount of water it pushes aside* when placed in the glass.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Here's the key idea: **an object floats if it is less dense than water, and sinks if it is more dense — no matter what it weighs.**

Density means how much mass is squeezed into a given amount of space. Think of two boxes the same size: one stuffed with feathers, one stuffed with rocks. The rock box is denser.

Now try this thought experiment. Take your 9-gram ice cube, and take a 9-gram glass marble. They weigh *exactly the same*. Drop both in water:
- The marble **sinks**, even though it weighs the same as the ice.
- The ice **floats**.

So weight can't be the answer! The difference is density. Water has a density of about 1 gram per cubic centimeter. The marble squeezes its 9 grams into a very small space, making it denser than water, so it sinks. The ice spreads its 9 grams out over a slightly *bigger* space, making it less dense than water, so it floats.

Why is ice less dense than water? Cool fact: when water freezes, its molecules lock into a spread-out, cage-like pattern that takes up *more room* than the same water did as a liquid. That's why ice cubes are bumpy on top and why a full bottle of water can crack if it freezes — ice expands. Same 9 grams, more space taken up, lower density.

There's a second piece too: when you put something in water, the water **pushes up** on it. The more water the object shoves out of the way, the harder the water pushes back. An object floats when the water's upward push can match the object's weight — and that's possible whenever the object is less dense than water. Your ice cube only needs to dip partway into the glass before the pushed-aside water pushes up hard enough to hold all 9 grams. Then it bobs happily on top.

So the rule to remember: **compare densities, not weights.** Less dense than water → floats. More dense → sinks.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** A 100-gram wooden block floats in a bucket of water. A 5-gram steel coin sinks in the same bucket. Wait — the coin weighs 20 times *less* than the block. Shouldn't the coin float and the block sink? Explain what's really going on.

**Solution:** Weight doesn't decide floating — density does. The steel coin packs its 5 grams into a tiny volume, so it's denser than water (steel is about 7–8 g/cm³), and it sinks. The wooden block spreads its 100 grams over a much larger volume, so its density is less than water's (most wood is around 0.5–0.7 g/cm³). Even though the block is much heavier overall, the water it pushes aside when floating can weigh as much as the block does, so the upward push balances its weight and it floats. Heavier object floats, lighter object sinks — because floating is about density compared to water, not about grams.
