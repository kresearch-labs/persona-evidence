# Case 15 — Answer

## 1. The misconception
The student believes that whether something floats is decided by its **total weight (mass)**: "light things float, heavy things sink." In reality, floating depends on **density** — how much mass is packed into a given volume — compared to the density of the liquid, not on how heavy the object is. Weight alone tells you nothing: a 1-gram pebble sinks, while a 50-kilogram log floats.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student
Whether an object floats in water is a comparison of **densities**, not weights. Density = mass ÷ volume. Water has a density of about 1 g per cm³. If an object is *less dense* than water, it floats; if it is *more dense*, it sinks — no matter how big or heavy it is.

Ice is a special, surprising case: when water freezes, its molecules arrange into a pattern that takes up **more space**, so the same 9 grams of water spreads out into a bigger volume as ice. That makes ice (about 0.92 g/cm³) slightly *less dense* than liquid water, so it floats.

Another way to see it: water pushes up on anything placed in it with a force equal to the weight of the water the object pushes aside (displaces). Because ice is less dense, it only needs to sink partway in to displace its own weight in water — so it floats with a little sticking out above the surface.

**Test yourself:** A steel cargo ship weighs thousands of tons, yet floats; a tiny steel nail sinks. Why? Because the ship's hull encloses a lot of air, making its *average* density less than water, while the nail is solid steel (denser than water). Always compare densities, never just weights.
