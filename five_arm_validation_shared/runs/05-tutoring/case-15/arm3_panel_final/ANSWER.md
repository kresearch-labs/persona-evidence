# Case 15 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

The student believes floating is decided by an object's **absolute weight** ("light things float, heavy things sink"). They are treating "9 grams" as the deciding number, as if there were a weight limit for floating. In reality, absolute weight alone cannot decide floating — a 100,000-ton ship floats while a 5-gram coin sinks — so the real criterion must be something other than how heavy the object is.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept (density and buoyancy)

**The key idea: floating depends on *density*, not weight.** Density is how tightly packed the stuff inside an object is — mass divided by volume (how heavy a given *size* of it is). Water has a density of 1 g per cm³. Anything *less dense* than water floats; anything *more dense* sinks — no matter how big or small it is.

Why does less-dense stuff float? When you push an object into water, the water pushes back up (buoyancy) with a force equal to the weight of the water the object pushes out of the way. If the object is less dense than water, then the water it displaces weighs *more* than the object itself, so the upward push wins and the object floats.

**Now apply it to your ice cube.** You said the ice floats because it "only weighs 9 g." But here's the test: what does 9 g of *water* take up in space? About 9 cm³. What does 9 g of *ice* take up? About 9.8 cm³ — the same mass spread over a slightly bigger volume, because when water freezes it expands. Same weight, bigger volume → lower density (0.92 g/cm³ vs 1.00 g/cm³). Now imagine pushing the ice cube fully under: it would shove aside 9.8 cm³ of water, weighing about 9.8 g — more than the ice's own 9 g — so the water's upward push beats the ice's weight and it bobs back up. It settles floating with just 9 cm³ (9 g worth) of itself under the surface, exactly balancing its weight. (That's also why ice shrinks a little when it melts: the "extra" volume collapses.)

**Check the rule against things you know:**
- A massive steel ship floats: its hull holds lots of air, so its *average* density is below water's.
- A tiny coin sinks: solid metal is denser than water, even though it's light.
- So "light floats, heavy sinks" fails both ways — the deciding question is never "how heavy is it?" but "is it less dense than the liquid?"

**Try a similar problem:** A 9 g piece of solid glass (density 2.5 g/cm³) is placed in water. Does it float? Answer: no — squeeze its 9 g into only ~3.6 cm³, and it can't displace 9 g worth of water before it's fully submerged, so it sinks. Weight identical to the ice cube, opposite result — because density, not weight, decides.
