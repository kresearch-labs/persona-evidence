**1. The misconception**

The student is making a **single-cause error** (also called "monocausal thinking"): treating one dramatic event as *the* cause of a huge, complex outcome. Specifically, they are confusing the **trigger** (the immediate spark) with the **causes** (the underlying conditions that made war likely). The "representative wrong reasoning" shows the same error as a faulty counterfactual: "remove the one event, remove the war" — as if history were a row of dominoes where one shot set everything off.

**2. Explaining the correct concept**

Historians usually explain big events like World War I with **multiple causes at different levels**:

- **Long-term (underlying) causes** — conditions building up for years: rival alliance systems (Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente), an arms race and war plans (like the Schlieffen Plan) that made mobilization almost automatic, imperial rivalry, and aggressive nationalism, especially in the Balkans.
- **Short-term causes** — the crises of the early 1900s (Morocco, the Balkan Wars) that kept raising the temperature.
- **The trigger (immediate cause)** — the assassination of Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo. This was the *spark*, not the fuel.

A good image: dry forest + hot summer + lightning. The lightning starts the fire, but the fire happens because the forest was dry. Remove the lightning and a fire is still likely — maybe from a campfire or a power line. Likewise, Europe in 1914 was "dry": the alliances and war plans meant that any major crisis could pull everyone in. In fact, Europe had already survived earlier crises (like Agadir in 1911) that nearly caused war, and leaders at the time expected a war eventually.

So the correct evaluation of the claim is: the assassination was the **immediate trigger**, but the war had deeper **underlying causes**. Without Sarajevo, war was not guaranteed — but it was still likely, because the tensions, alliances, and military plans were all in place waiting for *some* crisis.

**How to solve a similar problem:** when asked to evaluate a claim about cause, (1) sort the causes into long-term, short-term, and trigger; (2) ask whether the claim's one event could really have produced the outcome *by itself*, or whether it needed the background conditions; (3) test the counterfactual carefully — ask "if this event were removed, what else could have produced the same outcome?" If the background conditions remain, the single event was a trigger, not the cause.
