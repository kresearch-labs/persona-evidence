# Case 17 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

**Single-cause thinking (monocausal reasoning): the student mistakes the *trigger* for the *cause*.**

Tracing the student's own reasoning steps, not just the verdict:

- "Wars start from one event" — the student is applying a general rule that big outcomes have one big cause. That rule, not the assassination, is the real error.
- "The shooting is what made all the countries declare war" — this conflates the *immediate trigger* (the event that set things off) with the *underlying causes* (the conditions that made a general war possible at all). The declarations of war were only possible because of the alliance system, the arms race, and existing rivalries.
- "Removing that one event removes the war" — a counterfactual that assumes nothing else could have ignited the same situation. It ignores that Europe had already come close to war in earlier crises (Morocco 1905 and 1911, the Balkan Wars 1912–13) without a Sarajevo-style assassination.

So the claim "the assassination caused World War I" is being accepted because the student has no category for *multiple, layered causes* — only for a single visible event.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

You're actually half right, and it's worth seeing exactly which half. You said the shot in Sarajevo *started* the war — true. But **starting** a war and **causing** a war are different things.

Think of a dry forest in summer. A match is struck and the whole forest burns. Did the match cause the fire? Only in the sense that it was the *spark*. The real cause of the fire is the months of dryness — and if that match hadn't been struck, the next match, or a lightning bolt, would have done the same thing. The dryness is what made the forest ready to burn.

World War I worked the same way. By 1914 Europe was the dry forest:

- **Alliances** had split the great powers into two armed camps, so a fight between two countries automatically pulled in the others — a chain reaction was built into the system.
- **Militarism**: the powers had spent years in an arms race, with detailed war plans (like Germany's Schlieffen Plan) that made quick mobilization almost irresistible once one country moved.
- **Imperialism and nationalism**: rivalries over colonies and, above all, tension in the Balkans — where the assassination itself happened — had already produced several near-wars.

The assassination was the **trigger**: the event that finally set the dry forest alight. Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and issued a harsh ultimatum; Russia mobilized to defend Serbia; Germany backed Austria-Hungary and then declared war on Russia and France; and Britain, committed to Belgian neutrality, joined after Germany invaded Belgium. One shot became a world war *because* the underlying conditions were already there.

Now test the classmate's claim the way historians do, with a proper counterfactual: *if Princip had missed, would the dryness have gone away?* No — the alliances, the arms race, and the Balkan tensions would all still have existed, and some other crisis (there had been several in the decade before) could have been the spark. So the assassination explains the war's *timing* — why it broke out in summer 1914 — but not its *cause*.

**A checklist you can reuse on any "X caused Y" claim in history:**

1. What was the immediate **trigger** — the single visible event?
2. What **underlying causes** made the outcome possible? (List more than one.)
3. Ask the counterfactual properly: *if the trigger hadn't happened, would the underlying causes still have made the outcome likely?*
4. Then evaluate: a claim that names only the trigger is too simple; a good evaluation separates "spark" from "dry forest" and weighs which mattered more.

Quick practice: "The sinking of the Lusitania caused the USA to enter World War I." Run the checklist — the sinking was a trigger that inflamed American opinion, but US entry also needed underlying causes (American trade and loans with the Allies, German submarine warfare generally, and eventually the Zimmermann Telegram). Same structure, same fix.
