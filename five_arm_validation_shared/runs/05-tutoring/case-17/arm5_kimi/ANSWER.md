# Case 17 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is confusing a **trigger (proximate cause)** with **the cause of the war**, and treating a complex event as if it had a single cause. The underlying error is monocausal thinking: "wars start from one event, so removing that one event removes the war." The assassination of Franz Ferdinand was the spark that set off the July Crisis, not the reason Europe was ready to explode. The counterfactual "if Princip had missed, there would have been no war" is therefore unsupported — it assumes the powder keg disappears when you remove one match.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Historians separate **long-term underlying causes** from **short-term triggers**.

By 1914 Europe already had deep tensions that made a general war likely, often summarized as **M.A.I.N.**:
- **Militarism** — an arms race (especially the Anglo-German naval race) and general staffs with detailed war plans (like Germany's Schlieffen Plan) that pushed leaders to mobilize fast.
- **Alliances** — the Triple Alliance and Triple Entente turned a regional dispute into a continent-wide one: a conflict between Austria-Hungary and Serbia automatically pulled in Russia, Germany, and France.
- **Imperialism** — great-power rivalry over colonies and influence created constant friction.
- **Nationalism** — both aggressive great-power nationalism and Slavic nationalism in the Balkans, which is exactly why Austria-Hungary and Serbia were on a collision course.

The assassination in Sarajevo was the **trigger**: it gave Austria-Hungary a pretext to crush Serbia, and the alliance system did the rest. But the conditions for war existed independently of Princip. If he had missed, war might have started later, elsewhere, or over a different crisis (there had already been near-misses — the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911, the Balkan Wars of 1912–13) — or conceivably been avoided, but you cannot conclude peace would simply have continued.

**How to use this on a similar problem:** whenever you evaluate "X caused Y" for a big historical event, ask two questions: (1) What long-term conditions made Y likely? (2) Was X the trigger, the underlying cause, or both? A trigger explains the *timing*, not the *existence*, of the event. So the correct verdict on the classmate's claim is: the assassination caused the war to break out *when and how it did*, but it did not single-handedly cause World War I.
