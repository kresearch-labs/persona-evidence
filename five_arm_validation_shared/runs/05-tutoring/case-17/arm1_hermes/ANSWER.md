# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-17 (causes of World War I: trigger vs. underlying causes)

## 1. The misconception

The student is committing the **single-cause fallacy** (also called "monocausal thinking"): treating one dramatic event as *the* cause of a complex historical outcome. Specifically, they are **confusing the trigger with the underlying causes**. The assassination of Franz Ferdinand was the *spark* — the immediate event that set things off — but the "fuel" had been accumulating for decades: rival alliance systems, an arms race, imperial competition, and aggressive nationalism. Because the student sees only the spark, they also make a faulty counterfactual argument: they assume that removing the single event (Princip missing his shot) would automatically remove the entire outcome (the war). That logic only works if the event really was the *only* cause.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Historians sort causes into two categories:

- **Long-term (underlying) causes** — the conditions that made a big war *likely*: 
 - **Militarism**: Germany and Britain raced to build dreadnoughts; armies grew huge. 
 - **Alliances**: Europe was split into two armed camps (Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente), so any fight between two countries risked dragging everyone in. 
 - **Imperialism**: rivalry over colonies created repeated friction. 
 - **Nationalism**: especially in the Balkans, which people called the "powder keg of Europe."
- **Short-term trigger** — the event that actually lit the fuse: the assassinations in Sarajevo, June 1914.

Here's a concrete analogy. Imagine a forest after months of drought: trees are bone-dry, temperatures are extreme, and it hasn't rained in weeks. Then a camper's campfire throws off one ember and the whole forest burns. Did the ember "cause" the fire? Partly — but so did the drought. If that particular ember had never flown, the *next* spark almost certainly would have set it off, because the forest was ready to burn.

Europe in 1914 was that dry forest. In fact, the Great Powers had already come close to general war several times *before* the assassination — the Moroccan Crises (1905, 1911) and the Balkan Wars (1912–13) — and only careful diplomacy had held things together. So when evaluating the classmate's claim, the accurate answer is: the assassination was the **trigger**, but the alliance system, arms race, imperialism, and nationalism were the **conditions** that turned a regional murder into a world war. Without Sarajevo, some other crisis — and there were plenty brewing — could easily have provided the spark.

**Rule of thumb for problems like this:** when asked to evaluate a "X caused Y" claim, ask (1) Was X a trigger or a deeper cause? (2) Were there conditions that made Y likely even without X? A strong evaluation credits the trigger *and* weighs the underlying causes.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** A classmate claims: "The attack on Fort Sumter caused the American Civil War. If the Confederates hadn't fired on Fort Sumter in April 1861, there would have been no war." Evaluate this claim.

**Solution:** This repeats the single-cause fallacy. Fort Sumter was the *trigger*, not the root cause. By the time shots were fired, seven Southern states had *already* seceded and formed the Confederacy — the Union and Confederacy already existed as rivals. The underlying causes ran deep: the fight over the expansion of slavery into new territories, the collapse of compromises (Kansas-Nebraska Act, Dred Scott, "Bleeding Kansas"), the economic and cultural divide between North and South, and the bitter election of 1860. Tensions were so high that some clash between the two governments was very likely regardless. So the claim is partly right — Sumter started the actual fighting — but wrong to suggest that removing that one event would have removed the war. Like Sarajevo in 1914, Fort Sumter was the spark in a room already full of dry tinder.
