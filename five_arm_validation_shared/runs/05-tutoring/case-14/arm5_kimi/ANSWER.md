# Case 14 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student believes voltage and current are the same quantity measured in different units — that "volts" and "amps" are just two ways of saying "how much electricity is flowing." This leads them to swap the two roles: they say the outlet "pushes 230 volts of current" and that the lamp "uses 2 amps of voltage." In reality, voltage and current are two distinct physical quantities, and neither one is "used up" by the lamp.

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Voltage and current are not the same thing — they describe two different aspects of the circuit, and the units are not interchangeable.

- **Voltage (230 V)** is the *push*, or more precisely the energy the source gives to each unit of electric charge. One volt means one joule of energy per coulomb of charge. So "230 V" tells you the outlet supplies 230 joules of energy to every coulomb of charge it sends around the circuit. Voltage is a property of the *source* — it is there whether or not anything is plugged in.

- **Current (2 A)** is the *flow rate* of charge — how much charge passes a point in the circuit each second. One amp means one coulomb per second. So "2 A" tells you 2 coulombs of charge flow through the lamp every second. Current depends on both the voltage and what is connected; the lamp, not the outlet, determines that 2 A flows.

A useful picture: think of water in pipes. Voltage is like the water pressure (how hard the pump pushes), and current is like the flow rate (how much water actually moves per second). Pressure and flow are related, but they are not the same thing, and you measure them in different units.

Two more points that fix the student's exact wording:

- The outlet does not push "volts of current" — it provides a voltage (a push), and that push *causes* a current to flow through the lamp.
- The lamp does not "use up" the 2 amps — the same current flows into the lamp and back out. What the lamp uses up is the *energy* the charges carry (it converts electrical energy to light and heat). The charges themselves keep flowing.

**Check on a similar problem:** If a different outlet supplied 120 V and a toaster drew 5 A, you should be able to say: 120 V is the energy per coulomb the outlet provides (the push), and 5 A is the rate at which charge flows through the toaster (coulombs per second) — two different quantities, not two names for one.
