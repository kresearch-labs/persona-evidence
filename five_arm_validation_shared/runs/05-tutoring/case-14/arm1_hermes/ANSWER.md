# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-14 (voltage vs. current)

## 1. The misconception

The student believes **voltage and current are the same quantity measured in different units** — that "volts" and "amps" are interchangeable labels for "how much electricity flows." This is why they swap the terms in their answer ("the outlet pushes 230 volts of *current*," "2 amps is how much *voltage*").

In reality, voltage and current describe **two different things**:
- **Voltage (V)** is the *push* — how much energy each bit of electric charge gets.
- **Current (A)** is the *flow* — how much charge actually moves past a point each second.

They're related, but they are not the same measurement, any more than the *pressure* in a water pipe is the same thing as the *amount of water* flowing through it.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Think of electricity like water in a hose:

- **Voltage is like water pressure.** It's the push that shoves the water through the hose. High pressure = a strong push.
- **Current is like the amount of water flowing** past you every second — a trickle or a torrent.

A garden sprayer connected to your house might have huge pressure but only a thin stream coming out. A wide-open fire hose might have lower pressure but move far more water. Pressure and flow rate are clearly *different* things — one doesn't automatically mean the other.

Electricity works the same way:

- **230 V** tells you how hard the outlet *pushes* the charges — how much energy each unit of charge is given.
- **2 A** tells you *how much charge actually flows* through the lamp each second.

So the outlet provides the push (230 V), and because the lamp lets current through, that push results in a flow of 2 A. If you plugged in a different device, the voltage would still be 230 V (the outlet always pushes the same), but the current could be different — a big heater might draw 10 A, while a tiny LED nightlight might draw only 0.02 A. Same push, different flow.

That's also why they can't be "two ways of saying the same thing": changing the appliance changes the amps but not the volts.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** A car battery supplies 12 V, and a headlight connected to it draws 5 A. What does each number tell you about the circuit? Then predict: if you connect a smaller dome light instead, which number stays at 12 and which one changes?

**Solution:**
- The **12 V** is the *push*: each unit of charge from the battery is given 12 units of energy. That's fixed by the battery.
- The **5 A** is the *flow*: 5 units of charge worth of electrons pass through the headlight every second.
- With the smaller dome light, the **voltage stays 12 V** (the battery's push doesn't change), but the **current changes** — the dome light lets less charge flow, so it draws fewer amps. Voltage comes from the source; current depends on what's connected.
