## 1. The misconception

The student believes voltage and current are the **same quantity in different units** — that "volts" and "amps" are just two names for "how much electricity flows." This is the classic volts–amps conflation, and it usually rests on a deeper idea: that electricity is one single "stuff," so every electrical term (voltage, current, power) must be measuring that one thing. The answer shows it in three places:

- "The outlet pushes 230 volts of current" — treats volts as a kind of current.
- "2 amps is how much voltage the lamp uses" — treats amps as a kind of voltage.
- "Two ways of saying the same thing" — the explicit claim that the units are interchangeable.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

Voltage and current are two **different** things, and neither one is a unit of the other.

Think of water in a hose:

- **Voltage (volts, V)** is like the water **pressure** — how hard the pump pushes the water.
- **Current (amps, A)** is like the **flow rate** — how much water passes a point each second.

A wall outlet is a pump for electric charge. **"230 V" tells you how hard the outlet pushes each bit of charge** — the push it has available. **"2 A" tells you how much charge actually flows through the lamp every second** — the flow that results from that push.

Why they can't be "the same thing in different units": pressure and flow can change independently. A car battery is only 12 V but can push a large current through a headlight; a static shock is thousands of volts but almost no current flows. If volts and amps measured the same quantity, both of those would be impossible.

What connects the two numbers is the **lamp itself**. The lamp's filament resists the flow, and that resistance decides how much current a given push produces:

**V = I × R**  (push = flow × resistance)

So in this circuit: the outlet supplies the push (230 V), the lamp's resistance lets a certain flow through in response (2 A). Neither number "uses" or "pushes" the other — the outlet doesn't push amps, and the lamp doesn't use volts.

A quick check you can use on any similar problem: **volts describe the push supplied by the source; amps describe the flow through the device; the device's resistance links them.** As a bonus, push × flow gives the power in watts: 230 V × 2 A = 460 W.

Try this one to check yourself: "A 1.5 V battery is connected to a small bulb and 0.5 A flows. Which number is the push and which is the flow — and what would you need to know to predict the current?" (Push = 1.5 V, supplied by the battery; flow = 0.5 A, through the bulb; to predict the current you'd need the bulb's resistance, R = V/I = 3 Ω.)
