# Case 20 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is showing the **"single great cause" (hero-inventor) misconception**: the belief that one invention — Watt's steam engine — single-handedly caused the Industrial Revolution, and that everything else (factories, city growth) followed automatically from it. This is monocausal, "great man" thinking: big historical changes get credited to one person or one gadget instead of to a set of conditions that built up over time.

It also contains factual errors folded into the misconception: James Watt did not *invent* the steam engine (Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine was pumping water out of mines by 1712, decades earlier; Watt's key contribution from the 1760s–70s was a much more efficient *improvement*). And people were not "forced" to cities by an engine — urban growth came from economic pushes and pulls, not from a machine.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

Think of it like a fire. A match can start a fire, but only if there's dry wood, oxygen, and no rain. The match gets the credit, but the *conditions* did most of the work. The steam engine was Britain's match — here is the dry wood that was already piled up:

- **Food and workers:** The Agricultural Revolution (new crops, crop rotation, enclosure of common land) meant fewer farmers could feed more people — and many rural workers who lost access to land needed new work. That is the *push* toward cities.
- **Money to invest:** Profits from overseas trade and empire, plus banks and a stable government, gave Britain capital to build factories and machines.
- **Raw materials and energy:** Britain had abundant coal and iron, often close together — exactly what steam power and iron machines need.
- **Markets:** A growing population, overseas colonies, and trade networks meant there were buyers for mass-produced goods, especially cloth.
- **Ideas and incentives:** A culture of practical science, patent protection, and skilled craftsmen (not just Watt — Hargreaves, Arkwright, Crompton, Newcomen, Stephenson and many others) kept improving machines.

Now the chain makes sense: factories using machines (first water-powered, later steam) were built where coal, workers, and transport were. Factory jobs were the *pull* that drew people to cities like Manchester and Leeds, while the agricultural changes *pushed* them off the land. Cities grew from that push-and-pull migration plus natural population growth — not because a steam engine "forced" anyone to move.

**The rule to carry to similar problems:** when you see a huge historical change, resist naming one cause. Ask instead: *What conditions had to be in place for this to happen here and now, and what event or invention acted as the trigger?* Distinguish the trigger (Watt's improved engine) from the underlying conditions (agriculture, capital, coal, labor, markets), and check your facts about who actually did what.
