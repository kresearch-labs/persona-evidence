# Case 20 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is showing **single-cause thinking** (sometimes called "the one great cause" or "great man" fallacy), mixed with **technological determinism** — the belief that one invention automatically causes everything else. Three clues in the answer:

- "James Watt invented the steam engine" → treats one person and one machine as the whole story (and it's factually shaky: Watt *improved* an earlier engine invented by Thomas Newcomen).
- "the revolution happened automatically" → history as a chain reaction with no human choices, conditions, or context.
- "the steam engine forced everyone to move" → cities grew because of one push, when in fact people moved mostly because they *chose* to — factory wages pulled them, and hard times in the countryside pushed them.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

Big historical changes don't have one cause — they happen when **many causes line up at the same time and place**. Think of it like a river: not one faucet, but many streams feeding into one channel. Britain in the late 1700s had an unusual number of streams flowing together:

- **Resources:** lots of coal and iron ore, close to each other and to ports.
- **Money:** profits from trade and empire gave people capital to invest in factories.
- **Labor:** an agricultural revolution produced more food with fewer farmworkers, so there were plenty of hands free for factory work.
- **Markets:** colonies and a big merchant navy meant someone to sell goods to — so there was a reason to mass-produce.
- **Politics and stability:** a government that protected property and patents, so inventors and investors could keep their gains.
- **Technology:** yes, steam engines — but notice the direction of the arrow. Mine owners needed a better way to pump water out of coal mines, and Watt's improvement answered that need. Invention usually *responds* to demand; it doesn't appear from nowhere and then cause everything.

And cities grew for the same "many causes" reason: factories were built near coal, rivers, and ports; those factories offered steady wages; workers moved toward the jobs (a *pull*), while enclosure of farmland and rural poverty pushed others off the land (a *push*). No one was "forced by the steam engine" — people moved because of economic choices and pressures.

**How to use this on a similar problem:** when a question asks "why did X happen," don't hunt for the single cause. Instead, check several categories — resources, money, labor, markets, politics, technology, ideas — and explain how two or three of them *connected* (cause A made cause B possible). And be suspicious of words like "automatically," "forced," or "because of one man" — history is conditions plus choices, not a domino chain.
