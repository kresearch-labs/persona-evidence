# Case 20 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

Two linked misconceptions:

- **Single-cause thinking ("one invention caused it all").** The student treats one invention as *the* cause of a huge historical change, ignoring the many conditions that had to already be in place. It also contains a factual error: James Watt did not invent the steam engine — he greatly improved an earlier engine (Thomas Newcomen's) with his separate condenser in 1769. And causation often runs the other way: inventors improved steam engines *because* there was already demand (mine owners needed to pump water out of deep coal pits).
- **Technological determinism ("the machine forced people").** The student assumes technology automatically makes society change. Steam engines did not force anyone to move; people responded to economic incentives — jobs and wages pulled them to cities, while loss of rural land and livelihoods pushed them out of the countryside.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

The correct concept is **multiple causation**: big historical changes happen when several factors line up — geographic, economic, political, social, and technological — and historians sort them into background conditions, triggers, and consequences.

Think of it like a fire: a match may light it, but you also need dry wood, oxygen, and heat. Britain around 1750 was full of "dry wood":

- **Natural resources:** abundant coal and iron ore, often located near each other.
- **Agricultural revolution:** better farming fed a growing population and freed workers from the land.
- **Capital and trade:** profits from commerce and empire provided investment money; colonies supplied raw cotton and bought manufactured goods.
- **Political stability:** secure property rights, patents protecting inventors, no internal customs barriers, a government friendly to business.
- **Transport:** canals, rivers, and harbors moved coal and goods cheaply (railways came later).
- **People and skills:** a rising population and skilled craftsmen supplied labor and know-how.

The steam engine was one important factor among these: it freed factories from the limit of water power, so mills could be built in towns near workers and coal. But factories existed before steam (early mills used water wheels), and Watt's engine improved machines already used to pump mines — so it accelerated the revolution rather than starting it.

Cities grew for **push and pull** reasons, not because engines "forced" anyone:

- **Pull:** steady cash wages in factories, where whole families could earn.
- **Push:** enclosure and farm consolidation left rural laborers with less land and work; population growth meant fewer farm jobs to go around.
- **Momentum:** once people clustered, cheap housing, urban jobs, and migration (for example from Ireland) kept the cities growing.

**How to solve a similar problem:** when a question asks "why did X happen," never answer with one person or one invention. List at least three factors from different categories (e.g., resources, money/trade, politics, society, technology), explain how they connected, and identify which was the trigger and which were background conditions. Warning signs you're slipping back into the misconception: your answer names only one cause, or uses words like "automatically" or "forced."
