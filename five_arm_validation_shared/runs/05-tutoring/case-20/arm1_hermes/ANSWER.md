# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-20 (Industrial Revolution — single-cause fallacy)

## 1. The misconception

The student has fallen into the **single-cause fallacy**, sometimes called **technological determinism** — the belief that one dramatic invention (here, the steam engine) caused an enormous historical change all by itself, and that once the invention existed, everything else followed "automatically."

There are three related errors bundled inside this answer:

1. **One cause instead of many.** Big historical changes almost always have several overlapping causes — resources, money, labor, government policy, and demand. The student treats the steam engine as both necessary and sufficient.
2. **Inventions appear out of nowhere.** The student imagines Watt inventing the steam engine in a vacuum, when in fact the engine was a *response* to conditions that already existed in Britain (it was built to solve a coal-mining problem, and Watt *improved* an earlier engine designed by Thomas Newcomen).
3. **People move because machines make them.** The student thinks the steam engine "forced" people into cities. In reality, people migrated voluntarily because of economic push and pull factors — the engine shaped *where* factories could go, but it didn't drag anyone anywhere.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

Think of the Industrial Revolution like baking a cake. Flour alone doesn't make a cake — you need flour, eggs, sugar, and heat all at the same time. Britain in the late 1700s happened to have **all the ingredients together for the first time**:

- **Energy and materials:** Lots of coal and iron ore, often close together.
- **Food and workers:** An *agricultural revolution* (new farming methods, crop rotation, selective breeding) meant fewer farmers could feed everyone. That produced a **surplus of food AND a surplus of workers** who no longer were needed on farms.
- **Money:** Merchants, banks, and profits from global trade (including colonial trade) provided capital to invest in factories.
- **Stable government:** Property rights and patent laws made people willing to invest in new machinery.
- **Transportation:** Canals, harbors, and later railways moved coal and goods cheaply.
- **Skilled hands:** Britain had generations of clockmakers, millwrights, and metalworkers who could actually build and repair complex machines.

Now here's the key insight about the steam engine: it wasn't the *first domino* — it was a *response* to these conditions. Thomas Newcomen built an early steam engine in 1712 mainly to **pump water out of coal mines**. Watt didn't invent it; he patented a much more efficient version in 1769. The engine only mattered because Britain already had coal mines, investors to fund Watt, and workshops to build his parts. Other countries later bought British steam engines too — but they didn't all industrialize at the same speed. If the machine alone caused the revolution, they would have.

As for the cities: people moved for **jobs and wages**, not because engines forced them. Two forces worked together:

- **Push:** Enclosure and modern farming meant fewer rural jobs.
- **Pull:** Factories offered steady cash wages.

Steam power did matter for *where* cities grew — before steam, factories had to sit beside rivers for water power; steam let owners build anywhere near coal, canals, and workers. That's why places like Manchester exploded in size. But the people walking there were chasing paychecks, not being pushed by pistons.

So a strong answer to "why Britain?" names **several causes and explains how they connected** — one invention is never the whole story.

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

**Problem:** A classmate writes: *"Britain's cotton textile industry boomed in the late 1700s because John Kay invented the flying shuttle."* What's wrong with this explanation, and what would a complete answer include?

**Solution:** The mistake is the same single-cause fallacy — one invention credited with the whole boom. A complete answer would show a *chain of connected causes*: Kay's flying shuttle (1733) let weavers work faster, which created huge demand for thread; that demand *prompted* new inventions like the spinning jenny (1764) and water frame (1769) — inventions responding to need, not appearing randomly. But the boom also required cheap raw cotton from plantations in the Americas, port cities and merchant money to import it, water and later steam power to run mills, and a large workforce plus growing markets to sell cloth to. Remove any ingredient — no cotton, no capital, no workers — and the boom stalls. The flying shuttle was one link in a chain, not the whole engine of change.
