# Case 18 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student assumes revolutions are caused by the sheer *amount* of taxation — that the colonists rebelled because taxes were financially crushing. The given data actually contradicts this: colonists paid only about 1 shilling per year, one of the lightest tax burdens in the world, while Britons paid 26 times more and did not rebel. The misconception is confusing the *level* of a tax with the *principle* behind it — mistaking a symbol/trigger (taxes) for the actual grievance (who had the right to impose them).

## 2. The correct concept, explained to the student

Look at the numbers again: if high taxes caused revolutions, people in Britain — paying 26 shillings a year — should have revolted long before the colonists paying 1 shilling. So the dollar amount can't be the reason. The colonists' real complaint was summed up in their slogan "No taxation without representation." They believed that only a legislature they had elected could legitimately tax them. Britain's Parliament had no colonial representatives in it, yet it passed laws like the Stamp Act and the Tea Act taxing the colonies anyway. To the colonists, this meant the government was taking their property without their consent — and if Parliament could do that, it could do anything to them. The issue was power and rights, not money: a small tax imposed without consent was seen as more dangerous than a large tax imposed by your own elected assembly. So when you see a question like this, always ask not just "how much did they pay?" but "who decided they should pay, and did they have a say?" The colonists rebelled over the principle of self-government and consent, with taxes serving as the flashpoint — not over an unaffordable tax burden.
