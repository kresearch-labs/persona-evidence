# Case 18 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

The student holds a **"revolutions happen when taxes become financially unbearable"** misconception — they reduce the American Revolution to a cost-of-living problem. Two specific errors are visible in their work:

- **They ignored the evidence given in the question.** The question hands them the numbers (colonists ≈ 1 shilling/year, Britons ≈ 26 shillings/year), and those numbers *disprove* "extremely high taxes." The student never uses the data; they answer as if the question were just "why did the colonists rebel?"
- **They confuse the *amount* of a tax with the *authority* behind it.** Their reasoning ("taxed far more than they could pay") assumes the complaint is quantitative. The actual colonist complaint was qualitative: *who has the right to tax us at all?* A small tax imposed by a body you have no voice in felt illegitimate; a much larger tax passed by your own representatives felt legitimate.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

"Look at the numbers in the question again — they're the clue. Colonists paid about **1 shilling a year**; people in Britain paid about **26 shillings a year**. So colonists were paying roughly **26 times less** than the people at home. If the Revolution were really about taxes being too expensive, the British — not the colonists — should have been the ones rebelling. The numbers tell you the cause can't be the *size* of the tax. So what was it?

It was about **who had the right to impose the tax** — the principle of **'no taxation without representation.'** Before the 1760s, Britain mostly left the colonies to run themselves, and colonists taxed *themselves* through their own elected colonial assemblies. Then Parliament began passing direct taxes on the colonies — the Sugar Act (1764), the Stamp Act (1765), the Townshend Acts (1767) — and colonists had **no elected representatives in Parliament**. Their argument was: as English subjects, we can only be taxed by people we elect. Parliament's answer was that it represented them anyway ('virtual representation'), which the colonists rejected. It was also about **precedent**: even a tiny tax mattered, because accepting Parliament's *right* to tax them once would let Parliament tax them however it liked forever.

So the rebellion wasn't 'the taxes cost too much' — it was 'these taxes are *illegal by our rights*, no matter how small.'

**How to handle a similar problem:** when a question gives you data, your explanation must fit that data. Ask two questions: (1) *How much?* — if the numbers contradict 'too expensive,' drop the money-burden explanation; (2) *Who decides?* — if the grievance isn't about the amount, look for a cause about **rights, consent, and representation** rather than economics. An answer that ignores the evidence in the prompt is wrong even if it sounds plausible."
