1. The misconception

The student has confused the *size* of a tax with the *right* to impose it. Reading the numbers as "taxes were extremely high" gets them backwards: the figures in the question actually show colonists paid about 26 times *less* than people in Britain (1 shilling vs. 26). Behind the answer is the false rule "revolutions happen when taxes get too expensive" — the idea that rebellion must be a response to economic pain. The student treats the tax amount as the cause, when the real grievance was not how much the colonists paid but *who had the authority to tax them and whether they had consented*.

2. The correct concept

The colonists rebelled over **taxation without representation**, not over the size of the tax bill.

Here's how to think it through:

- **Look at what the numbers really say.** Colonists paid about 1 shilling a year; Britons paid about 26. If crushing taxes were the problem, Britons — not colonists — should have been the ones revolting. So the cause must be something other than the amount.
- **The issue was consent, not cost.** Colonists elected their own colonial assemblies, which had traditionally taxed them. After 1763, Parliament began taxing the colonies directly (Sugar Act, Stamp Act, Townshend duties, Tea Act) — and the colonists had **no elected representatives in Parliament**. To them, being taxed by a body they couldn't vote in violated their rights as British subjects, whatever the amount.
- **The precedent mattered more than the pennies.** Even a tiny tax was alarming, because it set a principle: if Parliament could tax colonists directly once, it could tax them anything, anytime. The colonists were defending self-government ("no taxation without representation"), not their wallets.

A check to use on similar problems: when a question gives you numbers, ask "compared to what?" — decide whether the claim is about an absolute amount or a relative burden. Then separate two different questions: "How much?" (an economic question) and "By what right?" (a political question). If the data contradicts "too much," the real cause usually lies in the second question — in rights, power, and consent.
