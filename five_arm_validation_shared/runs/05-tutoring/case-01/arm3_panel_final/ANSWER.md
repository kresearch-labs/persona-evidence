1. The misconception: the student has overgeneralized the unit-fraction rule. "Smaller denominator = bigger pieces" is true for unit fractions like 1/5 vs 1/7, but the student applies it to *all* fractions, comparing only the denominators and ignoring the numerator — as if fifths always beat sevenths no matter how many of each you have.

2. Explanation to the student:

You're half right — fifths ARE bigger pieces than sevenths. But a fraction is two things at once: how big the pieces are AND how many of them you have. Bigger pieces help, but fewer pieces can lose.

Here's a way to see it. Imagine two candy bars of the same size. One is cut into 5 equal pieces and you get 2 of them. The other is cut into 7 equal pieces and you get 3 of them. Two big pieces against three smaller ones — which pile is more candy? It's not obvious from piece size alone, so we need a fair way to compare.

The fair way: cut both bars into the SAME size pieces, then count. The size that works for both 5 and 7 is 35ths.

- 2/5: each fifth cuts into 7 thirty-fifths, so 2/5 = 14/35
- 3/7: each seventh cuts into 5 thirty-fifths, so 3/7 = 15/35

Now the pieces are the same size, so just compare the counts: 15 > 14. So 3/7 is greater than 2/5 — by one small thirty-fifth. The bigger pieces of the fifths almost won, but having only 2 of them wasn't quite enough.

Try one yourself with the same method: which is greater, 2/3 or 3/5? Cut both into fifteenths (2/3 = 10/15, 3/5 = 9/15) and compare the counts. Whenever the bottoms differ, don't ask "whose pieces are bigger?" — ask "how many pieces does each one actually have, once the pieces are the same size?"
