# Case 01 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is comparing fractions using **only the denominators** — they see that fifths are bigger pieces than sevenths and conclude that *any* number of fifths beats *any* number of sevenths. In other words, they have correctly learned "the smaller the bottom number, the bigger each piece," but they are ignoring the **numerator** — how many of those pieces you actually get. Comparing the size of one piece is not the same as comparing the whole amounts when the counts differ. (Their answer, 2/5, is also wrong: 3/7 is greater.)

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

"You're half right — fifths really are bigger pieces than sevenths. But a fraction tells you two things, not one: **how big the pieces are** AND **how many pieces you get**. You can't decide which amount is bigger by looking at only one of them.

Think of it like pizza. Would you rather have 3 slices cut into sevenths (a pizza cut into 7 slices) or 2 slices cut into fifths (a pizza cut into 5 slices)? Yes, the fifth-slices are bigger — but you only get 2 of them, while you get 3 of the smaller seventh-slices. Two big pieces can still be less than three slightly smaller pieces.

To compare fairly, cut both amounts into **the same size pieces**, then just count. Cut sevenths and fifths both into 35ths:

- 3/7 = 15/35 (each seventh becomes 5 thirty-fifths, so 3 × 5 = 15 pieces)
- 2/5 = 14/35 (each fifth becomes 7 thirty-fifths, so 2 × 7 = 14 pieces)

Same-size pieces, so now just count: 15 > 14, so **3/7 is greater than 2/5**.

Try one yourself: which is greater, 2/3 or 3/5? Cut both into fifteenths and count the pieces — you'll see 2/3 wins (10/15 vs 9/15). The rule: **same-size pieces first, then compare the counts.**"
