# Case 01 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student overgeneralizes a true rule about unit fractions. It is correct that 1/5 > 1/7 (fewer equal pieces means each piece is bigger). The error is extending that rule to "anything in fifths beats anything in sevenths" — treating the denominator alone as deciding the size of any fraction, and ignoring the numerator (how many pieces you have). The size of a fraction depends on both: how big the pieces are AND how many of them you have.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept

You're right that fifths are bigger pieces than sevenths — but that only settles it when you're comparing the same *number* of pieces, like 1/5 vs. 1/7. Here you have different numbers of pieces: 2 biggish pieces vs. 3 smaller pieces. More small pieces can add up to more than fewer big pieces — think of 3 slices of a small pizza vs. 2 slices of a slightly bigger one.

The reliable fix: stop comparing piece sizes and compare the actual amounts. Two easy ways:

- **Same denominator:** Rewrite both fractions with a common denominator. 3/7 = 15/35 and 2/5 = 14/35. Now the pieces are the same size (thirty-fifths), so just count them: 15 pieces beats 14 pieces, so 3/7 > 2/5.
- **Same numerator:** Make the tops match instead: 3/7 = 6/14 and 2/5 = 6/15. Now both have 6 pieces, and fourteenths are bigger than fifteenths, so 6/14 > 6/15 — again 3/7 > 2/5.

Quick check you can always do: compare each fraction to 1/2. 3/7 is less than 1/2 (since 3.5/7 would be half) and 2/5 is also less than 1/2 — that doesn't separate them here, so you need the common-denominator method. Rule of thumb: the denominator-only shortcut works only when the numerators are equal; otherwise, get a common denominator (or cross-multiply: 3×5 = 15 vs. 2×7 = 14, so 3/7 wins) and then compare.
