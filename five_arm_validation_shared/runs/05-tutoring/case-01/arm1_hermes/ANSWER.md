# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 05-tutoring case-01 (fractions: 3/7 vs 2/5)

## 1. The misconception

The student believes the **denominator alone determines which fraction is bigger** — "smaller denominator wins." They've correctly learned the half-truth that *unit* fractions work that way (1/5 > 1/7), and over-generalized it to *all* fractions. The missing idea: a fraction compares **the number of pieces to the size of the pieces**, so you can't judge a fraction by its denominator alone — 2/7 is two small pieces, and 1/5 is one medium piece, and either can win.

## 2. Explaining it to the student

"Your rule works for *one* piece: yes, 1/5 is bigger than 1/7, because cutting a candy bar into 5 pieces gives you bigger pieces than cutting it into 7. Good thinking — you've got half of it.

But a fraction isn't just the size of the pieces — it's **how many pieces you get**. Look at 2/7: you get TWO small pieces. Look at 1/5: you get ONE medium piece. Two small pieces can beat one medium piece!

Let's check with pictures. Draw two same-size candy bars.

- Bar 1: cut into 7 equal pieces. Color 2. 
- Bar 2: cut into 5 equal pieces. Color 1. 

Hold them side by side: the two colored sevenths together are *longer* than the one colored fifth. So **3/7 > 2/5**.

Here's a trick that always works, called **making the pieces the same size**: 
- 3/7 = 15/35 (multiply top and bottom by 5) 
- 2/5 = 14/35 (multiply top and bottom by 7) 

Now both bars are cut into 35ths — same-size pieces — so you just count: 15 pieces vs 14 pieces. 15 wins, so **3/7 is greater**."

## Quick check for the student (same idea, new problem)

*Which is greater, 3/4 or 5/8?*
Make the pieces the same size: 3/4 = 6/8, and 6/8 vs 5/8 → six eighths beats five. So 3/4 is greater — even though eighths are "smaller pieces," there are more of them.

**Rule to remember:** the denominator tells you the *size* of the pieces; the numerator tells you *how many*. To compare fractions, make the piece sizes match (common denominator), then count.
