# Case 04 — Answer

## 1. The misconception

The student is treating percentages as **fixed amounts that add and subtract**, instead of as **multipliers applied to whatever the current price is**. "+20% then −20% = 0% net change" is only true if both percentages are taken of the *same* base. Here they aren't: the 20% increase is taken of $80, but the 20% discount is taken of the *new, higher* price ($96). Since the second percentage is computed on a bigger number, the discount ($19.20) is bigger than the increase ($16), so the two changes do **not** cancel — the student loses money overall.

In short: **percent changes are multiplicative, not additive**, and each one acts on a different base.

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

Here's how I'd say it to the student:

> "A percent is never a thing by itself — it's always a percent **of something**. '20% up' means 20% *of the price right now*, and '20% off' means 20% *of the price right now at that moment*. Those two 'right nows' are different prices, so the two 20%s are different amounts of money.
>
> The easy way to never get this wrong is to turn every percent change into a **multiply**:
> - +20% means multiply by 1.20 (you keep the whole price plus 20% more)
> − 20% off means multiply by 0.80 (you keep 80% of the price)
>
> Then just multiply the steps together:
> - Start: $80
> - After +20%: 80 × 1.20 = **$96**
> - After 20% off: 96 × 0.80 = **$76.80**
>
> Notice the 20% off was taken of $96, not $80 — that's why it's a bigger discount ($19.20) than the raise ($16), and you end up *below* $80, not at it.
>
> Quick shortcut: multiply the two factors. 1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96, so the final price is 96% of the original — a net **4% decrease**, never 0%."

**Similar problem to check understanding:** "A bike costs $50. The price rises 10%, then a week later it's discounted 10%. What's the final price?" (Answer: 50 × 1.10 = $55, then 55 × 0.90 = **$49.50** — not $50.) If the student can explain *why* it's $49.50 and not $50, the misconception is fixed.
