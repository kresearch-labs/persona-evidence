# Case 07 — Answer

## 1. The misconception behind the answer

The student has swapped **factors** with **multiples** — the two directions of "goes into."

- **Factor:** a number that goes *into* another number evenly (12 goes into 24 and into 36).
- **Multiple:** a number you get by *multiplying* — a number that 24 and 36 go *into* (72 is one).

The student's own words give it away: "the biggest number both 24 and 36 **go into**." That is the definition of a **common multiple**, not a common factor. So they computed a common multiple (72) instead of the greatest common **factor** (12). A second, smaller slip piled on top: they chose the *greatest* common multiple, when even the multiple-based question (LCM) asks for the *least* one — and they trusted "72 is the greatest, so it must be the GCF," when a GCF can never be larger than the smaller of the two numbers (here 24).

## 2. Explaining the correct concept to the student

Here's how I'd explain it:

> You mixed up two ideas that sound almost the same — let's separate them.
>
> **"Goes into" means divides.** Ask: what number goes *into* 24 and *into* 36 with nothing left over?
> - 12 goes into 24 → 24 ÷ 12 = 2 ✓
> - 12 goes into 36 → 36 ÷ 12 = 3 ✓
>
> That's a **common factor**. The **GCF** is the *biggest* number that goes into both — for 24 and 36 that's **12**.
>
> A **multiple** is the opposite direction: it's a number that 24 and 36 go *into*. 72 works (24 × 3 = 72, 36 × 2 = 72), so 72 is a common **multiple** — but that's a different question.
>
> **Why 12 kits and not 72?** Think about the kits: Mrs. Lee is *splitting* her 24 pencils and 36 erasers evenly among the kits. The number of kits must go *into* 24 and *into* 36. With 12 kits: 2 pencils and 3 erasers in each kit, nothing left over. With 72 kits she'd need to put 24 ÷ 72 of a pencil in each — impossible, and you can't have more kits than items.
>
> **Quick check you can always use:** the GCF can never be bigger than the *smaller* number. 72 is bigger than 24, so it can't be the GCF — that's a red flag to re-read the question.
>
> **Try one:** "Sam has 16 stickers and 24 stamps and wants identical pages with nothing left over, using the greatest number of pages. How many pages?" (Answer: 8 — it goes into both 16 and 24.)
