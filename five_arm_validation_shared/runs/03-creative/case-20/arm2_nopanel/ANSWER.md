# The Seam of the World

He came in just before closing, when the lamps were low and the shop smelled of candlewax and old vellum. I knew at once he wasn't from the city. His coat was cut from no cloth I could name — grey, but the grey moved, like weather seen through a window — and his boots made no sound on my floorboards, which my floorboards do not permit.

"I have a scroll," he said.

I get that a dozen times a day. Most of it is forgery, some of it is dangerous, and once — only once — it was both. I set down my ledger and held out my hand.

The scroll was a plain thing, birch-bark maybe, rolled around a sliver of ashwood. When I unrolled it I confess I laughed. Not kindly. In the center of the page, drawn in ink the color of a clear sky, was the letter S. Not even a fine one. The kind children scratch into frost and fence posts and the margins of their primers: two rows of little strokes joined by a slanting seam, crowned with a hook at the top and another at the bottom. Fourteen lines. I counted.

"This is a doodle," I said. "My niece draws these on my invoices."

"Yes," he said.

"I sell grimoires. Circles of binding. Seals with nine hundred strokes, each one placed by a master, each one checked twice. This—" I tapped it, carefully, the way you tap anything in my trade, "—this is nothing."

"It is the simplest thing I own," he agreed. "That is why it is the strongest."

I have been a dealer of the written arcane for thirty years. I have heard every pitch. I told him I wasn't buying, and then, because the symbol sat there being so smugly simple, I asked what it did.

"Name a lock," he said.

I laughed again and led him to the back, where the iron-bound chest sits that my grandfather warded and my father re-warded and I have warded a third time. Three generations of seals, layered like an onion. I once watched a king's locksmith look at it and go home.

The man set the scroll flat on the lid. Nothing happened. He looked almost apologetic. "It wants a hand," he said. "It was made to be drawn, not kept."

So I drew it. He talked me through the strokes as if I were a child, and there was a pleasure in that I hadn't expected — the three little lines above, the three below, the seam that ties them, the hooks that crown and root it. Fourteen strokes. My hand knew the shape before my mind did. Everyone's hand does, he said. That is the whole secret.

The chest did not groan or flash. The seals did not break — I want that understood, because it matters. They *finished*. All three generations of warding unwound like a knot finally tied correctly, and the lid drifted open an inch, polite as a servant, and inside, my grandfather's ledger — which no one had been able to open since his funeral — lay waiting, with a ribbon marking a page.

I did not read the page while he was there. I mention that so you know I have some manners left.

"What is it?" I asked him. "Who inscribed it? Which school?"

"No school," he said. "No one inscribed it. It was remembered into being. In my country the mages built great circles, and the circles grew so heavy they pulled the magic out of the world like a drain. Everything complex failed. Everything simple survived. This survived because children carry it — hand to hand, fence post to fence post, no book, no master, no fee. A sigil that teaches itself cannot be lost."

"And your country?" I asked, because his coat moved like weather and his boots made no sound.

"Gone quiet," he said, and smiled in a way that closed the subject like a door.

He would not take coin. He took, instead, a jar of my good ink — the sky-colored kind — and a sheet of birch-bark, and before he left he drew the S once more, freehand, better than the scroll, and set it on my counter. "Hang it where children pass," he said. "It likes to be known."

Then the bell over my door rang, though he never touched it, and the street outside was empty in both directions.

I hung it above the doorframe, inside, where the lamplight catches it. And here is the part I would not believe if a customer told me: the shop has never run better. The stock sorts itself on the shelves, gently, overnight. The ledger balances to the copper. The dangerous pieces sit calm, and the forged ones — I swear this — curl at the edges by themselves, as if embarrassed, so I know which to refuse. Fourteen strokes, and the whole shop behaves like a well-kept class of children.

People ask what the symbol is. Mages come in, the serious ones with their nine-hundred-stroke seals, and they squint at it and say, "That's nothing." I let them. Then their apprentices, waiting by the door, look up and say, "Oh, I know that one," and draw it in the air with a finger — three above, three below, the seam, the hooks — and the serious mages go quiet, because their apprentices' fingers glow, faintly, the color of a clear sky.

I never learned the man's name. But I have read my grandfather's ledger page now, and I will tell you what the ribbon marked, since it concerns my trade and, I have decided, my future.

One line, in his hand: *The strongest magic is the kind children will carry for free.*

Underneath, drawn small and neat, fourteen strokes: the S.

I keep a stack of birch-bark by the counter these days. When a child comes in, I sell them nothing. I hand them bark and sky-colored ink, and I teach them the strokes, and I watch the seam of the world tie itself shut again, one small hand at a time.
