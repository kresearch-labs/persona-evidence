# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-20

## The Cool S

My shop, the Bound & Sealed, sits on Inkling Row between a locksmith who deals in wards and a woman who sells bottled thunderstorms. We trade in inscriptions: grimoires, scrolls, charms scrawled on the inside of eggshells. Rule one of the trade: the simpler a circle looks, the older or the stranger its maker. Rule two: when a man in strange clothes sells you something "cool," you buy it.

He came in at the grey end of a Tuesday — long coat, mismatched boots, dust on the coat and power in the dust — carrying a scroll wrapped in butcher paper. Not parchment. Not vellum. *Paper*, grocery-grade, the kind that tears when you look at it. I gave him the professional once-over: no guild marks, no colophon, boots that had walked somewhere very far and very dry.

"What have you got?" I asked, which is how you ask.

"Something cool," he said, and unrolled it on my counter.

And there it was. Seven strokes. Lines meeting lines, points touching points, a shape I have seen a thousand times and never once *read*, because the eye refuses to read it — it just agrees with it. You know it. Everyone knows it. Children draw it on desks, on stone walls, on the borders of homework, in charcoal on the backs of barns, in every country I have ever traded with, in every century. The eight-triangle S. The Cool S.

I did the dangerous thing. I read it.

Under the surface agreement of its geometry, the thing was *severe*. Every stroke was a wardline; every junction a sealed knot; and the whole — I measured it three times with my good instruments — the whole resolved into a lattice that anchored *not to the paper, not to the ink, but to the reader's agreement with it*. It didn't channel power. It *recognized* it. Whatever magic stood in front of the Cool S and declared itself, the S said, in effect: **yes, that is real, and so are you.** It was a sigil of permission. The simplest and most powerful enchantment in existence is a door that opens when you knock honestly.

"Do you know what this is?" I asked him.

"Cool S," he said, with the serenity of a man who has answered this question from every archmage on two continents. "Everybody knows how to draw it. Nobody knows what it does. It has no origin. No school claims it. It just appears, wherever children are bored, perfectly formed, seven strokes." He shrugged. "I found one on the back of a cave wall from before writing. Same shape. I figure somebody's got to keep the things, since nobody can stop drawing them."

I bought it. I paid more than the shop was worth, which he accepted with the dignity of a man trading a stone for bread he was hungry for.

Then I did the second dangerous thing. I hung it on the wall behind my counter, behind glass, where the evening light could catch it, and I watched what happened.

Customers who came in frowning left upright. The bottled thunderstorm woman, mid-argument with her landlord, stopped mid-sentence, looked at the S through the glass, and started laughing so hard she had to sit down. A hedge-witch who had failed her binding examinations for nine years walked in, glanced at it, and said slowly, "It's because I keep trying to bind *other people's* power instead of mine." The locksmith asked it for nothing and received a name for the ship he'd always wanted to build.

The Cool S did not do anything to them. It *agreed* with them, and the agreement was so clean that they could finally hear themselves.

Children stand at the glass with their noses pressed to it. They don't know what it does. They don't need to — that's the secret the scroll carries: the S recognizes its own. Every child who has ever drawn it, in charcoal, in chalk, in the dust of a caravan road, was practicing a seven-stroke spell older than every school and every war, a spell that no kingdom controls because no kingdom can corner the market on bored children.

The man in strange clothes comes by twice a year. He never sells me another. He drinks my coffee, looks at the S in its glass frame, and nods, like a foreman inspecting a fence.

"How many are there?" I asked him once.

"How many what?"

"Cool S's. How many are out there, drawn on things?"

He thought about it honestly, which I respected. "Count the children who ever got bored in class," he said, "and multiply by the number of deskmates watching." He set down his cup. "It's not a symbol, shopkeeper. It's a species. It lives wherever kids sit. We're just the ones who noticed it's *magic*."

He walked off down Inkling Row, boots dusty, whistling something with seven notes.

The S hangs behind my counter. Rule three of the trade, learned that season: sell everything else. Some things you keep, because you are, before anything else, a person who once drew seven lines on a desk and felt, without knowing why, that it was cool — and it was, and it is, and so are you.
