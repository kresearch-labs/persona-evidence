# The Cool S

The most dangerous thing ever left in my shop cost two coppers and a cup of tea.

I buy and sell the written kind of magic: grimoires with their clasps rusted shut, scrolls that hum when the moon is wrong, seals pressed in wax that still remembers the hand that made it. My counter is scarred by a century of bargains. I know what power weighs. It comes in heavy, usually. It comes with a price that makes you count your coins twice.

The man came in at dusk, wearing too many clothes for the season — a winter coat over summer linen, buttons of horn and glass, a hat that cast its shadow the wrong way. He set a scroll tube on the counter as gently as if it were sleeping.

"Inside," he said, "is a cool S."

I unrolled it. One symbol, drawn in an ink that sat a hair above the paper: fourteen strokes, three tiers, the sharp-pointed S every child scratches into a desk. Except this one was drawn true — every line meeting every line exactly, the way no hand can manage twice.

"Two coppers," he said.

"In my trade, the price is a spell too," I said. "Nothing costs two coppers."

"Everything does, eventually." He drank the tea I'd poured. He didn't take the coppers at first; he looked at them a long time, like a man remembering money. Then he left, and his shadow left a moment after him, in the wrong direction.

I did what any sane dealer does with an unknown: I tested it away from the shop, in the yard, at arm's length.

I held it near a jam jar sealed with a keeper's knot. The knot untied itself, politely.

I held it near my strongbox. The lock forgot it had ever been locked.

I held it near my wardstone, and the wards around my shop loosened like a held breath — and I stopped, because I understood two things at once. First: this was the strongest thing I had ever held. Second: it was not a lock and not a key. It opened nothing. It reminded things that they were lines.

I spent the night copying it. The copy worked, faintly — enough to untie a shoelace across the room. My hand couldn't make the lines meet exactly; the true one met the way water meets a level. And I began to see the shape of the thing. Fourteen strokes, three tiers. The first shape every apprentice scribe doodles in the margin, in every country, in every century, and no master teaches it. It comes with the hand, like gripping.

It isn't a sigil, I realized. It's the scaffold sigils are built on. Every seal is a sentence; this is the alphabet learning to stand.

Word of a strong artifact travels, and it brought me Marlow, the debt-collector's clerk, gray in the face, carrying a contract that had grown teeth. His client had signed with a demon of the second circle, and the demon had come to collect the signature — the hand that signed, specifically.

"Can you bind it?" Marlow asked.

"Binding is what started this," I said.

So I unrolled the cool S over the contract instead. The contract unwrote itself from the bottom up, clause by clause, politely, like the knot. The demon stepped out of the paper the way a man steps out of a coat, shook itself small, and turned out to be hardly bigger than a cat and mortally tired of paperwork. It bowed to the scroll. Then it bowed to me, which no demon has ever done, and left through the wall without breaking it.

I should have stopped there.

That night I held the S over my own ledger, where I keep what people owe me. The debts vanished — every one, including three I'd have paid good money to lose. Then the ledger's memory of itself began to go, the pages paling, and my name on the front began to thin like ink under rain. The S does not know a curse from a contract from a name. To it, everything written is a line, and every line can be reminded.

I slammed the ledger shut and pressed my palm flat over my own name until the ink remembered itself. Then I capped the scroll in black wax, wrapped it in three wards of my own devising, and put it in the deepest drawer, under the false bottom, under the thing I keep even deeper.

For a month I sold nothing strange, and the shop was quiet, and I told myself the story was over.

Then a girl came in at the end of summer, maybe ten, selling paper. Schoolwork, mostly — sums, a map of a country that doesn't exist, a dragon with an unreasonable number of wings. In the corner of the last page, in pencil, crooked, drawn by a hand that had never been taught: a cool S.

It was working. Faintly, the way my copy worked. The pencil lines sat a hair above the paper. Where she'd drawn it, the page had stopped aging — I checked with a lens; the paper around it had gone soft and brown, and under the S it was white as the day it was made.

"Who taught you that?" I asked.

"Nobody," she said, shrugging. "Everybody draws it."

I bought the whole stack for a silver and told her it was for the texture. She left happy, and I sat with the page in the lamplight for a long time.

Here is what I know now, and what I will never write down where the S can reach it. The strongest seal in the world was never sealed, never sold, never owned. It is passed hand to hand in the margins of homework, in the dust of school desks, in every language and none, and it keeps a little of the world fresh wherever a child has drawn it — which is everywhere, which is always.

The man in the wrong-season clothes was not a courier. He was a librarian.

The scroll is still under my counter, in the deepest drawer. Some nights, when business is slow, I take it out, uncap the wax, and add one crooked stroke of my own to the margin — badly, the way a child would, the way you're supposed to.

The most dangerous thing ever left in my shop cost two coppers and a cup of tea. I have never charged anyone for it since.
