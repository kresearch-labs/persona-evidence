# The Cool S

In my trade you learn to read a scroll before you unroll it. Vellum that old has a smell, a weight, a way of holding the light. I have appraised binding circles from dead languages, seals pressed in wax by kings who never existed, inscriptions that had to be read in mirrors for everyone's safety. My shop sits between a laundromat and a pawn broker, and the bell above my door has rung for emperors' agents and for grandmothers selling their late husbands' mistakes. I thought nothing could surprise me anymore.

Then the man in the strange clothes came in.

I say strange because I cannot describe them better. His coat was fashionable in several centuries and in none of them recently. His shoes made no sound on my floor, which is a trick I have only ever seen from people who are not, strictly speaking, people. He laid a scroll on my counter, and he asked for twenty dollars, and when I told him — honestly, out of professional habit — that an item properly appraised might fetch more, he looked at me with enormous tired eyes and said, "Twenty is fine. It has to be a sale."

There is a rule in this business: never buy what a seller is too eager to lose. I have followed it for thirty years. I broke it that afternoon, and I cannot tell you why, except that he looked like a man at the end of a very long shift, and some part of me understood that the twenty dollars was not the price of the scroll. It was the price of my consent.

He left. I unrolled it.

On the vellum, in ink that had not faded so much as a shade in what I now believe to be a very long time, was a single symbol. Two short vertical strokes, one above the other. Three lines meeting them from the left, three from the right, joined by diagonals into a lean, pointed figure. Six lines in the upper half, six in the lower. Elegant. Symmetrical. Absurdly simple.

You know it. You drew it on the cover of your school binder. Everyone did. No one taught you; a friend showed you, who was shown by a friend, who was shown by a cousin, and the chain goes back further than anyone has ever been able to trace. The man had called it, with the straightest face I have ever seen, "a cool S."

I laughed, alone in my shop, for a full minute. Then I stopped laughing, because the candle on my desk was leaning toward the scroll. Both candles. All four. Flame does not lean toward ink. Flame does not lean toward anything but air.

I spent three weeks on the diagnostics. I will spare you the tedium and give you the results. The symbol is a seal — the purest, most efficient seal I have ever encountered, and I have handled work attributed to Solomon's own circle. Most seals are baroque things, rings of names and warnings, because binding is hard and the universe negotiates. This one is six lines. It does not negotiate. Whatever it holds closed, it holds closed the way a held breath holds: totally, and with effort.

And it is everywhere. That is the part that took me the longest to accept. Once I knew what I was looking for, I found it in the margins of a ninth-century psalter, doodled by a monk who should have known better. Scratched into the base of a Roman milestone. Chalked on the wall of a cave whose other paintings are forty thousand years old. In every archive, in every era, in every culture, the same six lines, unattributed, unexplained, dismissed by every scholar as a modern contagion — except that it is older than writing. The scholars of my trade had all made the same mistake I did. They assumed something that simple could not matter.

Here is what I believe now, and I will set it down plainly, because plainness is the whole point.

Seals decay. Ink fades, stone crumbles, the names of the binding angels get mispronounced and lose their grip. If you wished to maintain a ward across ten thousand years, you would not carve it once in a mountain. You would make it so simple, so satisfying, so *cool*, that every generation would teach it to itself. You would hide the spell in the wanting. Every child who props a ruler against a binder and draws those six lines tightens the seal a little. Humanity is the largest monastic order in history, and not one of us knows we took vows.

And somewhere there must be a master copy — the original, the anchor from which the pattern propagates, the one true instance that all the billions of little copies answer to.

It is in my back room, in a climate-controlled case, next to the register.

The man was not a seller. He was a keeper, and a keeper can only be relieved by a willing buyer — that is why it had to be a sale, and why he could not take more than twenty dollars, and why his shoes made no sound, because a man who has carried that weight for long enough stops being entirely a man. I do not know how long his shift was. I know how mine ends, and with whom: some future fool, in some future shop, who will break the oldest rule in the trade for reasons he cannot explain.

Until then, business is good. Word gets around, the way it gets around about the S itself. Children come in off the street with drawings — on binder paper, on napkins, on the backs of math tests — and ask, giggling, whether I buy cool S's.

I buy every one. A dollar apiece, no haggling, filed flat in the drawer by date. Their parents think I am a harmless eccentric. The children think I am in on the joke. On quiet nights the scroll in the back room hums like a struck glass, and I take out a clean sheet and draw the six lines myself — two strokes, three and three, point and point — and feel the whole vast sleeping world pull its blanket a little tighter.

The bell above my door still rings. I still read a scroll before I unroll it. But I keep a pencil by the register now, and I am never out of ones.
