## The Hoard of Emberwick

The dragon's name was Percival, and his hoard was one coin.

It was not a modest coin. It was a sovereign of the Old Mint, gold so deep it looked like it had been dipped in sunset, with a king on one side whose face had worn smooth from two hundred years of being admired. Percival had been given it at hatching, as all dragons are — the first coin of a lifetime's hoard — and he had guarded it ever since.

The problem was that Percival was the size of a robin, and the world was not.

He had made his lair in the hollow wall of an old clockmaker's shop, behind the gears, in a nest of lost buttons and one sigil-damp matchhead. The shop's cat, a vast grey continent named Duchess, had long ago decided Percival was a piece of the furniture that sometimes talked, and left him alone. The clockmaker himself had never noticed the dragon at all, which was how Percival liked it. Treasure that is seen is treasure that is counted, and treasure that is counted is treasure that is taken.

Then, one autumn, the coin was seen.

A magpie saw it — a glint through a crack in the wall — and came. She landed on the sill and rapped her beak on the wall. "Little lizard. I see what you keep. I will trade you a whole *string* of shinies for it. Shiny and blue."

Percival raised his head from the coin and let a thread of smoke curl from his nostrils, the way his mother had taught him: **this hoard is defended**. "My name is Percival," he said, in a voice like a struck key, "and this is the Hoard of Emberwick. It is not for trade."

"It's one coin," said the magpie, reasonably.

"It is the *foundation*," said Percival. "Everything else is built on the coin you have. I have this one. It is my whole mountain, and I am its whole dragon, and it is defended."

The magpie could not lift the coin — it was heavy as a horseshoe to her — but she left thinking, and thinking, for hoards, is how sieges start.

The next threat wore a waistcoat. The clockmaker's nephew came to sort the shop's inventory, found the hollow wall, and reached in with a greedy hand. He grasped not the coin but Percival, lifting him up by the tail, blinking at the tiny furious wings. "Well," said the nephew, "a curiosity. The collector in the city would pay —"

Percival did the only thing a small dragon can do against a large hand: he made himself *true*. He flared — all the fire a body his size could hold, every scrap of heat his little furnace-heart had banked — and it was not enough to burn, but it was enough to *startle*, and the nephew dropped him, and in the fall Percival sank his needle-teeth into the thumb that had dared.

The nephew sucked his thumb, swore, and — being practical — pocketed the coin instead.

Percival watched his mountain leave the wall in a stranger's pocket, and felt the specific horror of a dragon flying at pocket-speed, too small to matter.

But here is what the nephew did not know, and what the magpie could not see, and what even Percival had only suspected until that terrible moment: the coin was *sigil-damp* for a reason. The old clockmaker had cured the wall with it, decades ago, a coin laid in the heart of his shop the way you lay a keel in a ship — and a coin that has lain in the heart of timekeeping for two hundred years does not sit still. It keeps time, a little. Enough.

At midnight, in the nephew's pocket, on the day the clocks fall back, the coin *ticked*. Once, loudly, like a door unlocking. And the hour rewound itself around it — one small, permitted hour, the hour the world gives away anyway — and the coin was back in the wall, and Percival was coiled beside it, heart hammering, before the nephew's dream had even finished turning.

The nephew woke with a pocket full of nothing and a story nobody would believe.

The magpie came back in the spring, because magpies forgive. She stood at the crack with a pearl in her beak — a real one, and Percival's resolve wavered, because a hoard is meant to grow and his had not grown in two hundred years.

"Trade," she said. "Pearl for coin."

Percival looked at his coin a long time. The king's worn face. The first and only. And he understood, finally, what his mother had never gotten to tell him: the hoard was never the coin. The hoard was the *defending*. He was not the guardian of one coin; he was the shape a promise makes when it keeps watch — small, stubborn, burning.

"No," he said gently. "But stay, and hear how the coin was won. It's a better story than the pearl."

The magpie — being a magpie — stayed for the story, and kept the pearl, and came back every spring after, which is how Percival's hoard grew: not in gold, but in visits, one per year, each one defended, each one kept.

And if you press your ear to the wall of the old clockmaker's shop, behind the gears, you can hear it: the tiniest dragon in the world, counting his treasure. *One.* The number never changes. The value always does.
