# The Last Coin of Emberwick

Pip was a dragon the size of a teacup, and he was at war.

His hoard lay before him: one gold coin, worn smooth by a hundred years of thumbs, resting on a scrap of velvet in the hollow of an old oak. It was not much, as hoards went. His grandfather had slept on mountains of it. His mother had guarded a chest so deep that thieves had drowned in it. Pip had a coin. But it was *his* coin, and the world was full of thieves.

The first came at dawn — a squirrel with a soldier's eyes. She did not creep. She marched down the trunk, tail flicking like a banner, and stopped three paces from the velvet.

"That's mine," she said.

"It is not," said Pip. He climbed atop the coin and spread his wings, which had a wingspan roughly equal to a spread hand, and were therefore more gesture than threat. "It is the hoard of Emberwick, last of the furnace line, and it is defended."

"By what?"

"By me."

The squirrel looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the coin, and Pip saw her do the arithmetic — one dragon, teacup-sized; one coin, pocket-sized — and he watched her decide. She lunged.

What happened next was not dignified. Pip breathed fire, a jet no thicker than a candle flame, and it caught the tip of her tail. She shrieked, spun, and swatted him with the burning tail, and the two of them went rolling through the leaves in a ball of squeaks and smoke. When it ended, the squirrel sat in the nettles, tail smoldering, and Pip sat on his coin, one wing bent at an angle wings do not go.

"Keep it," the squirrel muttered, and limped off. "It's not even worth it."

Pip straightened his wing, which took most of the morning, and did not think about what she'd said. It was not even worth it. The thought sat in his ear like a burr.

The second came at noon, and it was not a squirrel. It was a magpie, and magpies do not march. She simply appeared on the rim of the hollow, head tilted, one eye like a bead of black glass.

"Beautiful," she said, meaning the coin.

"Yes," said Pip.

"I could get you ten more."

"There are no more."

"There are always more," said the magpie. "That's the thing about gold. It moves. It circulates. It comes around again, if you're clever enough to catch it. But you—" she hopped closer, and Pip flared his wings, and she stopped, unbothered, "—you're sitting on the last one. Do you know what that makes you?"

"A dragon," said Pip, "with a hoard."

"It makes you a museum," said the magpie. "And museums get robbed."

She left without stealing anything, which Pip somehow found more frightening than if she had. He spent the afternoon counting his hoard. One. He counted it again. One. The number did not improve.

The third came at dusk, and it was a girl.

She did not sneak, and she did not bargain. She knelt in the leaves beside the oak, and she looked at the coin, and then she looked at Pip for the longest time of anyone had ever looked at him.

"You're the one from the stories," she said. "The dragon of Emberwick. My grandmother said you used to guard the whole valley's treasure. Before."

"Before," said Pip. He did not move from the coin.

"The men came and took it in wagons," the girl said. "When the valley emptied. I know. I'm sorry." She reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward something that might bolt — and set something down on the velvet beside the coin. A button. Brass, with an anchor stamped in it. "I don't want your coin. I wanted to see if you were real."

She left. Pip looked at the button for a long time.

That night, three thieves came in one hour, and Pip understood, finally, what the magpie had meant. Word had spread. A dragon who guards one coin is a dragon who can be overwhelmed. The first was a stoat, and Pip drove him off with fire and fury. The second was a rat, and Pip drove him off with less fire and more fury. The third was a boy with a net, and by then Pip was bleeding from a bite along his flank, and his flame was a blue whisper, and the boy was reaching, reaching—

Pip did the only thing left. He picked up the coin and *ran*.

He ran on wings and legs together, a lopsided, clattering, undignified flight, the coin clutched against his chest like a heart he was carrying home. The boy chased him to the edge of the hollow and gave up. Pip did not stop until he was deep in the bracken, under a stone he'd never used, in a dark he did not know.

He set the coin down. He curled around it. And in the silence, with his flank aching and his fire guttered, he finally heard what the squirrel had said. *It's not even worth it.*

The coin was worth perhaps a loaf of bread. His grandfather's hoard had bought a kingdom. And Pip had spent a whole day of fire and blood defending a loaf of bread, and he would spend a hundred more, and somewhere in that arithmetic — the magpie had been right — he had stopped being a dragon and become a keeper of a single small thing.

He almost went back to the oak. It was a better hollow. Everyone knew it was his.

Instead, Pip looked at the coin in the dark, and then at the brass button, which he had brought too, without deciding to. One was worth a loaf. The other was worth nothing at all. He had defended both the same way.

"That's the hoard," he said aloud, to no one, and the dark did not argue. "Both of them."

He slept. In the morning, the war would start again — the stoat, the rat, the boy, the magpie's clever circle of gold. Let it come. Emberwick was small, but Emberwick was *defended*, and the world would learn, one thief at a time, exactly how much a single coin is worth to the dragon who holds it.

More than a loaf. More than a kingdom.

It was worth the burning.
