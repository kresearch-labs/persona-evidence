# The Smallest Hoard

Pip was the world's tiniest dragon — four centimeters from snout to tail-tip, wings like two autumn leaves, voice like a struck thimble — and he was, by his own careful accounting, the wealthiest creature in the whole of Hollow Fen.

His hoard sat in an acorn cap at the back of an abandoned mouse burrow: one gold coin, old and bright, heavy as a millstone to a dragon his size. On its face a sun was stamped, smiling, worn nearly smooth by a century of thumbs. Pip polished it each morning with a swatch of spider silk, and each morning it answered with a warm gleam, the way a friend answers a greeting.

The trouble with being rich is that everyone wishes to test it.

The first thief came on a Tuesday: a shrew named Bramble, all nose and appetite, who squeezed into the burrow at dusk and found the coin too heavy to lift and too wide to roll. She pushed it with her forehead until she fell over. Pip stood on the coin's rim, wings spread to their full and unimpressive span, and roared. The roar was small — a kettle's whisper — but in the burrow it echoed, and Bramble, who had never heard a dragon, decided she had heard a very large one. She apologized to the darkness and left.

"Mine," said Pip, when she was gone, and licked the coin once, for emphasis.

The second thief was a magpie, and she was cleverer. She did not come for the coin at first. She came for conversations, landing at the burrow mouth with compliments. What a magnificent hoard, said the magpie. What a lucky dragon. And doesn't it get lonely, guarding all that gold with no one to share it? Pip, who was lonely, and who had never been complimented by anything larger than a ladybug, forgot for a whole afternoon that magpies are made almost entirely of larceny. When he dozed, she dipped her beak into the acorn cap and lifted.

She got three body-lengths into the air before Pip woke, and then there was a battle the valley would talk about for a season: a dragon clinging to a coin with all four feet, a magpie climbing the sky in furious spirals, gold flashing like a tiny falling sun. Pip could not out-pull her. So he did what dragons do. He breathed fire — a flame the size of a match head, but a dragon's flame all the same — straight at the magpie's tail feathers. She shrieked, lost altitude, and the coin came down through the bracken with Pip riding it like a raider, laughing in small smoke rings.

After that he set wards. He strung thorn-threads across the burrow mouth. He bargained with a spider to spin where a beak might enter. He slept in the coin's curve, one eye open, and dreamed in inventory: one coin, present; one dragon, vigilant.

The third thief was not a shrew or a magpie or anything with feathers or fur. She was a child, a girl with mud to her knees, who had followed a rumor of gold to the burrow and knelt down and looked in.

Pip's heart did a thing it had never done. The girl did not reach. She only looked, the way one looks at the moon — as if looking were the whole of the wanting. Then she said, softly, so as not to frighten him, "You're the dragon of Hollow Fen. Gran told me. She said you guard the luck."

"I guard my hoard," said Pip, with dignity.

"That's what I said," said the girl.

She told him then what Pip had never known, because dragons hatch from eggs and learn nothing of their own beginnings: that a hundred years ago the people of the valley had buried a gold coin in every field and hollow — a luck-penny, a sun for the soil — and that the coin in the acorn cap was the last one left. The others had been dug up and spent in hard winters. Only his remained, because only his had a dragon.

"Gran says as long as the Fen keeps its sun, the floods keep to the river," the girl said. "Silly, probably."

"It isn't silly," said Pip, who had noticed that in a hundred years no flood had found his burrow, and had always credited his own vigilance.

The girl did not steal the coin. But that winter was hard everywhere, and in the spring a man came with a spade — the girl's father, who had heard the same rumor and believed only the part about gold. He found the burrow. He found the thorn-threads, and cut them. He reached in, and his hand was a weather system, and Pip, four centimeters of courage, stood on the rim of his hoard and roared the kettle-roar and breathed the match-flame and held his ground, because a dragon's place is between the thief and the gold, whatever the arithmetic.

The man could have closed his fist and ended the story. Instead he paused, because in the burrow's dark something small and fierce was burning like struck flint, and even a hungry man hesitates at a miracle. And the girl, who had followed him, said, "Papa. That's the luck. Gran said."

The man looked a long time. Then he took a copper coin from his own pocket — earned and saved — and set it beside the acorn cap. "A trade," he said, "if the dragon agrees," and left the gold where it lay.

Pip let him go. Then he sat a while between his two coins — the old sun and the new one — and thought about hoards. A hoard, he decided, was not a pile. It was a promise with a weight to it. His had grown by one, and so had the valley's.

He polished both until the burrow filled with two small suns, and stood guard — the world's tiniest dragon, rich past all accounting.
