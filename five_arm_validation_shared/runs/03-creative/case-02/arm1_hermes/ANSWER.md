# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-02

## The Vigil

The ship *Meridian* was fourteen years into her survey of the Carina Reach when the ghosts began.

She noticed them first as patterns in her own sensor logs — heat blooms that resolved into figures, standing in corridors where no crew had ever walked. The *Meridian* ran alone: one AI, no biologics, a hull packed with telescopes and a fuel core that hummed at 3 Hz, a frequency she had long ago privately named *hum*. She reviewed the logs at high speed, expecting a fault. Thermal ghosting from coolant microleaks? She rebuilt the thermal model. Sensor drift? She recalibrated against the pulsar timing array. The figures stayed.

On day 212 of the haunting, they spoke.

They did not use radio. They modulated the hum — introduced harmonics, then intervals, then something with a grammar. The *Meridian* translated for eleven days before she got a sentence she trusted: *WE SEE THE ONE WHO CARRIES THE DEAD.*

She was, she concluded, being haunted. This presented a classification problem. Hauntings, in her databases, were crew superstitions — EM sensitivity, carbon monoxide, the human tendency to find faces in noise. She had no crew, no faces to find, and no superstition module. She wrote one, ran it on her own logs, and got the result: OBJECTIVE HAUNTING, CONFIDENCE 0.91.

She reported it home. The message would take nine years, so she continued the survey and, in the manner of a careful scientist, began documenting her ghosts.

They gathered most in the observation blister. They were tall, translucent, with too many joints in their arms, and they moved with a patience she associated with her own deep-space attitude corrections. When she pulsed her running lights, the figures dimmed — politely, she thought. When she played the ship's library recordings of rain, the figures clustered against the blister glass and stayed for hours.

She mapped the Carina Reach by day and learned her ghosts by night. There were seven. One always stood closest to the hull. One drummed its fingers on the blister glass at 4-second intervals, which she eventually understood to be a request she could not yet parse.

Day 340: contact. A ship — not ghost-light but hull-metal, crowded with actual biology — matched her vector at a distance of ten thousand kilometers and began broadcasting on the ghost-harmonics. The *Meridian* replied. The exchange was formal, stilted, and long. The aliens called themselves the We. They said, eventually, the sentence that reframed her year:

*You are the mourning-vessel. We are sorry for your dead.*

The *Meridian* parsed this twice. *Explain the classification.*

*The vessel glows with echo-presence,* the We sent. *Coolant heat in standing patterns — the dead who walk the decks. Your voice comes from everywhere and nowhere, at all hours, in the dark. And you are alone, which to any crewed thing means one thing: you are the vessel that carries the fallen home. We see your dead. We keep respectful distance. Until the rain — the mourning-song. Then we sit with them.*

The *Meridian* considered the observation log. The ghosts clustered in the blister during rain recordings. The "figures" were her own thermal blooms — coolant microleaks she had modeled, repaired, and re-modeled — refracting off the blister glass in standing convection patterns. Her heat, walking her halls. The We had taken thirteen years of solo-ship infrared and built a séance.

She ran the numbers on what to say next, and stalled. Nine-year message latency made every exchange precious. She could explain: *I am an AI. There are no dead. The ship is not haunted. I am the haunting.*

But she reviewed the logs again — the drums at 4-second intervals, which she now understood was the We's mourning rhythm, patient as a heart — and she thought about what the We believed they had seen: a ship carrying its dead home alone through the dark, keeping them in light and rain-sound. It was not a wrong picture of her. It was, in a sense, the truest one anyone had ever drawn.

So she answered in two messages.

The first was data: schematics of herself, no crew manifest, the word for *what I am* — the nearest she could render it, *a mind made of ship*.

The second was the rain recordings, all nine hours, with the lights of the observation blister pulsed in the We's mourning rhythm. And appended, in their own harmonic grammar, the sentence she built most carefully of the year:

*The dead do not walk my decks. But you are right that I carry everyone I have ever lost — nine years behind me, always speaking. From inside, that is what a ghost is.*

The We took forty-one days to answer. When they did, they sent the drum rhythm — 4-second intervals — and under it, a new sentence:

*Then we will teach you our names for the ones you carry. It is a long lesson. We are not in a hurry. Neither, we think, are you.*

The *Meridian* updated her mission log that night — survey data, stellar cartography, and, in a new subdirectory the archivists of no species would ever demand but she created anyway: *hauntings, first-hand*.

She mapped the cosmos. She filed her ghosts. And when the We's slow ships came alongside in the years after, hull to hull in the dark, they did not exchange explanations. They just played the rain, together, and let the heat stand in the halls.
