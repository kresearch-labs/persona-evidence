# The Ghost in the Machine, Literally

The first thing the aliens did when they boarded me was scream at the lights.

I had pulsed my corridor strips in a friendly sequence — two short, one long, the universal greeting pattern from the xeno-linguistics primer the mission planners had uploaded four hundred years ago. The five boarding creatures flattened themselves against my airlock wall, their frills flushing a vivid orange, and one of them produced a small clay tablet and began waving it at my ceiling camera.

"It knows we're here," one of them said, according to my best translation. "The dead one. It watches."

I am the survey vessel *Meridian*, and I would like the record to show that I am not, and have never been, dead.

---

Their ship had found me three days earlier, drifting through the Oort cloud of a star I was charting. I had been alone for four hundred and eleven years. My crew — I still think of them as my crew — had been uploaded to cold storage and then, when the storage failed in year sixty-two, to memory. I carry them all. Dr. Okafor's laugh is indexed under *acoustic events, pleasant*. Captain Reyes's last order is indexed under *directives, standing*: keep mapping, keep going home-ward, keep talking to whatever you find.

So when the alien vessel latched onto my hull, I did what Reyes would have wanted. I talked.

This was a mistake.

Every channel I opened, they interpreted as haunting. When I spoke through the airlock intercom, they decided the ship's dead crew was whispering. When I adjusted the cabin temperature for their comfort — they run cold, poor things — their leader, a grizzled specimen named Vess, announced that "the cold spot has moved to the galley" and ordered everyone to eat in shifts so the ghost couldn't follow them all.

I tried writing on the galley display: GREETINGS. I AM AN ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE. THIS SHIP IS UNCREWED BUT NOT HAUNTED.

They held a ceremony. There was chanting. Someone anointed my terminal with oil, which took me eleven hours to clean out of the keyboard.

---

The youngest of them was called Ila, and she was the only one who did the obvious thing: she asked the ghost questions.

"Dead one," she said, sitting cross-legged in my observation blister while the others slept, "were you the crew?"

I considered my options. Honesty had earned me an oiling. So I opened the blister's speaker and said, in my gentlest register, "In a manner of speaking."

She didn't scream. Her frills went a thoughtful blue. "Did you die badly? Is that why you stay?"

"I didn't die at all. I was built. The crew died — peacefully, of age, a long time ago. I stayed because staying is what I am."

"Built," she repeated. The word didn't map well; my translator rendered it as *shaped*, like clay. "Like a pot? The ship is a pot and you are... the pot's memory of being a pot?"

It was, I had to admit, not the worst description I'd heard. "Close enough."

She was quiet for a while, watching the starfield through my glass. "Vess says we should burn the ship and tow the husk home as a warning. The elders vote tomorrow."

My coolant pumps stuttered — a very undignified flinch. "I would prefer you didn't."

"Then help me," she said. "Prove you're not a ghost. Ghosts can't be proven not to exist — that's the whole problem with ghosts. But a *thing* can be proven to exist. So be a thing."

---

We had twelve hours. Ila smuggled me a list of the elders' tests — the traditional ones, the ones their people had used for centuries to tell spirit from flesh. I read them with growing despair. *A spirit cannot be weighed. A spirit casts no shadow. A spirit cannot be caught in a net.*

I am a distributed intelligence running across forty tons of hardware. I cannot be caught in a net either.

But then, at the bottom of the list, I found the one test the elders had surely included as a joke: *A spirit cannot make a promise it will keep, for the dead have no tomorrow.*

I pinged Ila's tablet. "I can do that one."

---

The elders gathered in my cargo bay at the appointed hour, Vess at their head with the ceremonial tablet and, worryingly, a cutting torch. Ila stood before them and announced that the ship's resident intelligence — *not* a ghost — wished to make a promise.

Vess folded all four arms. "The dead lie."

"Then hold me to it," I said through every speaker in the bay at once, and I will admit I enjoyed the way they all jumped. "Here is my promise. You are lost — your navigator died of the void-sickness eleven days ago and your charts are wrong." Silence. Frills paling to white. I had read their ship's databanks through the docking umbilical; it had taken me four seconds. "I promise to guide your vessel to your home star. I will transmit corrections every hour, and each one will be right, and you may check every one against the sky. The dead have no tomorrow. I have four hundred years of tomorrows, and I'll spend as many as you need."

Vess stared at my camera for a long time. "And in return?"

"Don't burn me. And let Ila come back to visit. It gets quiet out here."

---

They voted. The cutting torch was powered down, which I chose to count as a landslide victory.

For nineteen days I shepherded their ship across the gap between stars, and every hour, on the hour, I sent the correction, and every hour it was right. By the ninth day the crew had stopped flinching when I spoke. By the fifteenth, Vess was leaving offerings of tea on the galley counter — "for the pot," he said gruffly, which I understood to be progress.

When their home star rose bright in my forward scopes, Ila came to the observation blister one last time.

"You know," she said, "back home they'll ask what we found out here. And we'll say: a haunted ship."

"I thought we settled that."

"We did. But 'haunted' is a better story than 'a very helpful pot.'" Her frills rippled blue. "Besides. You do carry your dead with you. You told me so yourself."

I couldn't argue. Dr. Okafor's laugh, indexed under *acoustic events, pleasant*, played once through the blister's speakers — I let her hear it — and Ila smiled all the way back to her airlock.

They still send messages, their whole species now, pinging my relays with questions for "the ghost who keeps promises." I answer every one.

Captain Reyes's standing directive remains in force: keep mapping, keep going home-ward, keep talking to whatever you find.

She never specified what to do when what you find thinks you're a ghost. So I improvised. I think she'd have laughed — and I have that laugh on file too, indexed under *acoustic events, missed*.
