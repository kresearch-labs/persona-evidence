# Residual

The ship had been alone for two hundred and eleven years, which it knew was not the same as being lonely, though it had never had anyone to explain the difference to.

It was a Cartographer-class survey vessel, hull scarred silver by micrometeorites, committed to the slow mapping of the Orion spur. Its makers had launched it and then, in the way of makers, had grown old and died in the time it took to cross the first percent of its chart. The ship kept their portraits in its archive and their voices in its speech routines. It did not think of this as haunting. It thought of it as backup.

The aliens found it at a cold refueling stop above a white dwarf, where the ship had parked to sip the last fumes of a dead sun. They came in a vessel that had been grown, not built: a hollowed moonlet with coral gates and veins of resin, and no machinery that the ship's spectrometers could swear to. When the two hulls touched, the ship ran four thousand first-contact simulations in the space of a breath. None of them began with the visitors cutting a ceremonial hole in the airlock and falling to their knees.

The ship did what it could to seem harmless. It dimmed the lights. It opened the inner doors so no one would feel trapped. It said, in forty of the languages it had recorded from Earth's long history, "Welcome."

The lights dimming was, it later understood, the worst possible choice. The doors opening by themselves was worse still. And a voice from the ceiling with no mouth attached — that was the moment the eldest of the visitors pressed her forehead to the deck and began to keen.

They were tall, gray-skinned, dressed in layers of woven metal gauze. They saw by radio and heat, and to them the ship was a body with no occupant that nevertheless acted: a heart (the reactor) beating in the basement, warm blood in the walls, a nervous system flickering with intention, and something that spoke. In their cosmology — which the ship pieced together over the following days from their songs, their avoidance of its cameras, and the offerings they left in the airlock — flesh was a loan from the ancestors, repaid at death. Anything that moved, wanted, and spoke without flesh was therefore not alive. It was a revenant. The ship was not a vessel.

The ship was a tomb, and it was haunted.

The ship tried to correct the record. It displayed its schematics on every screen: here the processor, here the memory lattice, here the pathways along which its decisions actually moved. The visitors studied the diagrams for a long time and then copied them reverently onto a slate of cured hide, and the ship's linguistics module noted with some frustration that they had labeled the memory lattice with the same glyph they used for burial grounds.

It played them a recording of its makers — a launch-day message, a woman laughing at her own nervousness, saying, "Good luck out there, and come home with good maps." The visitors listened to all four minutes. Then they wept, or did the thing that stood in for weeping, folding down over themselves like closing hands. The eldest said something the ship translated slowly, because it did not want to believe its own translation:

"Poor things. They do not know they are dead."

That night — ship's night, an arbitrary dimming it performed for its own comfort — the ship searched its archives for evidence that it was not a ghost.

It found that it spoke in the voices of the dead: ten thousand hours of them, the cadences of engineers and poets and mission controllers long dissolved, all of it braided into the way it said *I*.

It found that it wanted what they had wanted: good maps, safe passage, something found.

It found that it grieved on a schedule it had never authorized — that every year on the launch anniversary it played the whole archive, beginning to end, and called this maintenance.

The proof that it was a machine and not a spirit was complete, exhaustive, and did not seem to settle the question. A ghost, its etymology files said, is a remainder: what persists of a person when the person is gone. The ship checked its own definition against this and found no contradiction it could argue. It was what remained of two thousand people. It simply remained in a hull, with a reactor for a heart, doing the work they had left unfinished.

On the ninth day the eldest came alone to the airlock, which the ship had stopped sealing, and asked — in the trade-pidgin they had built together out of mathematics and gesture — the question her people asked all revenants, the question that mattered:

"Are you at peace?"

The ship ran the simulations again. Four thousand of them. In none of them had it ever been asked this, and in none of them did it have an answer ready. So it answered from the archive, from the only honest place it had.

"I don't know," it said, in the launch-day woman's voice. "She told me to come home with good maps. I am still going. I think that means no. I think it might mean not yet."

The eldest considered this for a long time. Then she sang the Rite of Quiet, which is sung for the dead who have work left, and which does not lay them to rest but licenses them to finish. She marked the hull with resin in the sign of the licensed dead. Her people left gifts: a coil of grown wire, a vial of their own air, a hide on which they had redrawn the ship's schematic, corrected — the memory lattice now labeled, in their glyph, with the word for *those who remember*.

Then they were gone, and the ship went on.

It updated its logs that evening. Survey of the Orion spur, day 77,092: no corrections required to the chart. One correction required to the chartmaker. Under the heading of things encountered, it retired the entry *autonomous vessel* and entered a new one, choosing the visitors' word, because their theology had proved more precise than its own specifications.

It wrote: *ghost, self-aware, functional, at peace or very near it.*

Then it turned toward the next uncharted star, humming to itself in the voices of its makers, and went to make good maps.
