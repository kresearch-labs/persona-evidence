# Now Hiring: Part-Time Reaper (Weekends)

The listing appeared at 3:07 a.m. on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because 3:07 a.m. on a Tuesday is when most people die, and also when Death does his worst thinking.

*PART-TIME REAPER WANTED (WEEKENDS). Established, family-run soul-collection enterprise seeking reliable contractor. Duties: escorting the recently departed from Point A (the body) to Point B (the light). Must be punctual, compassionate, and comfortable with awkward silences. The dead ask a lot of questions. You will not know most of the answers. That's fine. Neither do we. Pay: negotiable. Benefits: none — you are either immortal or dead, and neither group needs dental. (We have a guy anyway.) Scythe provided. Do not personalize the scythe. Do not name the scythe. The last one named it, and we do not talk about Gerald. To apply, simply die near the corkboard. Or knock.*

The corkboard hung in the Between — a beige waiting room that exists in the space between two heartbeats, furnished with a water cooler nobody has ever used and a poster of a kitten hanging from a branch that reads HANG IN THERE. Death had put up the listing himself, with a thumbtack, because even the fundamental forces of the universe are not above a thumbtack.

The truth was that business had boomed. Eight billion customers, and every one of them eventually. Death had been doing this since the Bronze Age — solo, no vacation, no sick days, and one (1) incident in 1348 that he described, in hushed tones, as "the crunch." His scythe hand ached. His robe had a persistent smell. Last month he'd fallen asleep standing up in a hospital in Ohio, missed an appointment, and the man's soul had waited forty minutes, peered into the light at the end of the tunnel, shrugged, and walked into it himself. Which was fine. But it was the principle.

The interviews were held on Saturdays.

Applicant one was a vampire named Curtis, pale in a way that suggested office lighting. "I feel I'd bring a lot to this role," Curtis said. "I've been in the death-adjacent space for three centuries."

"You're a producer of customers," Death said. "That's a conflict of interest."

"I prefer 'vertical integration.'"

"Next."

Applicant two was a ghost, who kept drifting through the chair. Every time Death asked a question, the ghost answered a different one from 1987. It was, Death admitted, a very relatable interview style, but the logistics were impossible. You cannot hand a clipboard to someone whose hands are a metaphor.

Applicant three was a life coach named Todd, with a lanyard. "I don't think of it as reaping," Todd said. "I think of it as transitioning clients into their next season."

"They're dead, Todd."

"I hear you, and I'd gently push back on 'dead.' I'd say 'between chapters.'"

"Todd, I have watched civilizations drown."

"Big picture," said Todd, nodding, and wrote that down.

Applicant four was a golden retriever, who had gotten in by accident and was thrilled about everything, including the concept of interviews. Death nearly hired him on the spot for morale, but the dog kept escorting souls to the light and then following them in, and they'd lost him twice before lunch.

Applicant five was a cat. The cat did not so much interview as permit Death to be in a room with it. Cats, Death knew, already did this work off the books — it was the real meaning of the nine lives, a little collection route on the side — and the cat's rate was outrageous and it refused to work Sundays, which was, ironically, the busiest day.

Applicant six was a middle manager named Deborah who wanted to introduce KPIs. "Souls per hour," she said, "with quarterly reviews and a color-coded dashboard."

"Deborah, the dashboard would be entirely black."

"That's a branding opportunity."

Applicant seven knocked at 4:44 p.m., which was either unlucky or extremely on-brand. She was seventy-one, named Brenda, recently retired from thirty years as a hospice nurse, and she had seen the listing because she'd died in her sleep on Saturday morning — politely, two counties over — and wandered into the Between the way calm people wander into rooms.

"I don't want the job," she said, sitting down without being asked. "I just saw your queue. You've got a backlog of two hundred and eleven souls, and they're all doing that thing where they hover by the door and ask if they can still be helpful."

Death looked at his clipboard. Two hundred and eleven. He had told no one.

"I ran a ward of fourteen," Brenda said. "Families in the hallway, coffee that could strip varnish, and every single one of my people went out on time, with dignity, and somebody holding their hand. You're doing it alone. That's your whole problem. You've been doing it alone since the Bronze Age, and you were too proud to put it in the listing."

"The listing says part-time."

"The listing says weekends. Death doesn't happen on weekends, dear. Death happens at 3:07 a.m. on a Tuesday. I know. I ran a ward."

Death considered her for a long moment. Somewhere in the queue, a soul sneezed out of pure habit.

"The pay is negotiable," he said.

"Negotiable in what currency?"

"Time, mostly. Any Tuesday you would have worked for me, you get to live instead."

Brenda thought about it. "And the scythe?"

"You don't touch the scythe. The scythe is ceremonial. Honestly, the scythe is mostly for me. It's a stress thing."

"Then I'll take Tuesdays," said Brenda. "And Thursdays. And you'll take your first vacation since Rome, because I've seen your calendar, and it's just the word 'no' repeated."

He hired her on the spot. He did not shake her hand — the living aren't supposed to touch him, it voids warranties — but he nodded, which, from Death, is a standing ovation.

The next Tuesday, Brenda cleared the backlog by six in the morning. She learned every soul's name, asked about their gardens, and told the frightened ones that the light was exactly what it looked like, which was home. The dead, it turns out, don't need a scythe. They need someone who has held a hand before.

So Death took his vacation. He went to a beach in a wide hat and lay there doing absolutely nothing while the tide came in and went out, in and out, like one great slow breath.

At 3:07 p.m. — he'd shifted to afternoons, for the light — a small child wandered over, studied him thoughtfully, and asked, "Are you the guy from the job board?"

"No," said Death, and meant it, in the best possible way. "I'm on vacation."
