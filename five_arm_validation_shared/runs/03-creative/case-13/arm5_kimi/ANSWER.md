# Help Wanted

The ad went up at 3:47 a.m. on a Tuesday, sandwiched between a listing for a line cook and one for a haunted-house actor, which Death found quietly hilarious, because he was alone, because he was always alone.

**PART-TIME REAPER,** it read. *Flexible hours. Cloak provided. Must be comfortable with silence. No benefits. Obviously.*

He had written it on his lunch break, which was not a lunch break, because he did not eat, because he had not stopped moving in four thousand years. The backlog had reached the ceiling of his office, which was not an office, which was a grey room that smelled faintly of mothballs and other people's last words. Somewhere in the pile was a Viking who had been pending since the ninth century and had started, impossibly, to complain.

So: the ad.

The applications arrived by raven, which was a problem, because he had not specified ravens, and the ravens knew it.

The first candidate was a young man named Chad who asked whether the scythe came in other colors, and whether the job was "like, mostly cardio," and whether there was a path to full-time, by which he seemed to mean immortality, which was not what that phrase meant, and Death made a note.

The second candidate livestreamed the interview. Death let the ravens handle it.

The third was a hospice administrator named Deborah who arrived eleven minutes early with a spreadsheet, a cardigan, and a Tupperware of casserole "for whoever looks thinnest," which, in the grey room, was a competitive category. She had read the job description twice. She had questions about the filing system. She had *opinions* about the filing system, which did not exist, which was, she pointed out gently, sort of the problem.

Death hired her on the spot, which was also how she scheduled things.

Her first week, the backlog dropped by a third. She did not stride; she *proceeded*. She did not loom; she knocked. She sent calendar invites — *Reaping, 4:00–4:15, location: your kitchen, dress casual* — and people, bewildered into politeness, mostly showed up on time. She labeled the Viking. She labeled the shelf she put him on. She left casseroles for the grieving with heating instructions written in a hand so steady it was itself a little frightening.

Death, who had not had an unscheduled hour since the Bronze Age, stood in the middle of his grey room and did not know what to do with his hands. He tried a hobby. The hobby was whittling. He whittled a small wooden scythe, looked at it for a long moment, and put it down.

Then, on the ninth day, he found the error.

It was a man named Arthur Bell, eighty-one, whose time had come on Thursday and who was, on Saturday, still breathing. Death pulled the file. Deborah's initials were on it, in her terrifyingly steady hand, next to a single annotation: *Deferred. See note.* The note said: *He is writing a letter. Let him finish.*

Death was not angry. Death was *professional*, and professionally, this was a breach, and he went to fix it himself, the way you redo a thing a new hire has done wrong, sighing the whole way.

Arthur Bell lived in a small yellow house with a garden he had clearly lost an argument with. Death arrived at dusk, cloaked, scythed, invisible, and stood in the corner of the study the way he had stood in ten thousand corners, and watched.

The old man wrote slowly. His hand shook. He crossed things out and started over, and the wastebasket filled with beginnings: *Dear Margaret,* and *Margaret,* and *My love, which I never once said right—*

Death had seen every kind of ending. He had rarely seen anyone work this hard at a beginning.

The letter took three days. Death told himself he was being efficient, that reaping a man mid-sentence created paperwork, that Deborah's filing system — which was excellent, which he had adopted entirely — had a category for this. On the third evening, Arthur Bell signed the letter, sealed it, addressed it, set it on the mantel, and looked, for the first time in days, finished. He died that night in his sleep, gently, and Death was there, and it was, by every metric Deborah had invented, a clean job.

Back in the grey room, Death did not write her up. He updated the manual instead. Page one now read: *Let them finish the letter.* He did not explain the addition and Deborah did not ask, though she did leave a casserole on his desk, which he did not eat, because he did not eat, but which he kept, because the grey room had never once, in four thousand years, been given anything.

The backlog grew again, as backlogs do. Death found he did not mind it the way he used to. It was no longer a wall; it was a queue, and a queue, Deborah had taught him, is just a line of people waiting to be treated properly.

He took the old ad down on a Sunday. The new one went up at 3:47 a.m. on a Tuesday, because some things should not change.

**PART-TIME REAPER, SECOND SHIFT,** it read. *Flexible hours. Cloak provided. Spreadsheet skills a plus. Must be comfortable with silence, and willing, occasionally, to wait.*

The ravens, sensing what was coming, began to gather.
