# Now Hiring: Part-Time Reaper

Death had not taken a vacation since 1348, and the paperwork from that year had only just cleared.

It wasn't the dying that exhausted him. Dying was the easy part — a door, a hand, a walk down a hallway that smelled faintly of the person's favorite decade. It was everything around the dying. The intake forms. The souls who wanted to argue. The souls who wanted to finish a crossword. The man in Tulsa who, upon being told it was time, had asked Death to wait while he watered his plants, and then, plant by plant, by fern and by ficus, had watered every plant he owned.

The backlog was now measured in geological eras. There were souls in the waiting room who had arrived expecting valet service and had instead watched four centuries of magazines pile up.

So on a Tuesday that felt like every other Tuesday, Death did something no Grim Reaper had done in the history of grim: he opened a hiring portal and typed.

**HELP WANTED — PART-TIME REAPER**

Duties: appear at scheduled deaths; escort souls to the waiting room; light filing. Occasional haunting of the wicked (optional, unpaid, honestly just for fun).

Requirements: own scythe (cloak provided; one size; it's a cloak). Punctuality — time is a construct, but be punctual about it. Must be comfortable with silence, with crying relatives, and with the phrase "you're not my real reaper."

No experience necessary. Experience is unavoidable anyway.

Pay: negotiable. Hours: flexible. Mortality: not required, not disqualifying.

Apply in person. Do not haunt the office. The office is not haunted. The office is staffed.

The applicants came in droves, which was the problem. Most of them were ghosts, and ghosts, in Death's professional opinion, were clients, not contractors. Hiring one would be like the ocean taking swimming lessons from a puddle.

There was a life coach named Chad who wanted to rebrand the position. "I don't see endings," Chad said, steepling his fingers. "I see transitions. I'd call it a Life Continuation Specialist role, and every soul gets a workbook."

Death slid the workbook back across the desk. "The souls are dead, Chad. They've done enough work."

There was a vampire, which was a conflict of interest. There was a teenager who wanted the job "for, like, personal reasons," which Death found chilling in the wrong way. There was a middle manager who tried to synergize the afterlife. There was a man who had misunderstood and was applying to die, and Death gently walked him back to the living world and told him to drink some water.

Then a cat came in. Death had a soft spot for cats, who had been doing the work informally for millennia — every cat on earth was already a part-time reaper, knocking glasses off counters and staring at their owners with professional detachment. But the cat refused to wear the cloak, napped through the skills assessment, and left when the treats ran out. Death marked it *strong candidate, will not commit to weekends*, which was also the review his own last performance had earned.

On Thursday, a woman came in with a rolling walker that had tennis balls on the back legs. She was seventy-four, retired after forty years in hospice care, and she did not so much as glance at the scythe display.

"You're the one with the backlog," she said.

"I'm the one with everything," Death said. "That's the problem."

"Doris." She shook his hand. Her grip was firm; her hand was warm, which always startled him a little, like a letter arriving from another century. "I've been doing your job for free since 1971. Holding hands. Telling people it's all right. Sitting with the ones who had nobody. You do the door. I do the hallway."

Death looked at her file, which had appeared on his desk the way files did when the universe wanted something. It was thick. It was very thick. It was the thickest file he had ever seen for someone still technically alive, and it was full of hallway.

"Conditions," Doris said, before he could speak. "No nights. No tragedies before nine in the morning — people deserve to wake up first. I keep my bingo Thursdays. And I don't carry the scythe. I've got the walker. It's faster than it looks."

"The scythe is ceremonial," Death said. "Mostly."

"Then it can lean against a wall, mostly."

He hired her on the spot, which for Death meant he stopped time for eleven seconds and shook her hand again.

Her first weekend, Death did something unprecedented. He turned on an out-of-office message — *Gone fishing. If you are reading this, you are either dead or a soul, and either way, Doris has you* — and he went to the beach.

He wore a Hawaiian shirt over the robe. He got sand in the hood. He ate something called a fish taco and understood why humans put up with everything else. The ocean did not care about his job. The ocean, he reflected, was the only coworker he'd ever had who never once asked him to hold a soul's luggage.

His phone buzzed once, near sunset. A photo from Doris: a hospital corridor, a folding table with two cups of tea, and an elderly man in a gown laughing at something she'd said. The caption read: *Got it. Nice man from Ohio. He says hi, and also that your waiting room needs better magazines.*

Death looked at it for a long moment. Then he turned the phone off, because the dead could wait — for once, they would, and they were in good hands, and the hands were warm.

Somewhere, a man from Ohio crossed over with the distinct impression that he'd been met at the door by somebody's grandmother, and that it was, all things considered, a fine way to go.
