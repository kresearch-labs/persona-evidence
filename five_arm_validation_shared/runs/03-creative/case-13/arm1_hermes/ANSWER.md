# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-13

## Now Hiring: Assistant to the Regional Reaper

The ad appeared at midnight on a corkboard that doesn't exist, in a café that's only there when you need it, next to the lost-cat flyers:

**PART-TIME REAPER (ENTRY LEVEL)**
*The Department of Transition seeks a detail-oriented individual for a part-time position. Duties: accompany souls on their final commute; maintain the Schedule; no scythe provided, bring your own energy. Flexible hours — yours are literally flexible. Must be comfortable with people at their most honest. Uniform provided (you'll know). Rate: it's the one question everyone gets the answer to eventually.*

Death — Death himself, robes, hourglass, the whole ceremonial kit — had burned out. Millennia of the same shift. The paperwork alone had become Sisyphean: every death a form, every form a soul, every soul a story he no longer had the stamina to hear properly. He'd started skimming. When Death skims, people notice — a soul goes through the door holding the wrong memories, someone gets a goodbye they didn't earn. He'd had *complaints*. From the living. There were emails now. Death had an inbox, and the inbox had a flag he didn't know how to lower.

So: a part-timer. Someone human. Death had reviewed the literature (job boards, mostly) and concluded that what the department lacked was a *junior* — a person who still found endings interesting.

The applicant pool was, characteristically, the recently grieving. The job posting read like a personal ad to exactly those people.

He hired three, on probation.

**Marisol**, fifty-eight, hospice nurse for thirty years. "I've been doing this job unpaid since I was nineteen," she told him at the interview, which was conducted in a laundromat at 3 a.m. because Death had gotten romantic about the venue. "The difference between me and you is the paperwork."

She was right, and she was fast. Marisol's gift — the thing the hourglass confirmed on her first shift — was *easing*. Souls that came to her went quietly, mid-sentence almost, the way you leave a party when you're tired and the host has already said goodbye. Hospitals in her zone started reporting something the administrators called "the anomalous peacefulness" and the nurses called "a good night."

**Devon**, twenty-six, hospice volunteer, video-game reflexes. "I'm good at escorts," he said, which Death wrote down verbatim and later regretted. Devon's gift was *timing* — he arrived not a moment before it was asked of him and never after. He'd sit in the waiting room of a life like a co-pilot, reading the gauges nobody else could see, and only stand up when the soul did. He got lost twice the first week (the between-places are not wayfinding-friendly), showed up at a birth by mistake, stayed to hold the baby's door anyway. "Wrong direction," Death told him. "But good instinct."

**Prue**, seventy-one, retired mail carrier. The one nobody expected. Marisol eased, Devon timed — Dot *negotiated*. First shift, she met a terrified soul in a hospital stairwell, a man who'd spent his whole life bargaining, and instead of the robe-and-hush she sat down on the step with him like they were waiting for a bus and said, "So what are you hoping I'll say?" and they talked for an hour, and Death, watching from the doorway in violation of his own manual, understood he had hired something the Department had never had:

A listener with tenure.

The burnout didn't vanish; it *distributed*. Death took the catastrophic shifts back — the plagues of one person's private world, the sudden and unfair, which needed the weight of the office — and the part-timers took the long, quiet, ordinary passings that had been drowning him. On his first free weekend in an eon, Death went fishing in a dimension with no fish and no need for him, and slept, insofar as it is fair to say Death sleeps, like the dead.

The corkboard posting is still up. It says, under the benefits section, in handwriting like a signed sigh:

**"The hours are when people need you. The pay is that you'll understand everything, eventually, including why you took this job. Marisol, Devon, and Dot: your probation is concluded. I never doubted. — D."**

And below that, added later in ballpoint, in Marisol's hand:

*"The uniform is a cardigan. He wasn't kidding. — M."*
