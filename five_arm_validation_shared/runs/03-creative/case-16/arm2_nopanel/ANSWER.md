# Salt and Ink

My great-grandfather Tomás waited until the priest had gone before he told me, as if the Church might want a cut.

"Come closer." His hand on my wrist was light as paper. "The family story is that we came from Canary farmers. It is a good story. It is not ours. We are Morgan's — blood of Henry Morgan, who burned Panama and governed Jamaica with a hangover and a hangman's patience." He pressed a bundle of oilcloth into my hands, heavier than it looked, smelling of forty years of a drawer. "His diary. It came down the family like a debt. Now it is yours, because you are the only one who ever asked about the sea."

He died before dawn with the window open, listening to a harbor that was not there.

I expected a pirate's log — powder, plunder, prayers for wind. What I found was an accountant's hand: cramped, patient, mostly weather. Morgan, it turned out, kept books the way other men kept grudges. There were pages on the raid on Puerto del Príncipe in the year '68, on the division of a Spanish prize, on a mule that bit him in Camagüey and a fever that nearly finished him in the hills. Then, on a page dated some days later, this:

"We stood east along the coast to a great harbour the Indians call Guantánamo, which the Admiral Columbus named Puerto Grande. Deep as a cathedral and calm in all winds. There we careened the ship, and under a hook of land between the mangroves I buried that which I would not share with crews or governors. Let it sleep till a Morgan has need of it."

Folded into the binding was a chart, brown as toast, with a coastline I did not know and an X drawn in a bay on Cuba's far east — in what is now Guantánamo Province. The X sat on a hook of land inside the bay's western shore, a finger of green pointing at deep water.

It took me a year to get there. Havana first, then Santiago, then a bus east through country so dry it rattled — cactus where I had expected palms, flamingos standing in the salt flats like a rumor. In Guantánamo city an old man studied my map for a long time and said only: "The bay is deep. The land remembers." In Caimanera, a fisherwoman named Yusimí agreed to take me along the western shore, past the fence line of the naval base, its watchtowers pale as bone across the water.

"American ground," she said, nodding at it. "But the hook is ours. Nobody goes there. The mangroves eat boats."

The mangroves did try. We grounded twice, waded the last stretch, and came out on a spit of shell and sand where sea-grape trees bent inland under a century of hurricanes. The chart had shown a notch in the hook, and the notch was still there, and at its head stood a single lignum vitae tree, older than any fence in the world.

The X, I understood then, had never been a place. It was a set of instructions: stand at the notch, face the deep water, walk twenty paces toward the tree.

I dug with a shovel borrowed from Yusimí and a trowel I had carried across an ocean. Two feet down the sand turned to clay; three feet, to a floor of coral rubble that rang like crockery. I was ready to quit when the trowel struck wood — black, dense, preserved by its own tar. A chest the size of a suitcase, iron-hooped, its lock long since rusted into decoration.

Inside: pieces of eight, a dozen of them, dull as river stones. One small bar of silver, stamped with a Spanish mark. And a letter, folded in oilcloth, the ink faded to the color of weak tea but the hand unmistakably the diary's hand.

"To the child of my blood, whosoever digs this up," it began. "If you read this, then the sea has kept my books, and you are proof that I end in heirs and not in earth. Take the silver; it is yours. But leave the greater part, and add to it if you can, for a treasure that is found is worth less than a treasure that is waited for. — H.M., Port Royal, in the year 1686."

At the bottom of the chest, under the coins, was a second paper — not old. Typed, brittle, in Spanish.

"Found this place, 12 March 1958. Took one coin to believe it. Put back two. I leave it for whoever needs it more than I did. — Tomás Morgan Salgado, your great-grandfather, who learned the story too late to thank him properly."

I sat under the lignum vitae tree and laughed until Yusimí went to check the boat.

Here is what I did, and you may call it foolish. I took the two letters and one coin, and I left two of mine in their place, which comes to the same arithmetic. I wrapped the chest in its tar and its clay again and covered it over, and in the diary, beneath Morgan's entry, I wrote in my own careful hand:

"Found it, and left it sleeping. The story is the treasure; the coins are only the paper it came wrapped in. — Your descendant, who was told the story just in time."

On the boat back the bay lay flat and shining, deep as a cathedral, exactly as promised. Somewhere under that water, in the sand that slid into the sea when Port Royal sank, Henry Morgan's true grave is still keeping its own appointment. I like to think the old accountant would approve of the balance: two coins out, two coins in, one family story carried forward at interest.
