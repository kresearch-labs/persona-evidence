## The diaries of H. Morgan, private citizen

Great-grandfather Silas died the way he'd lived: withholding the punchline until the last possible moment. Ninety-four years old, six of us at the bedside, and with his final breath he opened one eye and said, "Check the ledger, girl. Page forty. You're richer than you think and poorer than you deserve," and was gone before anyone could ask which of those clauses was the joke.

The ledger was real. So was page forty. So, folded into the binding with a hand-drawn chart that smelled of salt and drama, was a map of a bay in Guantanamo Province, Cuba, with an X inked in the shallows like a man who'd been keeping a secret for a very long time and needed, at ninety-four, to set it down.

The diary underneath it was not, as the family legend insisted, written by Henry Morgan the pirate. It was written by a clerk. Enrique Morgan, 1671, literate, underpaid, employed as a scribe by a man who owed back wages to half the Antilles. Enrique had done what underpaid clerks with access to ledgers have always done: he'd noted the numbers, noted the shipping lanes, and quietly worked out where a certain Brethren captain had cached a prize that never made it into any report — a shipment of church silver taken under letters of marque and then "lost to weather," lost, in this case, to a shallow bay and a clerk's excellent memory for payrolls.

Enrique never went. That was the part that stopped me on the porch of the family home with the ledger in my lap: he'd drawn the map, marked the bay, written *when the family has need* — and then, for eleven generations, we'd had need and never gone. Wars, migrations, marriages, a hurricane in 1932 that took the eastern branch of the family and the map's left margin with it. The X survived. The family didn't go. Silas — my grandfather's grandfather, the last one who'd read the whole diary — went once, in 1954, got as far as a dock in Santiago with a permit he couldn't afford to renew, and came home. "The bay keeps what the bay keeps," he wrote in the margin, and underlined it twice like a coward.

I went in March, with a salvage-diving license, a borrowed boat, and my cousin Tomas, who believes in money the way other people believe in God.

The bay was exactly as the chart promised: shallow, green, guarded by a headland shaped like a sleeping dog. We found the wreck on the second day — not a grand galleon, just a small coastal trader, hull collapsed into a fan of old timber — and under the ballast line, exactly where a clerk's careful arithmetic said it would be, a chest.

Not treasure-movie chest. Document chest. Sealed with wax, wrapped in oilcloth, and inside — I am writing this with the inventory list in front of me because I still don't believe it — correspondence. The prize itself, the church silver, had been sold generations ago by the Brethren's quartermaster to pay a debt (Enrique's last letter, furious, beautiful, four pages about the quartermaster). What the chest held was what Enrique had gathered to *prove* it: manifests, letters, a receipt with a seal — the paper trail of a stolen fortune.

And one more letter, unsealed, addressed in different ink: *"To the one who reads this: the silver was spent before you were born, as silver is. What I leave you is the proof that we were honest men in a dishonest trade, and knew the difference. That is the inheritance. Enrique Morgan."*

Tomas was devastated. Silver! Gold! The word "emerald" appears nowhere!

I sat on the boat's rail with four hundred years of paper in my lap, understanding my great-grandfather completely. Silas hadn't sent us after treasure. He'd sent us after the *ledger* — the whole ledger, the family ledger, the one Enrique started when he decided that what his descendants would need someday wasn't coins. It was the knowledge of where we came from: not pirates. Clerks. The people who keep the true count while everyone else is drunk on legend.

The chest is in the national archive now, on loan — Tomas handled the paperwork and grudgingly admits the correspondence made the family "mildly famous," which for Tomas is a form of currency. And page forty of Silas's ledger has a new entry, in my handwriting, dated this year:

*Went. Found it. It was never the silver.*

*The bay kept what the bay kept. Turns out that was us.*
