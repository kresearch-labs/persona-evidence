# The Fine Print

The first image the Vessh sent was a photograph of their ambassador standing beside a mountain. It took the analysts six months to agree that the mountain was the ambassador.

Dr. Amara Osei had spent twenty years on the signal — prime sequences, then grammar, then the slow revelation of physics embedded in the grammar — and she thought she was ready. She practiced the greeting in the mirror of the shuttle: *We come in peace, and we come as equals.* The words felt large in her mouth. She liked that.

Then the shuttle docked at the Conclave station, and the window filled with a wall of grey flesh four hundred meters tall, and the wall turned out to be an ankle.

They were all like that. The Vessh, basalt-skinned, thinking in low frequencies that made the deck hum. The Oruun, columns of luminous gas held in magnetic harnesses, who communicated by aurora. The Keth'mari, crystalline, three hundred meters of faceted silence. Forty-one intelligent species catalogued across the galaxy, and every one of them was a kaiju. Humanity was the forty-second, and the only one you could lose in a couch.

Ambassador Vessh — the one from the photograph — knelt to address their delegation, and his eye filled the observation blister like a rising moon.

"Small ones," he said, through the translator Amara had built. "The Conclave welcomes you. Tomorrow you will declare your measure, as all species do, so that we may know what you are."

"What does that mean, exactly?" Amara asked.

"Say what you are. We will believe you."

That night, the human delegation argued in their quarters — a broom closet by station standards, though it had been built to their scale with eerie precision. The military attaché wanted to emphasize the nuclear arsenal. The scientists wanted to emphasize the science. Amara listened to them all and heard the same thing underneath: the fear that to forty-one species of giants, humanity looked less like a sibling and more like an infestation.

She had come to make them equals. She lay awake rehearsing declarations, and each one was bigger than the last.

The Conclave chamber was a canyon. Forty-one giants ringed it, and the human podium sat on a floating platform at its center, a mote. Amara stood at the podium with her prepared text and looked up at the moon-eyed ambassador, and something in her — twenty years of work, the whole proud species behind her — refused to be small.

"We are humanity," she said. "We split the atom. We move mountains. We have remade our world."

A sound went through the chamber like tectonic plates shifting. The Keth'mari resonated, a chord she felt in her teeth. The Oruun flickered through colors her translator rendered as: *alarm.*

Ambassador Vessh leaned closer, and the platform swayed in his breath. "You split atoms," he repeated. "You move mountains. You remake worlds." A pause the length of a weather system. "These are claims of the great measure. By Conclave law, such claims must be proven — or the claimant's world hosts a Proving, so that all may see."

Amara knew, with cold certainty, what a Proving between kaiju would do to a planet. They had dueled before; the station's archives were full of the craters. On Earth it would not be a duel. It would be an extinction with an audience.

The military attaché was saying something urgent in her earpiece. She muted it.

She thought of her prepared text. She thought of the mirror, and the large words, and how good they had felt.

"I must correct my declaration," she said. "I spoke the way a small creature speaks when it is afraid of being stepped on. Here is our true measure. We are small. Everything we have ever built would fit between your thumb and finger. We split the atom the way a child splits a seed — to see inside it. We are afraid most of the time, and we are curious all of the time, and we have come very far to meet you, and we are asking you not to step on us."

Silence. The Oruun dimmed. Amara stood in the center of the canyon with her career and her species' dignity in ruins around her, and felt, strangely, taller.

Then Ambassador Vessh did something the archives had no record of. He lay down — the whole mountain of him — until his eye was level with the platform, a hundred meters of iris, patient as geology.

"Small one," he said, and the translator rendered his tone as something like wonder. "Do you know what you are? You are the first voice we have ever heard from beneath our feet."

The Keth'mari chimed, and this time the chord resolved into words: *We cannot see the small. Our senses are tectonic. The fine structure of the universe is invisible to us — the small machines that fail, the small lives that blight our crops, the small print in which the universe writes its laws. We have been alone up here, reading only the headlines.*

"We have needed," said Ambassador Vessh, "someone to read the fine print."

The vote took eleven minutes. Humanity entered the Conclave not as a protected curiosity — though protection was granted, unanimously, the way one protects something irreplaceable — but as the Conclave's first small eyes. The galaxy, it turned out, had been waiting forty-one species for someone who could see the world at its actual resolution.

Afterward, on the platform, the military attaché asked Amara what would happen to her career. She said she didn't know, and discovered she didn't much care.

Ambassador Vessh extended one claw toward the platform, a grey arch the size of a cathedral door, and stopped it a careful hundred meters away — the closest thing to a handshake the galaxy could manage.

Amara reached out her hand anyway, into the empty air between them, and held it there.
