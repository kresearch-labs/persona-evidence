# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-10

## Small World

The day humanity got its first alien call, I was the junior analyst on shift at the Deep Sky Array, which is why the transcript has my initials on the opening line and my resignation in the footnotes.

The signal came from Wolf-359's outer system. Clean, deliberate, patient: primes, then hydrogen, then — unmistakably — a recording of *us*, our own first broadcast, returned with corrections. First contact. The room stood up. Somebody dropped a thermos that we, as a civilization, still owe somebody for.

Their ship entered cislunar space three months later, and this is where the record becomes difficult for outsiders.

It was four hundred meters tall. It walked — *walked* — out of a fold in space, planting each foot on the curvature of space-time itself, and when it sat down on the Moon it did so cross-legged, and the Sea of Tranquility got a new naming convention. Its body was obsidian and storm-light. Its voice arrived as aurora over every hemisphere at once, in every language, in the accent of your childhood.

"Greetings, small people," it said. "We have come to trade."

The world's xenolinguists, in that moment, achieved the professional fulfillment of their lives and the personal crisis of their decade, because the Being — they called themselves the Tall — were, unmistakably, *people*. Curious. Wry. Capable of small talk. Also three hundred meters minimum, ALSO capable of growing a new limb made of rain, of singing a moon's orbit a half-degree higher, of tectonic-speak — all of it riding on top of technology so far past ours that their "radio" was a hand gesture. (A gesture they made with a hand the size of Finland. We adapted.)

The second ship brought the diplomat-kaiju, who was *small* for them — a mere three hundred and twelve meters — and carried, in a fold of storm-light, our protocol teams up to the Lunar Table. What we learned in that first session reordered every textbook:

Intelligence, everywhere we've looked since, runs to scale. Big thinkers. Very big. The universe's great minds grow like sequoias and speak in weather. And the thing about being a kaiju with dominion over storms and orbits and geology is that your problems are *scaled too*. The We-of-the-Wind could not build a microscope. Its hands did not permit subtlety; its senses, tuned to atmospheres, had never once resolved anything smaller than a whale. Every civilization we've met since shares this: they are genius at the grand and blind at the small. None of them had ever sequenced a genome. None had ever *needed* to. Their technologies grew like temples — enormous, elegant, and scaled to hands that could hold hurricanes.

Which made us — soft, tiny, jumpy, soup-making us — the smallest intelligence anyone had ever met.

The trade, then, wrote itself, and it is why the galaxy works now. We are their microscopes, their hands for delicate work, their makers of the very small. They are our scale, our reach, our weather-lawyers and orbital engineers. The first treaty — humanity's opening offer, drafted at 4 a.m. by a committee that included a particle physicist and a gardener — was two lines:

*You bring the sky. We bring the small. Neither of us has ever seen what we're about to see.*

The first product of the alliance is something the We had never possessed: a lens. Not a magnifying glass — a *universe key*. Our instruments, their power: the We watched, for the first time, the machinery inside their own storm-limbs. They watched their children being born at cellular scale, in real time, through glasses we ground for them over four patient months. There is a recording of the first Elder-We to see a cell. It is the sound a mountain makes when it weeps, and it is in the archive.

And they gave us the sky in ways we'd only drawn. Orbital adjustments with a fingertip. Comets herded for water. A hurricane that was threatening Manila was not stopped — it was *talked down*, gentled over three days by a Being who finds barometry soothing, into a light rain that filled the reservoirs.

There have been misunderstandings. There is a whole field now, Small-Large Protocol, teaching both sides. The We do not shake hands; their greeting is a thunderclap at a respectful distance. We do not sit on planets; we're allowed to be *carried*, which nobody who has experienced being carried in a cupped mountain-range of palm will ever describe correctly.

My favorite artifact of the era is not in any treaty. It is the question the first We asked our first delegation, with what our linguists translate as genuine worry:

"Your scale frightens us," it said. "Everything you build is so *tiny*. How do you keep from losing them?"

And our delegation chief — may her name outlive the mountains — held up a pocket watch and said:

"We lose things all the time. That's why we write. Trade you: we'll teach you to write small, and you teach us to say it loud enough."

The We consider that sentence scripture now. It's carved on their side of the Moon, in letters you can read from the ground, next to the smaller inscription — ours — that says the same thing in smaller words.
