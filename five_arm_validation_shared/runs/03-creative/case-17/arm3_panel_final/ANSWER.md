# The Light at Carrick

The light at Carrick turns all night, every night, and the town sleeps with its curtains open.

I arrived in March off the morning bus, with a suitcase and a letter appointing me assistant keeper. I had my reasons for wanting a posting at the edge of a map. Everyone who takes a light does. Old Hewitt met me at the foot of the tower, a small man gone the colour of driftwood, and shook my hand like he was measuring it for rope.

That night he walked me up the hundred and nine steps while the lamp roared behind its lens. He laid his palm on the housing the way you'd steady a horse.

"The light can never go out," he said. "Ships don't need us. Haven't in quite some time. It's the people here on land who'll suffer if that light ever goes out."

"What would they suffer?" I asked.

"Nothing," he said, "so long as it burns." And that was the whole of my training.

I learned the town before I learned the lamp. One street, a harbour wall, a church. The churchyard was full of stones and not one of them carried a name — only dates worn to shallow shadows, as if the rain had taken the words and left the arithmetic. The vicar blamed the weather. The rain at Carrick doesn't fall upward, but I was new.

Nobody locked a door. Nobody sang the sad songs at the Anchor; the landlord had a rule about it, though he'd never had cause to enforce it in living memory. Funerals were quick, tidy affairs where people smiled at the widow and meant it. There was one woman on the front, Mrs. Calloway, who kept her curtains drawn day and dark, and she was the only soul in Carrick who wept. Her boy had drowned off the pier years before. The town brought her casseroles and spoke to her gently, the way you speak to someone doing a hard job you're relieved is not yours.

There was a box bolted to the tower door, with a slot in the lid, like a letterbox. On my rounds I'd find it filled: a wedding ring, a curl of baby hair, half a photograph, a child's drawing of a dog. Hewitt burned them in the lamp — not beside it, in it, lifted on a little brass shovel into the flame itself. The flame took what it wanted and left the metal, and the smoke went out to sea whatever the wind was doing. He kept a ledger of it. Names in one column, in pencil, and what was burned in the other. The oldest pages were faint, and getting fainter, as if the book were being let go of a word at a time.

I asked him once what had happened before. He was quiet on the stairs a long while.

"It went out in my grandfather's day," he said. "One night. The town remembered everything it had ever given us. In the morning they built the diesel house and the battery store, and made the rule." He looked at me then, and his eyes were the grey of the ledger. "The rule is: it can never go out."

It went out in November.

The gale came up the coast after dark and took the power line at half past one. The diesel caught and ran, and Hewitt went up ahead of me with the oil, and on the ninety-first step he sat down and didn't get up. He was grey, and one hand was closed over his chest, and his mouth was moving. I had two things in front of me and time enough to hold one of them.

I stepped over him.

In the lamp room, by torchlight, I trimmed and primed and lit, and the lens began to turn. Eleven minutes, maybe twelve, of dark. From the gallery I saw the town.

Every door on the front stood open. People came out into the street in their nightclothes, unhurried, like sleepwalkers called by name, and stood facing the sea. And they began to say names. A low sound, hundreds of names, ebbing and flowing like tide. Some of them I knew from the faint pages of the ledger. Some I knew from no page at all. Mrs. Calloway stood on her step with her curtains open for the first time in years, and she was not weeping; she was smiling, saying a name over and over, like a bell.

At the end of the pier stood a young man in a wet sweater, looking back at the town.

The beam caught him when it came round. He was gone between one turn and the next. Mrs. Calloway was holding a wool cap then, wringing wet, and she folded it against her chest, and went in, and drew her curtains.

The town went back into its houses like water finding its level. I carried Hewitt down the ninety-one steps. He was cold in the way the stones were cold. We buried him on the Thursday, and nobody wept, and I wrote his name in the ledger myself, in pencil.

A man from the far street had come out in the dark and could not stop remembering. The town called him dark-touched and were kind to him, and kept their curtains open.

I keep the light now. I burn what the box brings me. In my coat there is a ring that was my mother's, and I came to Carrick in March for a reason I wrote in my diary; I have read the entry twice, and the ink is going grey, and I no longer feel the weight of it. That was the whole point of coming, I think.

Some nights I take the ring to the top of the tower. Some nights I put it back.

The light turns. The town sleeps with its curtains open, faces to the sea, giving. And the sea is bright, some nights, all the way to the horizon — bright with everything we have ever sent it.

Ships don't need us. I trim the wick anyway.
