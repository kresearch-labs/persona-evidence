# The Ledger

I took the light from a man named Ellery Crane, who had taken it from his mother, who had taken it from hers. He was eighty-one the autumn he handed me the keys, and the first thing he showed me was not the lamp. It was the ledger — a fat, salt-swollen book going back to 1889, one line for every night the light had burned.

"No gaps," he said. "Not one."

I asked him the obvious question. The tower had been automated in the seventies; the Coast Guard had decommissioned it; the harbor had maybe three working boats left, all of them with radar, GPS, and no need of a lighthouse keeper. I had come out of curiosity as much as anything, and I said so. Ellery didn't take offense. He took me up to the gallery instead, and we stood in the wind while the beam swung out over the water and came around, slow as a lighthouse beam always does, and crossed the town.

"The light can never go out," he explained. "Ships don't need us. Haven't in quite some time. It's the people here on land who'll suffer if that light ever goes out."

I watched the beam pass over Harbor Row, over the church, over the graveyard on the point. For a half-second the whole town stood up out of the dark, white and still as a photograph, and then the beam went on to the sea and the town sank back.

"What happens if it does?" I asked.

He was quiet long enough that I thought he hadn't heard. Then: "It dreams," he said. "And when it dreams, it calls. And when it calls, people answer." He tapped the ledger. "That's what the book is for. Not ships. Them."

I should explain what "it" is, and I can't, not the way Ellery could, because he had things told to him and I only have things I've seen. The founding families drowned something in the harbor in 1889 — that part is in the town archive, in nicer language. A debt, a bargain, a thing that came up the bay during a red tide and made the fish float belly-up for a mile. The old stories call it the Neighbor. The ledger's first page calls it nothing at all. It just begins, in a hand very like Ellery's grandmother's: *Lit at dusk. Counted the town. All present.*

Counted. That is the word they used, all the way down the book, in every hand. *Counted the town. All present.* Some nights: *All present but one. Mrs. Tait gone in the night. Counted her at the waterline. She was smiling.*

I thought it was folklore, as any sensible person would. I stayed anyway — the pay was nothing but the house came with it, and I liked the climbing, the brass, the smell of oil and sea. Ellery stayed on in the cottage below until his heart gave out in March, and then I kept the light alone, and I kept the ledger, because it seemed rude not to.

Here is what I know now that I did not believe then.

The beam is not for finding. It is for counting. Every night it sweeps the water, and then it sweeps the town, and in the half-second the light lies across Harbor Row, everyone in it stops — mid-step, mid-word, mid-sentence — and faces the tower. You can't see it from inside the beam. I've stood on Harbor Row myself, on a dare I gave myself, and all I felt was a moment of blankness, like the gap between radio stations. But from the gallery I've watched a whole street pause and turn, all at once, like a field of flowers finding the sun. Then the beam moves on and they go back to their lives, having lost nothing but a half-second they never knew they'd spent.

It isn't only the living it counts. The graveyard on the point gets its half-second too. I've watched the stones stand bright and attentive in the beam, and I have never once, in six years, seen a stone out of line.

The light keeps it dreaming, Ellery told me the week before he died. Not asleep — he was firm about that; asleep was a different and worse thing — but dreaming, the way an old man dozes in a chair with one eye on the door. The beam is the turning of a page it has agreed to hear forever: the same town, the same harbor, the same little congregation of warm lives, presented nightly, in order, all present. As long as the story is told to it, it stays in its chair. It is when the story stops — when the page goes dark mid-sentence — that it wakes enough to wonder what became of the rest of the tale. And it gets up to look for itself.

The storm came in November, my third winter. The sea went over the seawall in slabs, and the power failed at 11:40, and the backup generator — Ellery's generator, maintained like a heart — coughed twice and died with salt water in its intake. I was in the lamp room with a flashlight in my teeth and my hands full of a machine that did not want to live, and I heard, under the wind, the sound of the town.

Not screaming. Nothing so useful as screaming. It was a sound like a congregation murmuring down an aisle — hundreds of people saying one thing, softly, in unison, and the thing was my name. Not my name; it only sounded like my name. It was the Neighbor's name for me, and it was being spoken by every throat in Harbor Row at once, and they were all walking. I could hear the footsteps beneath the murmur, hundreds of them, unhurried, wet.

The light came back at 11:47. Seven minutes. I got the beam up and swept it around and caught them — all of them, halfway down the beach and standing in the shallows and out on the rocks beyond the point where no one ever goes, a whole town in its nightclothes, ankle-deep to waist-deep in a sea that was receiving them politely, one by one, like a host at a door.

The beam crossed them and they stopped. All present. They turned around and came home, and in the morning the town went to work as if nothing had happened, because for them nothing had — seven minutes of blankness, the gap between radio stations. But the ledger made me write it down, and I wrote: *Light out 7 min. Counted the town. All present but three.* And I walked the beach at dawn and found three lines of footprints going out, and none coming back, and at the end of each line, in the wet sand, a place where someone had sat down — comfortably, the way you sit in a chair — before the sea closed over the seat of it like a lid.

Ellery's mother's hand appears in the ledger twice in eighty years with that same line: *All present but three.* Ellery's hand, once. Mine, once. I understand now why the book is fat, and why no keeper ever let a night go unlit, and why Ellery never slept more than two hours at a stretch, and why, when I asked him once what he was afraid of, he said: "Not the dark. The dark is only where the story stops."

The light can never go out. I am the one who keeps it that way, and I have begun to understand, in my body, in the ache behind my eyes at 3 a.m., what Ellery would not say plainly: that the counting is not for our benefit. That the ledger is not a record but a menu, kept accurate, itemized, all present — and that every night we line up in the beam and turn our faces to the tower, we are not being protected.

We are being shown.
