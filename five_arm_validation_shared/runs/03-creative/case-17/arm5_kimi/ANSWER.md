# The Keeper's Hours

The light went around every four seconds, and every four seconds it swept the town — chapel roof, harbor wall, the rows of sleeping windows — like a hand counting children in their beds.

I came home that October because my father had started forgetting things. He forgot to eat. He forgot that my mother's name was no longer spoken in that house. He never once forgot the lamp.

"Ships don't need us," he told me the night I climbed the tower with him. He was winding the mechanism the way he had done for forty years, his hands moving without his attention. "Haven't in quite some time. It's the people here on land who'll suffer if that light ever goes out."

I laughed at him. I had been away eleven years, long enough to become someone who laughs. There hadn't been a wreck on that coast since before I was born; the fishing fleet navigated by satellite now, and the lighthouse was a line item in a heritage budget. I told him so.

He didn't argue. He just watched the beam go around, and I noticed he counted under his breath — four seconds out, four seconds back — like a man timing contractions.

He died that winter, in his chair, facing the window the light crossed. The town buried him on the hill where the beam would pass over his grave. Everyone came. Everyone already seemed to know what I didn't: that the job was mine now, not by hiring but by blood, the way it had been his, and his mother's before him. The council sent a letter that read less like an offer than a reminder.

I took it because the pay was real and the work was nothing. Wind the mechanism. Tend the lamp. Keep the log. The log was the strange part — a shelf of ledgers going back a hundred and sixty years, and every entry, in every hand, ended with the same line: *The light did not go out.*

No entry ever said anything else.

The first night I understood, I had been keeper for five weeks.

It was a storm, the kind that comes in off the water like it has been personally offended. I was in the watch room at two in the morning when the mechanism — which had not failed since my grandfather's day — stuttered. The beam shuddered. And the light went out.

The dark that came in was not ordinary dark. It had weight. It pressed against the glass of the lamp room like deep water, and in the seconds before I got the backup burning, I heard the town below me make a sound — every dog, every gull, every sleeping person, one long inhalation, all at once, like the whole town had been held underwater and was waiting to find out if it would be let up.

I got the lamp lit. The beam went around. Chapel roof. Harbor wall. Sleeping windows.

In the morning the harbor was full of dead fish, floating silver-belly-up in a ring around the town, every one of them facing the shore. Mrs. Abernathy at the store told me her mother used to say the fish come to look when the light blinks. To see if it's really gone.

I opened the oldest ledger then, the one from the beginning, the one I had been avoiding because the handwriting was hard and the ink was brown. The first keeper had not written *The light did not go out* at the end of his first entry. He had written a whole page, and someone, later, had pasted paper over it. But paper is thin, and lamps are bright, and I held the page up to the light and read what was underneath.

*It is not a light for ships. It never was. We built it after the winter the sea gave our dead back. They came up out of the water with the fog and stood outside the houses of the people who had let them drown — and God forgive us, we had let them drown, we had chosen who the boats could carry — and they knocked, and if you opened the door they wore the face you remembered, and if you didn't they wore it anyway, behind your eyes, until you opened it. The light does not keep them away. Nothing keeps them away. The light keeps them remembering that they are dead. In the dark, they forget. In the dark, they think they live here still.*

I have been the keeper for nine years now. I wind the mechanism. I tend the lamp. I end every log entry the way my father did, and his mother, and all of them back to the first: *The light did not go out.*

Sometimes, on still nights, I watch the town while the beam goes around — chapel roof, harbor wall, sleeping windows — and I think about what my father said, and I understand the part he never said. The suffering would not be the town's alone. It would be mine, the keeper's, the one who let it happen. The dead don't blame the sea. They blame the light going out, and the hand that let it.

Last week a young man from the council came up the tower, talking about automation, about cost, about how ships don't need us. I let him finish. Then I told him what my father told me, and I watched him laugh the way I once laughed, and I did not argue.

The light went around behind me. Four seconds out. Four seconds back. Counting.
