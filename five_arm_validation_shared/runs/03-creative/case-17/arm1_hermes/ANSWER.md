# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-17

## The Light of Saltern Point

Every lighthouse on the coast went automatic in my grandfather's time, and the ones after that went dark entirely. Saltern Point held out longest, and then Saltern Point, too, was decommissioned — formally, with a plaque and a man from the Maritime Authority and a checklist.

And then the man from the Authority left, and my grandfather climbed the tower anyway, and turned the light on anyway, and has turned it on every night for eleven years since. The Authority looks away. Towns have arrangements. The arrangement here is that Old Ezra does not bother anyone with the light, and no one bothers him about it, and the light burns.

I'm the keeper now. Grandfather's knees gave out last spring, and I moved into the cottage because somebody had to, and because I'd heard the line my whole life and wanted, finally, to hear the whole of it.

"Ships don't need us," he said, the first night of my apprenticeship, standing at the lens with his hand on the housing like it was a horse's neck. "Haven't in quite some time. GPS, radar, charts in every pocket. A modern ship could thread this coast blindfolded and hungover. It's the people here on land who'll suffer if that light ever goes out."

I asked him to explain, because that's the job: you ask the keeper questions until he dies or you become him.

"Look out there," he said. "What do you see?"

Water. Rocks. The dark Atlantic doing its dark Atlantic work.

"Wrong," he said, pleased. "You see *order*. The light sweeps, and where the light has swept, people believe the night is managed. The fisherman coming home late believes it. The widow on the cliff walk believes it. The kid who's out past curfew with his first heartbreak — he sees the beam swing across the water every twelve seconds and some animal part of him settles, because something is *on watch*. Take the light away and nothing happens to the ships. Everything happens to the people. The night stops being managed and starts being merely survivable. You'd be amazed what a town does when it thinks no one is watching the dark."

I thought it was poetry. Grandfather's generation was allowed poetry.

Then November came. A storm took the coast road, the power, the phone lines — three days of storm, and Saltern Point went to work. The Authority's emergency beacon was down. The modern ships stayed in port; the older boats didn't. The *Maren*, wood-hulled, chart-plotted by hand, came home through it on the third night, and her captain told us afterward that he'd run the whole approach on Saltern's light because it was the only thing on the coast still turning — "the only thing that didn't flinch," is what he said, gripping his mug, and my grandfather pretended to find the floor interesting.

But the storm story isn't the one I mean to tell.

The one I mean is January. Quiet, cold, no storms. A Tuesday. The town's emergency: a boy — nine, asthma, a sledding accident up past the quarry — and the ambulance couldn't make the coast road, and the rescue boat couldn't find the little harbor jetty in the snow-fog. Radio static. Everyone at the harbor's edge with their torches, shouting into white.

I was up in the tower. Grandfather taught me the trick that week: the light doesn't just sweep the sea. You can *hold* it. Stop the rotation, aim the beam down the cliff along the shore path, and turn the tower into a hand.

I held the beam on the harbor mouth for forty minutes while the rescue boat came in on it, slow, breathing, following my light like a child follows a lamp down a hallway. The boy went up the tower stairs because it was the only warm room with space to lie down, and the paramedic worked on him in the lens room, under the lamp, while the beam stood still over the water — and afterward the boy's grandmother came to the cottage and didn't say anything, just held my grandfather's hand in both of hers, and he let her, and that was the whole conversation.

Ships don't need us. It's true. It's been true for a long time, and it will get truer.

But a light that is *kept* — not automated, kept, by someone who chose it — reads to the people on land as a promise: *the night has staff*. And towns run on that belief the way boats run on water. You never see the keel. You'd notice instantly if it left.

I keep the light now. I'll keep it until my knees go, and then I'll hear somebody asking me questions until they become me.

It's the people here on land who'll suffer if it ever goes out. Grandfather never said the second part out loud, but it's in the logbook, his handwriting, the last page:

*They suffer because the light is proof someone stayed.*
