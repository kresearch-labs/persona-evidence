# The Walker

The first time I stopped the world I was seven years old, and I did it to save a glass of milk.

It slipped from my hand and I reached for it with something other than my hand — a muscle I didn't know I had, somewhere behind my eyes — and the world simply held its breath. The glass hung at the height of my knee. The milk stood up out of it like a white flower. Dust hung in the sunbeam and did not fall.

Thirty seconds. I knew that the way you know your own name. I plucked the glass out of the air, set it on the table, and let go, and the world exhaled and went on as if nothing had happened.

That was when I saw it. Far off, through the kitchen window, at the edge of the field behind our house: a figure, dark, the wrong shape for a man and the wrong stillness for anything alive. It was gone when I blinked. I was seven. I decided it was a scarecrow. We didn't have a scarecrow.

I had thirty seconds a day. I never learned why. I learned everything else: that sound died in the stopped world, that the air went thick as water, that I could walk and breathe and touch but nothing else could. And I learned that every time I used it, the figure came closer.

At nine I stopped time to finish a spelling test. The figure stood at the tree line. At eleven, to pull my sister's coat out of the closing doors of a train. It was in the middle of the field. At thirteen I wasted a whole day's thirty seconds moving a bully's shoe six inches so he'd trip, and when I looked, the figure was at our fence, and I could see that it had no face — not a blank one, not a smooth one, just an absence, like a hole cut in the world and pasted over with dark.

I stopped using it. You would think that would be hard, living with a miracle in your pocket and refusing to spend it. It wasn't. Fear is a good vault. I went to school. I grew. I met Anna. Some days I would feel the muscle twitch behind my eyes — a glass tipping, a cyclist wobbling into traffic — and I would hold it down the way you hold down a sneeze in church, and the figure stayed where I had left it: at the fence of a house we no longer lived in, in a field I no longer saw.

Because that's the thing I never told anyone. It didn't stay in the field. It stayed exactly where it had been relative to me — a hundred yards off, always at the edge of vision, in every city, on every street, in the mirror of every shop window if I turned my head fast enough. It had my distance. It was keeping it like a promise.

I was thirty-one when I broke.

We were crossing at a light, Anna and I, and a delivery van came through the intersection doing fifty with its driver asleep at the wheel, and I had time to see it and no time to do anything at all.

So I reached behind my eyes and pulled.

The world held its breath. The van hung in a scream of frozen metal. The air went thick. I had thirty seconds, and I used twenty-six of them dragging Anna three steps backward, out of the path, my heart slamming against the silence, and I was turning to check the distance, the geometry, whether three steps was enough —

and there was nowhere left to check, because the figure was standing beside me.

An arm's length away. After twenty-four years of fields and fences and street corners, an arm's length. Up close it was not dark the way cloth is dark, or shadow. It was dark the way deep water is dark. It had my height. It had my shoulders. It had, I understood with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear, my exact stillness — the stillness of a man standing in a world that has stopped.

It raised one hand, slowly, the way you move around a frightened animal. And it held something out to me.

A glass of milk. Whole. Unspilled.

And I understood. Not in words — the stopped world has no sound, and it had no mouth to make them with — but the way you understand your own name.

Every second I had ever stolen had to be carried by someone. The world does not pause for free. When I reached behind my eyes and pulled, the weight of those thirty seconds went somewhere, onto someone's back, and someone had to walk in the silence I made, holding everything I had let go of. It had been walking toward me my whole life not to catch me. To show me. To hand it back.

It was me, of course. It was every version of me that had ever stood in a stopped world, all my stolen seconds stacked into one shape, patient as erosion, closing the distance one theft at a time. The figure had never been chasing me. I had been catching up to it.

It pressed the glass into my hands. It stepped back — one step, the first it had ever taken away from me — and I felt the muscle behind my eyes change, deepen, become a door opening both ways.

The world exhaled. The van passed through the space where Anna had been and struck nothing at all. She was crying and laughing and asking what happened, how did you — how did we — and I held her and told her the truth, which was that I didn't know.

But I do know what I see now, some afternoons, in the corner of my eye. A field. A fence. A kitchen window, far away, and a small boy on the other side of it, catching a glass that should have broken.

I keep my distance. I keep it like a promise.

He'll come to me. They always do, one second at a time.
