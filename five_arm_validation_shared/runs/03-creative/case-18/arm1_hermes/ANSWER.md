# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-18

## Thirty Seconds a Day, Terms and Conditions

I was six the first time I stopped the world. My mother had burned her hand on the stove, and I remember the scream, and I remember wanting — with my whole small body — for everything to *just wait* so someone could help her. The kitchen went still. Steam hung in the air like a held breath. My mother's face, mid-cry. And I stood in the stopped world, terrified and thrilled, until my thirty seconds ran out and she caught me catching her falling pan, and we both decided it hadn't happened.

The figure started the second time.

At first it was nothing: a smudge at the edge of vision, the way you see your own nose if you think about it. A dark suggestion at the corner of every frozen moment, far away — across the field, at the end of the street, at the back of the frozen classroom. Each day I used the gift, the smudge was closer.

I learned the rules by heart before I was ten:
Thirty seconds, once a day. The world does not remember stopping. Nothing within it can be changed while stopped — I could move through it, but everything left as-was resumes exactly. And the dark figure comes nearer, every time, by exactly the distance I traveled.

Grandmother figured most of it out from my notebook. She was the only one I told, and she didn't disbelieve me, which was her gift. She read my distance-log, did the arithmetic in her head, and said the sentence that governed my whole life: "Then you have to spend it well. It's a purse, love. You don't know how big. Walk light."

So I walked carefully. Fifty years of walking carefully.

I used it for rescues — a child on ice, a driver at a crossing, old Mr. Fenwick's ladder — always the minimum travel, always counting steps like a miser. I used it once for a goodbye, sitting in the stopped hospital corridor holding my mother's hand while the machines' frozen patterns waited, and I've never regretted it, and it cost me most of a decade's distance.

The figure wore a shape. I came to know its silhouette the way you know a favorite coat: a tall figure, still, with a stillness that made everything else's stillness look like fidgeting. It never hurried. It never turned away. When I traveled, it came toward me, and when my thirty seconds ended, it waited where it was, patient as tide, until the next time.

By forty, it stood across the street. By fifty, at the gate. By sixty, on the porch, just past the light of the kitchen window, close enough that on the last day of each use I could see it had a face I couldn't look at directly — not frightening, just *final*, like the last page of a book you love.

Yesterday, it reached me.

I'm seventy-one. I used the last of my distance yesterday morning — walked three steps through a stopped dawn to move a wasp off my sleeping grandson's arm. Three steps, and when the world resumed, the figure was an arm's length away, and I knew the ledger had come due.

It came last night, 3:11 a.m., the hour of all my uses. And here is what I learned, because I was given the one courtesy the whole arrangement never mentioned: the figure stopped, at arm's length, and *let me ask*.

Fifty years of questions. I asked the important one first.

"Why?" — why the gift, why the price, why the watcher.

And the figure — in a voice like the space between two ticking clocks — told me the arrangement. Every stopped second is *borrowed*. Somebody must carry the pause: hold the world's breath, keep the steam hanging, keep the rain in its arcs, while one traveler walks through. The figure was not my hunter. It was my *collaborator* — the one who stopped the world each day I asked, and who followed to collect the miles, and who had waited fifty years for me to grow into someone worth showing the truth.

The gift was never mine. It was a *partnership*. The world stops for everyone someday; I had merely been allowed to schedule it, thirty seconds at a time, and choose what to do inside them.

"And now?" I asked.

"Now you stop walking through stopped time," it said, "and start standing in it. There are things only a partner can do. The child on the ice. The man who must not get on the 8:40. I cannot touch the world. You always could."

It held out — and this is the part I will never manage to explain — its hand.

The figure didn't want my soul. It wanted an *assistant*. Someone who had spent fifty years learning exactly how precious one stopped moment is, and what it costs, and who to give it to.

I took the hand. My thirty seconds for today — the first day I have ever *not* used the gift — began.

The world is stopped. The figure — my colleague, my senior partner of fifty years — is gone now, retired at last, and the coat is mine.

My grandson is asleep down the hall, breathing. There is so much to learn. And today, at 3:12, the world will stop for someone's mother, or someone's child, and someone will need thirty seconds that nobody has.

I have thirty seconds. I have always had thirty seconds.

Now I finally know what they're for.
