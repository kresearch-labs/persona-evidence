# Two Centimeters a Second

The first time the world stopped, I was seven, and I was watching the sprinkler arc over the Kowalskis' lawn. It froze mid-throw. The water hung in the air like beads on an invisible string, and a dragonfly stood motionless inside one golden drop, and I walked the yard with my arms out, touching nothing, breathing the way you breathe in church.

I counted the seconds the way my grandmother taught me to count in hide-and-seek. One-Mississippi. Two-Mississippi. At thirty, the sprinkler woke and the dragonfly flew off as if nothing had happened — and at the far corner of the yard, at the very edge of my eye, something dark stood by the fence.

When I turned, it wasn't there.

It came back the next time. That was the first rule: thirty seconds a day, no more, the count starting over at midnight. The figure taught me the second rule. Every time I stopped the world, it was closer. It never moved while I watched, and it never let me look at it straight; the moment my eyes centered on it, it was standing somewhere else at the edge of things, patient, exact.

In fourth grade I started a notebook. Date. Seconds spent. Where the figure stood. By twelve I had enough numbers to see what I now do for a living, which is keep atomic clocks honest at the national metrology institute — cesium fountains, hydrogen masers, lasers. I know what a second costs. And the numbers said the figure's approach wasn't measured in days or years. It was measured in my seconds. Two centimeters for every second I borrowed. It moved only when the world was stopped, and the world was only stopped when I stopped it.

Which meant every second I took from the clock, I gave to it.

I didn't understand that at twelve. At twelve it was only a dark shape, and dark shapes at the edge of your vision are for fearing, and fear made me a miser. I spent my thirty seconds on falling glasses, on red lights, on one meteor shower I stretched until my eyes ached. I told myself I was saving them up, as if they accumulated. At nineteen I tested it: a full year without stopping anything. The figure didn't move a centimeter. It stood at the corner of the parking lot, just out of sight, and waited. That frightened me more than the walking did.

A rule like mine you verify, or it eats you. So I verified. A camcorder on a tripod, running while I stopped the world and crossed the room: the tape showed a clean excision, thirty frames of nothing, a jump cut, as if a surgeon had lifted half a minute out of the afternoon and sewn the edges together. Thirty seconds. Never twenty-nine. Never thirty-one.

And once, a photograph. During a stop I set a camera at the fence line and held the shutter open. Everything in the print was perfect — a bird pinned mid-wing, rain hanging like nails — except the figure, which was blurred. Mid-stride. The only moving thing in a still world photographs as a smear. I sat down on the kitchen floor and cried, because I had spent fifteen years being afraid of something that was, unmistakably, walking toward me.

The arithmetic finished when I was thirty-three. Distance remaining, divided by two centimeters a second, divided by the seconds I had left to give. The date came out twenty-six months away. I wrote it on the calendar in pencil, and then I never touched the pencil again.

On the day, it rained. I made tea I didn't drink and stopped the world at 4:17 in the afternoon. The rain became a cage of glass beads outside the window. I counted one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi, and I turned and looked straight into the corner of my eye.

It was a woman.

Old, thin, a canvas coat, a gray braid, walking shoes worn through at the heel. For the first time in twenty-eight years she stood in the center of my vision and did not step away, and I understood that she had never stepped away — only ever stood where I wasn't looking, waiting for me to be ready to look.

There is no sound in the stopped world. Air doesn't move. So she came to the window and wrote on the fogged glass with one fingertip, mirrored, so it would read true from my side.

YOU COUNTED TOO.

I nodded. She turned her palm up, and I gave her my notebook, the fourth-grade one, fat with eraser dust; she set her own in my hands. It was older than my mother. The first entry was in a child's careful hand, dated 1951: *Sprinkler froze over the Herrera lawn. Something dark at the corner of the yard. I am seven.*

She wrote again, slowly, the letters trembling at their edges the way she had blurred in my photograph.

YOUR SECONDS ARE MY HOURS.

I thought of the year I had spent nothing. Of twenty-eight years crossed two centimeters at a time, because a frightened child couldn't bear to spend.

I NEARLY DIDN'T GET HERE, she wrote. And beneath it: THANK YOU FOR EVERY ONE.

She had time for one more page. She opened her notebook to the last sheet and wrote while I counted eleven, twelve — what the still is (not stopped time; the world set down gently somewhere, and a small room left open inside it), what holds, what frays, what the room does to a person who lives in it too long. Under forty years of data, on the last line, she wrote only:

YOUR TURN.

The count reached thirty. Rain hit the glass. The kitchen was empty, the notebook in my hands was warm, and the window was only a window, drying in streaks.

I spend all thirty seconds now. Every day, deliberately, the way you keep a fire lit for someone still on the road. I walk the neighborhood while the world is set down. I sit on frozen porches. I read her notebooks — the still, it turns out, has weather, has seasons, has things living in it that are not people, and she charted all of it alone.

Last spring, on Delaney Street, I saw a boy standing at the edge of a lawn, staring hard at the corner of a fence, the way you stare at something just out of the corner of your eye. The sprinkler ran on without him. He was seven, maybe eight, and he wore the look I know from the inside: half wonder, half cold. Somewhere at the edge of his vision, a dark figure was taking its first step. Not me. Not yet.

One day my own count runs out, and I will be the shape at the edge of some child's vision, walking toward her the only way left to me: on time she dares to spend. I hope she is no miser. I hope she spends it all.

I have so far to go.
