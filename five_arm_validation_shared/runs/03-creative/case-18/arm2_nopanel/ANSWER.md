# Thirty Seconds a Day

The first time, I was seven, and I did it for a jar of plum jam.

It slipped off the counter — my mother's good jar, the last of the summer — and I wanted it not to break so badly that the wanting became a thing. The kitchen went quiet the way a photograph is quiet. The jam hung in the air like a small red planet. I had time to walk around it, to notice that the dust motes stood still in the light like punctuation. Then the world exhaled, the jar finished its journey into my hands, and I stood there with my heart going like a bird in a box.

Thirty seconds. That was the allowance. I learned the rules the way you learn the rules of a new school: by breaking them. Thirty seconds a day, no more. Unused seconds did not roll over; they vanished at midnight like Cinderella's coach. And I was not alone in the stillness. Never alone.

There was a figure. Dark the way a doorway is dark — not a thing, but a place where things weren't. It stood at the far edge of my vision, out on the lawn, and it did not move while I watched it. It did not move at all, in fact, except between one stopped second and the next. That took me years to understand. It only walked inside the stopped world. Every day I paused the universe, I let it take a few steps closer.

At seven, it was across the street. At nine, at the corner by the Hendersons' mailbox. I kept a ledger in the back of my math notebook, because that is what you do with a monster: you measure it. Eleven: the fence. Thirteen: the porch steps. I stopped using the gift for a whole year when I was fourteen, out of terror, and the figure did not come closer — but it did not go away either. It stood at the bottom of our driveway for three hundred and sixty-five days, patient as winter, and on the three hundred and sixty-sixth I gave in and spent one second catching a falling teacup, and it took its step, and I cried.

You learn to budget. I spent my seconds on small mercies: a glass nudged back from a table's edge, a bee escorted out a window in frozen mid-hum, the two seconds needed to look composed when I was about to cry in public. Once, at nineteen, I spent eleven of them pulling a boy back from the front of a bus. He never knew. The figure was close enough by then that I could see it wore a coat, or the memory of a coat, and had hands, or the memory of hands.

I did the arithmetic on a night shift at twenty-six, because fear loves arithmetic. Thirty seconds a day. Nineteen years so far — call it six thousand nine hundred days, two hundred and seven thousand seconds, fifty-seven hours of stopped world. The figure had crossed a town, a county, a lifetime of distance in those hours. At the rate I was spending, it would reach me somewhere in my late thirties. I cut my ration to one second a day, then to nothing most days. The figure waited at the end of my street like a debt.

Here is what I never wrote in the ledger, because I did not understand it until it arrived: the figure was not walking toward me to collect. It was walking toward me because I was calling it.

It reached me on a Tuesday, in a supermarket, when I was thirty-eight. I did not plan it. A woman ahead of me in the queue dropped her purse, and everything came out of it at once — coins, pills, a photograph, a small plastic frog her daughter must have given her — and I wanted to help her so much that the world went quiet. I knelt and gathered her life back into her bag, and as I rose I did what I had done ten thousand times: I looked to the corner of my eye.

The figure was beside me.

Close, I had imagined, would be frightening. It was not. Close was a face — and it was my face, the way your face is yours in a dark window: familiar and slightly wrong, older, kind. It had been walking for thirty-one years, and it was out of breath in a way that still things cannot be, and when it spoke, its voice was the sound my kitchen had made when the jam hung in the air.

"Thirty seconds a day," it said. "Do you know what that is, from the other side? It is a lamp in a window. The stillness is very large and very dark, and I have walked it since before there were clocks. Everyone who stops the world lights one lamp, somewhere ahead of me, and I walk toward it. You have been calling me since you were seven. I came as fast as the seconds allowed."

The purse-woman was reaching for her receipt, frozen. The queue hummed its frozen hum. I had perhaps twenty seconds left of my allowance, and I spent all of them just standing there, looking at the only other citizen of the paused world.

"What happens now?" I asked.

"Now you can stop calling," it said. "I am here. From tonight, when you still the world, there will be two of us in it, and it will be a little less large, and a little less dark." It considered. "You will find the seconds feel longer. That is only company."

The world exhaled. The woman paid and smiled at me for no reason she could name, and I walked home through the ordinary loud bright river of time and did not look to the corner of my eye, because for the first time in my life there was nothing lurking at the edge of anything.

That was three years ago. I still get my thirty seconds. I spend most of them on nothing — rain caught mid-fall, my partner asleep, the exact shape of a laugh. And the figure — my colleague, my counterpart, the other resident of the stillness — no longer stands at the corner of my eye but squarely beside me, and we watch the stopped world together like two people watching snow.

It was never a debt. It was never a monster.

It was only very far away, and I kept the light on.
