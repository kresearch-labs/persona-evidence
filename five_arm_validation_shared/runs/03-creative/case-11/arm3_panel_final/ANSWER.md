# The Eleventh Lesson

The Guild pays me forty silver a heist, same as any D-rank menace with a limp and a borrowed cloak. What the ledger doesn't say is that I once held a collapsing mountain the way a man holds a door, and that every third Tuesday I put the limp back on and go to work.

Today's work is the Sunlit Orb, second floor of the Harrow Museum, in front of six students on their first field assignment. The orb is painted glass. The guards are actors on a payroll I signed myself. The only real thing in the building is me, and I am doing my best to be less.

"Remember," I tell them, leaning on the rail, "you get one clean chance at a villain like me. He's slow, he's vain, and he monologues. Use all three."

They're good kids. Petra has memorized the museum's floor plan and mine, which is flattering and inconvenient. Kell's shield work is stiff but honest. The twins, Sef and Ilo, argue about everything except each other's blind sides. And Brannic — Brannic is the one I watch, because he watches me the way you watch a locked door, and locked doors are my whole profession.

At the appointed hour I limp into the gallery, announce my villainy to a room of actors, and let Petra's net take me at the knee. It's a good net. I helped her weight it. The students are laughing, the guards are surrendering on schedule, and I am composing my eleventh defeat — I vary them, it keeps the records interesting — when the skylight comes down.

Not broken. Removed, quietly, in one piece, and set aside. That's how I know before I see him. Actors break things. Professionals move them.

He drops through the hole without a sound and lands like a ledger closing. Gray coat, gray eyes, a rank-badge with a single burnished A. The guards' choreography dies mid-step. One of them curtsies, which is going to be a problem for my cover.

"Vane the Unremarkable," he says. My name. My real one, the D-rank one, pronounced correctly. "They're sending children to take the Orb now."

"Guild's stretched thin," I say, and put a wobble in it. "You want the glass ball? Take it. I've a bad hip and a train to catch."

He looks at me a moment too long. Then the wall behind him comes apart — not knocked down, unwoven, stone unlearning its own shape — and the museum's night custodian is hanging in the air by his collar, and the A-rank's hand is closed around a second orb, a small dull lead one, that I have walked past four hundred times without letting my eyes linger.

The Heart of Harrow. The real relic, two floors down, behind a vault I designed the decoy to make everyone forget. He hadn't come for my lesson. He'd come for the thing under it, and now he has it, and he is deciding what to do with the witnesses.

"Vane," Petra whispers, "what's the drill for an A rank?"

"Retreat. Civilians first, you second, me last." And then, because his attention had already turned to the children the way a man checks the weather: "Actually — change of plan. Kell, wall. Twins, the custodian. Petra, on my mark."

The mark was for the retreat. They heard a beginning. Kids always do.

Kell's shield took the first blow the way a bell takes a hammer — the whole gallery rang. The twins moved the custodian through falling stone like he was a tray of dishes they'd finally agreed on. Petra's second net, the one I didn't help her weight, caught the A-rank's ankle for exactly one heartbeat, which is how long it took me to cross the room.

I had meant to do it slowly. I had meant to stumble, to be lucky, to be small. But the lead orb was already in his coat, and the coat was already burning off him in gray fire, and Brannic was standing in the open between him and the stairs because no one had told Brannic to move.

So I stopped being less.

I don't remember the middle of it. That's the honest truth of power — the middle is a blur and the edges are arithmetic. I remember the gray fire meeting something older and going out like an argument losing its temper. I remember setting the Heart of Harrow on the information desk, gently, because it was never mine. I remember catching the skylight before it landed on the mosaics. I remember the A-rank on his knees, recalculating his whole career, and me leaning on the rail again, hip and all, saying: "The Guild's stretched thin."

He left the way professionals leave, through the hole he'd paid for.

For a while nobody spoke. The actors had found the exits. The custodian sat in a fountain, being brave about it. Then Petra said, "The second net. You weighted it differently on purpose, so I'd have to do it myself," and it wasn't a question, and I didn't answer it, because the answer was three years and forty silver a heist.

Brannic helped me up the stairs. He's a careful boy. At the top he said, "Your hip," and I said, "Is fine," and he said, "It switched legs during the fight," and we left it there.

The Guild's report calls it an unauthorized field exercise with a favorable outcome. The students get their first commendations. I get a reprimand for improvising, a new assignment for next Tuesday, and a note that the Orb will be replaced with a heavier decoy — as if weight were ever the trick.

Six kids saw a D-rank villain win a fight he shouldn't have. By spring they'll have talked themselves into believing I got lucky, because that's the story that lets them keep their courage. They'll train for luck. They'll be right.

Next Tuesday I put the limp back on and go to work.
