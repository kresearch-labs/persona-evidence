# The Curriculum

The bank alarm sang its little song, and right on schedule, the hero came through the skylight.

I checked my watch. 2:14 AM. Punctual. I made a note on the clipboard I kept for exactly this. *Response time: excellent.*

"Stand down, villain!" she shouted, landing in the three-point pose they'd all learned from the same streaming series. She couldn't have been more than nineteen. Her suit was new — I could smell the factory sealant on it. First field deployment. My favorite kind.

"Ah," I said, hefting the prop bag of cash. "A hero. Woe is me."

Her name, according to the file the Guild had sent over, was Brightline. Light manipulation, C rank, good instincts, tendency to monologue. They wanted her bloodied a little. Not broken — bloodied. There's a difference, and the difference is my whole profession.

Officially, I am Knockoff, D rank villain, threat assessment: minimal. A joke with a gimmick — I copy things, badly. Cheap knockoffs of whatever I touch. The Guild's database says I once tried to steal an armored car and produced a cardboard replica of it instead, then wept.

Unofficially, my file is kept in a different drawer, and the people with keys to that drawer pay me very well to lose fights on purpose.

Brightline fired a lance of hard light. I let it clip my shoulder — spun with it, sold it, crashed through a display of complimentary pens. She gasped. They always gasp the first time they actually hit someone.

"I — stay down!"

I got up slowly, because the curriculum says the student must learn that a downed opponent may rise. I produced a knockoff of her light lance — mine flickered like a dying fluorescent tube — and charged her at exactly the speed a C rank can dodge. She dodged. Beautifully, actually. I made another note.

We went three rounds. She learned to watch her footing. She learned not to announce her attacks. She learned that fear shakes the hands but doesn't stop them. By the end she was grinning through a split lip, and I was flat on my back in the wreckage of the deposit boxes, wheezing theatrically.

"You're under citizen's arrest," she panted.

"Curse you," I said, "and your superior training."

Which is when the ceiling came off.

Not the skylight. The *ceiling*. The whole roof peeled back like the lid of a sardine can, and the night sky above the bank was blotted out by a man-shaped hole in the stars.

Malice. A rank. Threat assessment: *do not engage, do not evacuate, simply hope he is elsewhere.* He'd killed four heroes in Prague. He didn't have a gimmick. He had a body count.

"Well," Malice said, and his voice was the sound of a furnace door opening. "A bank robbery. How nostalgic. I think I'll take everything. Including the witnesses."

I watched Brightline do the math. I watched her understand, in one terrible second, that the man floating in the torn-open sky was not part of anyone's lesson plan. Her light flickered. Her hands shook. And then — God, they break my heart, these kids — she planted her feet between Malice and the vault full of tellers, and she raised her fists anyway.

"Run," she told me, not turning around. "Civilian. Get clear."

Civilian. She thought the D rank joke on the floor was a bystander now. She was going to die for a villain's prop bag.

I sighed and stood up, brushing plaster dust off my coat.

"Brightline," I said. "Final lesson. Watch closely. This one's not on the exam, but it's the whole curriculum."

Malice tilted his head. "The clown wants to die first?"

I touched the floor of the bank.

Here's the thing about copying. The Guild tested me once, long ago, and I copied a teacup and produced a cracked one, and they wrote *D rank* and moved on. Nobody asked the obvious question: what happens when he copies *a person*? What happens when a man who can copy anything touches the ground beneath an A rank monster and copies *him*?

The answer is: a knockoff. A cheap, flickering, dying-fluorescent-tube knockoff of the fourth-strongest being on Earth — which is still, for about ninety seconds, the fifth-strongest.

I hit Malice with his own gravity well. He hit me back with mine, which is to say his, and the street outside became a crater. Ninety seconds is not long. It is long enough. I spent sixty of them making sure he understood that whatever he did to me, I would return at slightly worse quality and twice the enthusiasm, and that there was a non-zero chance — he could not calculate how non-zero — that tonight was the night the knockoff didn't crack.

He left. A ranks are survivors before they are anything else; you don't get that strong by gambling on unknowns. He tore a hole in the night and was gone, and I collapsed into the crater, every bone vibrating like a struck tuning fork.

Brightline climbed down after me, light trembling in her palms.

"You're not D rank," she whispered.

"Officially," I said, "I absolutely am. And officially, you fought off a D rank villain tonight, and the roof was structural damage. Gas leak. Tragic." I coughed. "Unofficially, kid, you stood your ground in front of an A rank to protect a stranger. Your instructor is going to hear about that."

"You're going to report *me*?"

"I report everyone. It's called a grade."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she did the thing that makes the whole rotten arrangement worth it — she laughed. Shaky, disbelieving, alive.

"Same time next month?" she asked.

"Jewelry district," I said. "Bring better footwork."

I limped home as the sun came up, filed my report — *student shows exceptional courage; recommend advancement; villain escaped, as scheduled* — and left out the part where the ceiling came off. Some lessons you can't put in a curriculum.

Some lessons are just a teacher, standing between the dark and his students, being exactly as strong as he needs to be.
