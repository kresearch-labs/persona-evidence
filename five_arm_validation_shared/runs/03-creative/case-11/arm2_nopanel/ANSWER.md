# The Easy One

Officially, I am D-rank. "The Grey Wisp," they call me — a minor nuisance with a smoke charm and a habit of robbing banks nobody audits. The Guild's registry lists my threat assessment as "negligible; recommend ignoring." I paid good money for that assessment. It's the best review I've ever had.

Unofficially, I'm the reason the hero retention rate in this region is eighty percent instead of forty.

The Guild figured it out years ago: fresh heroes die in their first real fight. Not because they're weak — because they've never been hit. Academy sparring is choreographed, polite, padded. The first time a goblin bites through a bracer, or a bandit swings for the throat instead of the sword arm, a nineteen-year-old with a magic sword freezes, and freezing is fatal.

So the Guild hired a villain.

That's me. Every few weeks I "heist" something — a grain shipment, a tax wagon, a museum's second-best crown — and a class of fledgling heroes is dispatched to stop me. I monologue. I telegraph. I lose in a way that feels earned. They go home believing they've won their first real fight, and six months later, when a real monster tries to kill them, they don't freeze. They've been hit before. They know what it costs.

It's good work if you can get it. The pay is decent, the hours are flexible, and I've never once had to actually hurt anyone.

Today's job: the Crossroads Bank, a squat building with a vault full of municipal promissory notes. Boring, perfect. My students are four of them, fresh badged, led by a girl named Tam Vess — the one with the shield and the stubborn jaw. I'd watched her class for two weeks. Green as spring wheat, but good.

I was halfway through my third monologue — you have to pace those, like a stew — when the wall came down.

Not a hole in the wall. The wall. All of it, outward, in one piece, hurled across the street like a discarded coat. Dust, screaming, and through the dust walked a man in black lacquered armor with a red silk sash, and my stomach dropped, because I knew that sash.

Karrow Vane. A-rank. "The Ledger." He collected debts in blood and called it accounting. Last year he put an entire Guild garrison in the hospital. His file was three pages long, and the last line of every page was *do not engage*.

He was supposed to be two hundred miles south.

"Grey Wisp," he said, smiling under his helmet. "They still have you playing pretend?"

Behind me, my four students had done exactly the right thing: shields up, formation tight, the little fire-mage crouched low with his hands ready. They were terrified. They were magnificent. And Karrow Vane was going to walk through them like weather.

"Boys and girls," I said quietly, not turning around, "remember your training. Shields angled. Don't chase. When I say *now*, you run for the Guildhouse and you don't look back. That's an order from your opponent, and you can trust it, because beating you is the only thing I want tonight."

"He's D-rank," Tam said. Her voice shook. She held anyway. Good girl.

"He is," I agreed. "Now."

They ran. Vane let them — no. He didn't. His hand came up, and there was a hum of gathering power, the kind of spell that doesn't care about shields or children, and I remembered the file's last line, *do not engage*, and engaged.

I put myself between the spell and the students and let my smoke charm burn off. All of it. Twelve years of careful, purchased, mediocre reputation, gone in one breath, because the alternative was scraping four teenagers off the cobblestones.

The spell broke on my palm like a wave on a pier.

Vane stopped walking. The helmet tilted. "Well. The stories were true."

"Karrow." I kept my voice bored. My heart was going like a drum line. "You're crashing my heist."

"I heard a D-rank was working this street. I thought I'd come see if the Guild's pet was as soft as his paperwork."

"Your file says you don't fight for free."

"My file says I collect debts. And you owe the profession one, Wisp. Do you know what it's like, being A-rank, when every strong hand in the region is retired, or bought, or playing nursemaid to children?"

Ah. Not a crash. A complaint.

"Tam," I called, without taking my eyes off him. "Still there?"

"Y-yes!" From the doorway across the street. She hadn't run. Of course she hadn't run.

"Then watch closely. This is the part of the lesson I'm not allowed to teach."

Vane came at me like a landslide, and for about forty seconds I gave the street the show of its life. I let him feel my real weight — once, twice — enough that he'd spend the rest of his career wondering. Enough that he'd write his own report, and the registry would gain a footnote, and some clerk would earn a bonus.

Then I did the thing I'm actually best at. The thing they really pay me for.

I lost.

Beautifully. Convincingly. I went down hard, coughed smoke, let him stand over me with his blade at my collar, and said, "Fine fight, Ledger. Fine fight." And he laughed — a real laugh, the first he'd had in a year, I'd bet — and let me live, because A-ranks don't kill D-ranks who've entertained them. It's in the file. I wrote that line too.

He walked away into the dust, satisfied, and I lay on the cobbles feeling every one of my three hundred years and listened to four pairs of boots come pounding back.

"You're hurt—" Tam started.

"Wind knocked out. Happens, at my rank." I let her haul me up. Her hands were steady now. All their hands were. They'd watched a villain lose, and they'd watched a villain *choose* to lose, and they didn't know yet that they'd learned the second thing.

"Sir," the fire-mage said, "when his spell hit you — you didn't even move your feet."

"Old man's trick. Brace before you're hit." I dusted off my coat. "Now — that vault is unguarded and the Guild pays recovery fees. Shall we finish our heist?"

They cheered. Tam offered me her arm, and I took it, D-rank to the last, and we limped off together to rob the Crossroads Bank while the dust settled on a wall I'd have to pay to rebuild.

Worth it. It's always worth it. Somebody has to be the easy fight.

Somebody has to be the wall they learn they can break.
