# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-11

## The Heist

Officially, I'm D-rank. "Waddles," they call me in the registries — a fat man in a bad mask who robs jewelry stores and always leaves a note and never, ever hurts anyone. D-rank. E-rank on my bad knees.

Unofficially — well. You're about to see unofficially.

Understand the arrangement first, because nobody outside three people and a pension fund knows it: fifteen years ago the Hero Academy realized their graduates were dying. First real fight, no scaffolding, no mercy — A-ranks ate them alive, or S-ranks found them and *made examples*. A generation of kids with good hearts and one month of experience. So they came to me, the one villain in the registry who had never once drawn blood, and we signed a contract that would make a labor lawyer weep: I rob things on their schedule. My "crimes" are training missions. Four fledglings at a time, a controlled site, a fight they're meant to *win* — barely — and walk away from taller. The paperwork calls me a contractor. The kids call me the practice dummy. The Academy calls me nothing, officially, because officially their students beat "a D-list villain" fair and square.

Tonight's class is five weeks out from certification. Good kids. The striker telegraphs left. The shield girl over-commits on angles. The flyer has never been hit while moving, not once, and that is a *wound* waiting to close. And the little mystic — solid hands, terrible instincts.

We're in the middle of my scripted heist — a decoy vault, three sets of harmless traps, a monologue I've delivered so many times I do voices — when the west wall comes off.

Not opens. *Comes off.* Lifted, balanced on two fingers, set aside like a lid.

Iron Meridian. A-rank, recently very ambitious. She stands in the doorway she made, and behind her the night is full of her crew, and she says, "Well. A real heist. And children." She'd been hitting Academy satellites all month, growing bolder, and now, while her people sweep the vault behind me, she cracks her knuckles and looks at my students the way you look at a buffet.

The kids square up. God love them, they square up.

"Positions," I say, and I watch four hearts nearly give out — because I've never used the command voice. I sound like a ledger. "Practice is over. This is the part you trained for. Striker — left is your tell, tonight you don't have one. Shield, stop guarding yourself, guard the mystic. Flyer: you're going to get hit tonight, that's not a failure, it's curriculum. Feel it once here, with me, and it never owns you."

Meridian laughs. It's a good laugh; that's the worst part. "You're the famous practice dummy," she says. "Waddles. I read your file. D-rank."

"File's accurate," I say, and take off my coat, and roll my shoulders, and set the coat on a crate where I can find it, because the coat is dry-clean only and I *am* kind of fat, that part's true.

What the file doesn't say — what four people in the world know — is that Waddles was S-ranked before the Academy had letters, before the war with the Nine made everyone polite about power, and that I retired the way you retire from anything dangerous: quietly, with paperwork, into a role where nobody dies. The Academy needed a monster kids could safely beat. Turns out I do great monster.

Meridian hits me like a freight car with opinions. I should wobble. I put out one hand.

The vault behind us stops making noise. Her crew stops, too, mid-loot, because the sound of a fat man in a bad mask catching an A-rank punch one-handed and *setting it down gently* is the sound of everyone's assumptions filing for bankruptcy.

"Twenty-two years ago," I tell her, conversationally, walking her backward one deliberate step at a time, "I was the thing they sent when an A-rank wasn't enough. Ask your mentors about the Halcott Index. Ask them who they still measure things against."

She's fast. Genuinely A-class. I let her see three of my four tells, because the kids need to watch how an experienced fighter reads a person, and I give her the full show: the redirect into her own momentum, the pressure-point tap that makes her gauntlet release, the little scooping step that turns her charge into a ballet. All of it slow enough for five weeks of training to learn from. The striker lands his first clean hit of his career on her exposed flank. The shield girl finally guards *someone*. The flyer takes a hit — stagger, tears, breath — and comes back up, and that's the lesson of her whole life, arriving on schedule.

Meridian pulls back out of range, breathing hard, recalculating. "You're not D-rank."

"I'm exactly D-rank," I say, wheezing theatrically, because the kids are watching and the wheeze is pedagogical. "Every single day. It's the hardest rank there is." I nod at her crew, who have remembered, slowly, that they are holding my decoy loot. "Take the street-level exit, leave the decoys, and I'll tell the Academy you gave them the fight of the year. Everyone walks."

And Meridian — because she's A-rank, which means she's smart enough to count — looks at my students, looks at me standing there with two decades of restraint like a held breath, and does the math out loud: "Contractor."

"Contractor," I agree.

She salutes, lazy and real, and takes her people out through the hole in the wall she's too polite to fix.

Behind me, five kids are absolutely lit up with the best night of their careers. The mystic is crying a little. The flyer is laughing, shaking, *alive*.

"Waddles," the striker says, "did you SEE—"

"I saw a bunch of D-ranks," I say, holding up one finger, "preparing to become A-ranks. Notes by Friday. Now help me find my coat."
