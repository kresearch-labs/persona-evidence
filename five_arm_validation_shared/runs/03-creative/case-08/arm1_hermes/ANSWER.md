# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-08

## The Long Way Around

I missed her thirty-one times before we finally stood in the same room.

I know the number because I found out — after — that she'd kept a list too. That's the thing about fate: it isn't subtle once you're allowed to read the file.

Here's the ledger. In third grade, she moved to my town the same week I broke my leg. Two years on crutches, and the girl across the street who was new and friendless and had already asked, apparently, whether the boy with the cast needed his mail brought up. My mother told me about it years later: "That sweet girl offered. You were asleep."

Age fifteen: same bus route, six weeks, seat across the aisle. I had headphones and a crush on someone two towns over. She had a book of poems she held like a plate she didn't want to drop.

Age nineteen: we sat the same section at the same concert, four rows apart, both there alone because of a mutual band neither of us would admit to liking. She says she saw me singing the wrong words and decided I was "probably insufferable." Fate's work, apparently, also involves character assessment.

Age twenty-four: a hospital. Her grandmother was on the fourth floor; my father the fifth. We took the same elevator eleven times. I remember the woman with the fixed gaze and the lanyard because she held the door for a nurse with too many things, and I remember thinking: *that's how you do it.* I didn't know I was memorizing her.

Age twenty-six: she applied to the bakery where I worked weekends. I wasn't on the schedule the day of her interview. The owner hired someone hungrier.

Age twenty-nine, the near-miss that still makes me ridiculous: a wedding, her cousin's, my best friend officiating. I stayed home with what I swore was flu. My friend, to this day, describes the "unbelievable woman" at table six. I have seen the photos. I am standing — absent — between us in every wide shot, a ghost at my own summons.

The list of misses goes on. A shared mechanic. A shared dentist. A book she left in a café; a book I bought the next day, used, the one with the marginalia I fell for and couldn't find. *Her handwriting*, it turns out, margin to margin, and I returned it to the café and the café lost it, and somewhere in the universe a clipboard full of instructions was being initialed by increasingly exasperated angels.

We finally met at thirty-four, in the least fated place imaginable: the DMV. Number 84. Number 85. Both of us there for the same absurd reason — address corrections, old licenses — both of us so resigned to the day that we were rude to it.

The vending machine did it. My card failed; her card failed; the machine ate both. She said, "It's a sign," with flat sarcasm, and I said, without thinking, "It's a sign it wants us to buy two coffees from the guy outside," and she laughed the laugh of a person who has decided, against all evidence, to like you.

Her name was on the coffee cup. Maren. The lanyard girl from the hospital. The interview the schedule missed. The band. The cousin's wedding, four rows away. Thirty-one coincidences, and I would have told you none of them until she pulled out her phone — she keeps an actual note, dated, everything — and we spent forty-five minutes in the DMV parking lot ticking off the file.

"Why now?" I asked. "Thirty-one chances."

She looked at the DMV doors, the fluorescent purgatory of it all, and said the thing I've kept since:

"Because you'd finally stopped trying to meet someone. Every time before, you were *performing*. Auditioning. Fate can't hand you to yourself while you're being somebody else. Today you were just a tired man who was kind to a nurse and sings wrong words. That's the person I was supposed to meet."

We've been three years now. The letters and the near-misses have become a shared album she maintains with terrifying diligence — bus transfers, hospital lanyards, the marginalia book, photographed, annotated. On our first anniversary she gave me the café book. She'd written one line under her own seventeen-year-old marginalia: *P.S. — he sings worse now. It's fine.*

So: is there a fate? I used to say no, and then I audited the file. Thirty-one misses, each one a door I didn't take, and one fluorescent afternoon when the universe, having run out of subtlety, deployed a vending machine.

I don't think fate wanted perfect. I think it wanted *ready*. And I think it was patient the way only something that has your whole address can be.
