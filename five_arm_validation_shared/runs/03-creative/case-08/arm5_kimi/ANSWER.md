# For the Drive You're Not Taking

The tape was in the glovebox when I bought the Honda: a black cassette, no case, a strip of masking tape across the spine with five words in blue ballpoint — FOR THE DRIVE YOU'RE NOT TAKING. The car was eleven years old and cost nine hundred dollars. The tape was worth more.

I played it driving home from the seller's driveway, and then for six years after that. It opened with a song I'd never heard by a band I had, moved through old soul and one terrible, wonderful pop-punk cover, and ended with forty seconds of ocean — actual ocean, recorded on a beach somewhere, gulls and all. I learned it the way you learn a road. I knew where the tape hissed. I knew the pause before the last song like I knew my own front step.

I never took the drive. I didn't know where it was, for one thing. But I had a theory: whoever made the tape had planned a route and not gone, and the tape had waited in the glovebox like a spare key, and I liked the idea that I was keeping it warm for someone.

Here's what I didn't know then.

I didn't know that in 2009, at a concert in a park in the city where I went to college, a girl two rows behind me ducked when the drummer threw his stick, and that I caught it, and that for one second we looked at each other with our mouths open, and then her friends pulled her toward the exits because it was starting to rain.

I didn't know that in 2011 she worked Thursdays at the used bookstore on Fourth, and that the copy of *The Moviegoer* I bought there — the one with the note in the margin on page 88 that said *yes, but he gets there in the end* — had been priced and shelved by her that morning.

I didn't know that in 2013 we lived on the same street in Portland, one year apart, and that the landlord showed me her old apartment and complained about the tenant who'd painted the kitchen yellow without asking. I painted over the yellow. I think about that now.

I didn't know that on New Year's Eve 2014 we were at the same party in the same too-small house, and that I left at eleven because I felt like a piece of furniture, and she arrived at eleven-thirty and stood, apparently, exactly where I had been standing, and was also mistaken for furniture by the same host.

I found all of this out in a diner off Highway 1, six years after the tape came into my life, because the Honda finally died the way old cars die — slowly, then on a Tuesday — and before I let the tow truck take it, I did the only thing that felt right. I took the drive. I got the car as far south as it would go, which turned out to be a turnout above a gray, gorgeous ocean, and when it shuddered and stopped I walked the last two miles to the diner with the tape in my jacket pocket like a passport.

I wasn't playing it. I want to be clear about that. It wasn't in a deck anywhere. It was just sitting on the table next to my coffee while I waited out the rain, and the woman at the counter — who had been eating pie and reading a paperback with the concentration of a bomb technician — got up, walked over, and said, "Where did you get that?"

Not "is that a tape?" Not "nice relic." *Where did you get that*, like I'd been caught with her wallet.

So I told her. The Honda, the glovebox, the six years. And she sat down across from me without asking and said, "I made that in 2004. I was going to drive to Los Angeles and become a different person. I got as far as the end of the driveway." She said the ocean at the end was recorded on a day so cold her hands went numb holding the microphone. She said the pop-punk cover was a joke that had stopped being funny and become sincere, which is the best thing a joke can do. She said she'd sold the car because the radio only worked if you hit it, and she'd forgotten the tape was in there, and she'd thought about it on every road trip since.

We talked until the diner closed. We found the concert, the bookstore, the street, the party — each one surfacing slowly, the way a photograph develops, both of us leaning in like we could make it come up faster. Twenty years of near misses, laid out on Formica between the ketchup and the pie.

Here is the part I keep turning over. Everyone wants to hear that it was fate — that some patient hand had been arranging us like furniture the whole time, sliding us into position, waiting for the room to be right. Maybe. But I was there for all of it, and what it felt like from the inside was not a plan. It felt like weather: two fronts moving around each other for years, close enough to change each other's pressure, never close enough to break.

Until the drive I wasn't taking.

She lives in my apartment now — the one above the shop she opened, which sells, among other things, used books, and I have it on good authority that the margin notes are hers. The Honda is gone, but we kept the glovebox. The tape lives in it, on a shelf by the door, where we can see it on our way out.

Sometimes, on Sundays, we take the drive. She was right about the route. It ends at the ocean, and the ocean sounds exactly like the last forty seconds of side two, and we stand there with our shoes in our hands like people who have finally arrived at a party — not early, not late, not furniture.

Guests.
