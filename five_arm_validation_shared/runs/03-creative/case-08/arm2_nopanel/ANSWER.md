# Nearly, Until Then

I met Nora at eleven minutes to midnight in a twenty-four-hour pharmacy, both of us reaching for the last box of children's antihistamines. Her kid had hives; my nephew did. She got the box because her hand was faster, and I got her number because I said, "If your night is as long as mine is about to be, the least I can do is buy you a coffee sometime that isn't at midnight."

That part is ordinary. People meet in pharmacies. What came after is what I keep turning over, the way you turn over a stone and find the whole river underneath.

We went for that coffee on a Tuesday. By the second cup we were doing what new people do — comparing childhoods, trading zip codes — and she said she'd grown up in Marlowe, and I said I'd spent every August of my boyhood in Marlowe, at my grandmother's, and she laughed and said, "Nobody's *from* Marlowe. Marlowe is where you're from near."

"Sandpiper Beach," I said.

"Sandpiper," she said, and stopped stirring.

Here is what we found, that Tuesday and the Tuesdays after, when we started keeping a list on a napkin because it had stopped being a coincidence and started being an inventory:

In the summer of 1998 there was a sandcastle contest on Sandpiper Beach, and a photographer from the county paper took a picture of the winners — two kids, eight years old, gap-toothed, holding a trophy made of painted shells. That photo hung in my grandmother's hallway for twenty years. The girl on the left was Nora. I am the boy on the right, and for twenty years I called her "the kid with the bucket," and she, at her own grandmother's house, called me "the boy who's crying." I was crying. We won, and I cried, and she remembers thinking the boy was lucky to have someone take his picture.

In 2006 she was the girl ahead of me in the rain outside the Orpheum, holding an umbrella that had surrendered to the wind, and I — I have thought about this for weeks — I had an umbrella, a good one, and I stood behind her for eleven minutes while we both waited for the same bus, composing the sentence I would say, and the bus came, and I said nothing. She remembers a boy sighing behind her. She remembers thinking, *he sounds like he's about to say something.* I was. I have been, in a sense, ever since.

In 2011 my phone rang from a number I didn't know, and a woman's voice, warm and hurried, said: "Hi, it's me — I'm running late, traffic on the bridge, start without me, I'll bring the wine." Then she hung up before I could tell the stranger on the other end that she had the wrong number. I kept the voicemail for years. I don't know why. It was the kindest wrong number I ever got, and on bad days I played it, the way you'd keep a letter that arrived for the person you intended to become. She was calling her sister. Her sister had changed numbers. For eleven seconds, I was the man her sister was waiting for.

And in 2019 — this is the one that undid us both — her father was in the cardiac ward at St. Ambrose, and mine was two floors up, in oncology, and for six nights that October we were in the same building at the same hours. We used the same vending machine, the one on three that ate dollar bills. She remembers a man who fed it four dollars, got nothing, and said, "Fine, keep it. Buy yourself something nice." She laughed, alone, at 2 a.m., and thought: *that's the first thing that's been funny all week.* I remember a woman laughing in the hallway, and how the sound went through me like a stitch through cloth, and how I thought, absurdly, *I hope whoever she's with knows how lucky he is.*

She was laughing at me. I was hoping some other man knew his luck. That is the whole story of us in one vending machine: close enough to touch, both looking the wrong way.

When we finished the napkin list — the beach, the bus, the voicemail, the ward, plus a concert in 2014 where we stood on opposite sides of the same crowd, plus a bookstore where she shelved the very book I came in to buy, plus the pharmacy — we sat back and looked at it. Twenty-six years of almost.

"I used to think I was bad at this," she said. "Timing. Life. Pick one."

"I missed every opportunity," I said. "Every single one. I stood eleven feet from you in the rain and said nothing."

"You fed a vending machine four dollars and told it to buy itself something nice."

"I did."

She turned the napkin over, wrote something, and slid it across the table. It said: *You didn't miss them. They were practice.*

Here is what I believe now, and I hold it the way you hold something warm in winter, a little too tightly, unashamed: fate is not a door held open while you walk through. It is a door that keeps opening — patiently, in all weathers, for years — while you look at your phone, or your shoes, or the wrong part of the room. It doesn't book one appointment and sulk when you cancel. It keeps making them. It is stubborn on your behalf. It will use a sandcastle, a bus, a wrong number, a machine that eats dollars, and finally, if it must, a box of children's antihistamines at eleven minutes to midnight.

We missed everything, once. Then, at the end of a long line of misses, we didn't.

The napkin is on my desk now, under a painted-shell trophy she found in her grandmother's attic and gave me for my birthday. The boy in the photo is still crying. The girl with the bucket is still eight. And every morning I look at them and think: *you have no idea how long it's going to take you — and no idea how worth it the waiting was.*
