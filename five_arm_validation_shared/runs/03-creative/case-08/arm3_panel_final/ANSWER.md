# The Long Way Around

The coffee shop on Delancey had a chalkboard sign that said FRESH MINT TEA in handwriting I would later recognize as hers; at the time I only noticed it because the F was backwards.

I ordered a mint tea. I burned my tongue on it. I sat at the one free table and watched the rain come down on Delancey like it had somewhere better to be. I did not know yet that I had been in this coffee shop before. That came later.

She came in at 4:12, shaking out an umbrella, and said to the man behind the counter, "The usual," and he said, "Haven't seen you in a while," and she said, "I moved to Queens," like that explained everything.

There was one other seat at my table. She asked if it was taken. I said no. That was the whole conversation for eleven minutes, all surface and weather.

Then she looked at my book.

I had been reading a paperback with a torn cover, bought for two dollars at a library sale in 2019. She went still, the way you go still at a song from childhood.

"Where did you get that?" she said.

"Library sale. Why?"

"Because I wrote my name in it."

I opened the cover. There, in blue ballpoint, slanted and sure of itself: *Mara Ellison, June 2011.*

"That's my handwriting," she said. "I donated that book when I moved out. I wrote my name in it because I loved it."

"You defaced it," I said.

"I annotated it," she said, and then, because she could not leave a thing half-said, "Check page ninety-four."

Page ninety-four had a sentence underlined twice and a note in the margin, small and furious: *this is the part everyone gets wrong.*

"I underlined that in 2011," she said. "You're reading it in 2025 — how long have you had it?"

"Since 2019."

"Six years," she said. "You've been carrying my handwriting around for six years."

Here is what I could not explain to her at the table. That book was not supposed to be at that sale. The sale was in a town I only visited because my flight got rerouted, because a wedding got moved up a week, because the bride's sister changed jobs. A chain of small collisions, each one nothing, each one load-bearing. I bought it because it was raining and the sale was free admission. I kept it through three apartments, though I got rid of everything else, every time. I could not tell you why. I just kept it.

And Mara — Mara had her own chain. She read it the summer her father was sick, because she needed something to hold that wasn't a phone call. She wrote in the margins because her mother used to, and it made the book feel like a conversation with someone still in the room. She donated it in the purge of 2023, when she left an apartment and a person in the same week; the box almost went to a church and went instead to the library, because the church was closed for a funeral.

A funeral. The thing has a sense of humor.

We sat there and did the math out loud, and it got worse the longer we did. She grew up in Hartford. I grew up in Hartford. Our schools were rivals — her team beat mine in the county final in 2004, and we were both there, and we have found the backs of our own heads in the same photo of that gym, on opposite sides. In 2013 we were both at the same show at a basement venue in Providence; she remembers the ceiling leaked; I remember meeting the person I would spend five years with, the wrong one, the one I had to get wrong first. In 2018 we rode the same delayed Amtrak, car four — she once said she always takes car four, and I said I always take car four, and we thought it was a small funny thing.

Every one of those was a door. I missed every door — or walked through, and the room closed up behind me like water.

"You know what I keep thinking," she said. "It's not that fate kept us apart. It's that fate kept trying, and I kept being busy."

"That's worse," I said.

"It's not fate's fault," she said. "We just weren't ready. You don't get the note until you've done the reading."

I want to be careful, because I know how this sounds. Two people can find coincidences anywhere; a mind looking for a pattern will always find one. Hartford and Amtrak and a paperback prove nothing except that the world is smaller than we pretend. I made that argument for years: there's no such thing as destiny, only probability and attention.

But I also know this: I kept a book for six years for no reason. She underlined a sentence in 2011 that I underlined, in pencil, in 2020, not knowing hers was there, two lines below mine, saying the same thing. When I found them — hers above mine, six years apart, the same argument in two hands — I sat on the floor of my apartment and felt something I can only call being expected.

That's the word. Not chosen. Expected. Like the seat was held.

We missed every opportunity until now. I used to think that was the sad version: fourteen years, one city, a hundred shared rooms, and nothing. But here is what I believe now, stirring my own tea, rain still coming down on Delancey: the opportunities didn't expire. They accumulated. Every missed door was a brick. The life I lived wrong in 2013 taught me what wrong felt like. The apartment she left in 2023 emptied out the space that 2025 would fill. We were not late. We were being carried the long way around, past everything we had to see first, and the route was the point.

The mint tea got cold. Neither of us got up to leave.

"Page ninety-four," she said finally. "Do you agree with it? That everyone gets it wrong?"

I looked at the margin. Her furious blue ink. My quiet pencil. The same sentence underneath both.

"Yes," I said. "Everyone gets it wrong. It took me thirty-four years and a two-dollar paperback."

"Thirty-one," she said. "But who's counting."

She was counting. She'd been counting her whole life; she just hadn't known what she was counting toward.

We closed the place down. The man never charged us for refills, and months later he told us the backwards F was how she first noticed the place, three years before, when I was out sick and she sat at my table alone.

There's a version of this where we met in 2004, or 2013, or 2018, and it went fine, and we were different people, not yet broken into the exact shapes that fit. I don't wish for that version anymore. Every missed train was a mercy. Every wrong room was a rehearsal.

We were one long sentence, and we had simply not reached the part with the verb.
