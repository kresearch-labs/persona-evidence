# The Moriarty of Idiots

I have solved the Case of the Clockwork Bishop in eleven minutes. I have identified a murderer by the way he buttered toast. My mind is a cathedral of logic, and every criminal in London is a hymn I have already memorized.

Then I met Dennis Puddle.

It began with the theft of the Harrington Emerald, a stone the size of a pigeon's egg, stolen from a vault guarded by three locks, two men, and one dog of proven loyalty. The police were baffled. I examined the scene and felt the old, delicious machinery of deduction begin to turn.

"The thief entered through the skylight," I announced, "using a rope of Italian manufacture. He knew the vault's combination, which means he has an accomplice inside the household. He left no fingerprints, which means he is a professional. Expect him to flee the country within the day, most likely to Antwerp, where the stone can be cut and sold. I shall have him by Thursday."

I was wrong about everything.

The thief had entered through the front door, which was unlocked, because he had simply pushed it. He knew the combination because it was written on a sticky note affixed to the vault by Lord Harrington himself, who was also forgetful. He left no fingerprints because he was wearing oven mitts, not as a precaution, but because he had been baking a potato and had not taken them off. He did not flee to Antwerp. He fled to his mother's house in Croydon, which was where he already lived.

His name was Dennis Puddle, and he was the stupidest man I have ever encountered, and I have testified before Parliament.

I learned all this not through deduction but through confession, because Dennis walked into Scotland Yard and attempted to report the emerald as lost property. He had found it, he explained, in his coat pocket, and could not remember how it got there. The desk sergeant, to his eternal credit, fainted.

You might think this would end the matter. It did not. While in custody, Dennis cheerfully confessed to six other crimes, five of which he had not committed, and one of which — the Great Bullion Vanish of last March — he absolutely had, though his account of it contradicted every fact I had established.

I had spent eight months on the Bullion Vanish. I had constructed a theory involving a disgraced naval officer, a submarine of private manufacture, and a mole inside the Bank of England. It was elegant. It was airtight. It was completely false. Dennis had stolen the gold by driving the bullion van away while its guards were at lunch, having found the keys in the ignition and assumed the van was, in his words, "up for grabs." He had then parked it behind a pub and forgotten about it. The gold sat there for eight months. The pub's regulars had been using the van as a dartboard.

I interrogated him myself, convinced that beneath this idiocy lay a genius of misdirection — a Moriarty playing the fool. I presented false evidence to see how he would react. He believed it instantly and confessed to that too. I laid logical traps; he wandered through them like a cow through a hedge, unaware the hedge existed. The only calculation I ever witnessed was Dennis attempting to work out whether a sausage roll counted as lunch.

"How did you evade my network of informants?" I demanded.

"What's an informant?"

"How did you disable the bank's alarms?"

"There was alarms?"

"Then how did you avoid triggering them?!"

He thought about this a long time. "Dunno," he said at last. "Luck, I reckon."

Luck. The word landed in my cathedral of logic like a brick through a stained-glass window.

Here is the truth I have spent my whole career refusing to learn: my method depends entirely on the assumption that criminals behave intelligently in their own interest. I predict what a clever man would do, and clever men, being predictable in their cleverness, oblige me. But Dennis Puddle did not act in his own interest. Dennis Puddle did not act on anything. He drifted through crime the way a leaf drifts through a river, and no mind, however great, can predict a leaf.

I could not anticipate his next move because he did not know his next move. I could not unravel his plan because there was no plan. Every theory I built, he demolished — not with cunning, but with the sheer, grinding, invincible force of having no idea what he was doing.

He was released on the bullion charge. The jury could not accept that a man who had tried to pay for a pasty with a stolen emerald — while a policeman stood behind him in the queue — could be legally described as a criminal mastermind. The judge called him "an act of God wearing a hat." He walked free.

I did not take it well. I locked myself in my study for a week and read every treatise on criminal psychology ever written. None of them covered Dennis.

Then, last Tuesday, a note arrived. It read: "i have stolen your watch. you will never catch me. yours truley, the napoleon of crime." My watch was, indeed, missing from my waistcoat.

I sat down. I cleared my mind. I abandoned every premise, every axiom, every hard-won principle of deduction. I asked myself not what a clever man would do, but what a man who once tried to mail himself to the seaside would do.

And for the first time in my life, I thought like an idiot.

I walked to the nearest pawnshop — the nearest, mind you, not the cleverest — and found my watch in the window, priced at four shillings, with a tag reading "slightly used, owner deceased." The pawnbroker described the seller: a man who had haggled the price *down*, believing four shillings sounded "more professional" than five.

I was waiting outside Dennis's mother's house in Croydon when he came home. He was wearing my waistcoat. He had forgotten to remove the watch from it, which meant he had, in effect, stolen my watch from himself and sold it back to me.

"Dennis Puddle," I said, "you are under arrest."

He looked at me with something approaching admiration. "Blimey," he said. "How'd you know I'd be here?"

"Because," I said, "it is where you live."

He nodded slowly, as though this were the cleverest thing he had ever heard. Perhaps, in his world, it was.

They say every great detective meets his match. Mine met me at the bottom of the stairs and asked me for directions up. I have never been so humbled, nor so certain of one thing: genius can be outwitted, reason can be defied, but stupidity — true, pure, weaponized stupidity — is the one lock for which no intellect can cut a key.

I keep the pawn ticket framed above my desk. It is the only case I ever solved by becoming, however briefly, the thing I despise.

It is also, I note with bitterness, the only one Dennis ever solved either.
