# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-14

## The Unpredictable Man

I have solved the Meridian Cipher Killings in eleven minutes. I deduced the Marbella Art Conspiracy from a smudge of varnish and a train timetable. I once predicted a hostage-taker's next three moves from the brand of his umbrella. In thirty years, the great criminal minds of the world have played chess against me, and I have never lost, because criminals are *systems*: motive, method, pattern. Give me a smart criminal and I will finish his sentence, his alibi, and his sentence.

Then came Gerald Plove.

The first crime was a bank robbery. Plove — unemployed, mild, once described by his own mother as "a disappointment with potential" — entered the Merchants' Trust wearing a colander on his head, carrying a *salmon*. Not a weapon in the salmon. Just a salmon. He walked to the counter and said, "I'd like to make a withdrawal of everyone's attention," and then held up the fish until the bank manager, weeping for reasons the hostage reports described as "unclear," opened the vault.

I built a model. Plove needed money; the salmon was bait for alarm; the colander defied facial recognition. Standard eccentric-layer. I predicted his next target: the jewelry district.

He robbed a *garden*. He dug up the mayor's prize hydrangeas at 2 a.m. and replaced them with identical hydrangeas. Identical! When I got the botanical forensics back — he'd indeed swapped them, why, WHY — I recalibrated. I built the Gerald Matrix: every variable I could find. His rent, his diet, his library card (he checked out *one book at a time*, always returned, always on Thursdays, always "The Big Book of Doors"). His purchases: 340 feet of garden hose, a tuba, a life-size cardboard cutout of a lighthouse, and — the outlier that broke my model and my sleep for a week — **fourteen porcelain pigeons, arranged alphabetically by expression**.

The Matrix said: escalator theft, next, Tuesday. He stole a *bus*. On Thursday. I was there — I had staked out the escalators in a janitor's disguise for two days, on the reasoning that even a stupid criminal, observed long enough, reveals pattern.

He walked past me, nodded politely, and stole the bus.

I found it, finally, parked in the botanical garden, hydrangeas loaded in the back seat. On the dashboard: a note. "Sorry about your Tuesday. This made more sense at the time. — G.P."

More sense *when*? When is "the time"? I ran the phrasing through every analysis I own. There was no sense to find. I had spent my life predicting men who wanted something — money, power, notoriety, revenge — and Plove wanted, as far as I could establish, *Tuesday to feel different*.

The City Bureau pleaded. Insurance actuaries wept. I requested a full psychological profile, and the state's examiner returned a single page that I keep framed in my study now: "Subject displays no diagnosable pathology. He is simply, sincerely, unpredictable. Recommend direct observation and humility."

Humility. From a government report.

So I did what I had never done: I abandoned prediction and took up *presence*. I followed him. Openly. I introduced myself, told him he had beaten me, and asked — the question that cost me more than any case — "What are you going to do next?"

He thought about it with visible effort. "I don't know," he said, glowing with sincerity. "That's the fun part."

"That's the — Gerald, you're being hunted by three agencies and two of them are scared of you."

"Scared of me?" He looked honestly alarmed. "Why? I fixed the escalator."

"You *stole* it."

"From the mall, yes. It was broken. It works now. It's in the garden, it's a lovely ride up to the hydrangeas." He frowned at my silence. "The mayor's mother couldn't climb the old steps anymore. Did you know that? Nobody visits her because of the steps. The garden is her favorite place. So now the escalator goes there."

I drove to the garden that night. There it stood, humming, hospital-handrails welded along the sides with a craftsman's care, riding gently up the hydrangea slope to a bench where the mayor's mother sat with her tea and the best view in the city. An old woman waved at me from the top. The whole thing — the salmon (to lure the city's attention away from the garden while he worked, he explained, because "mayors get weird about escalators"), the porcelain pigeons (stability), the hose (irrigation), the *tuba* (he'd always wanted to learn; the escalator ride is long).

I solved him, in the end. Not with my methods. There were none to use. I solved him the way you solve weather, or a child, or a Tuesday: I stood in the garden with my notebook closed, and I let the unpredictable man be unpredictable, and I wrote in my final report the sentence that ended my perfect record and began my better one:

*He cannot be predicted because he cannot be modeled: his method is kindness, at random, at scale. There is no pattern. There is only Plove.*

The Bureau keeps the file open as a warning to overconfident detectives. I keep the escalator token as a reminder.

He sent me a salmon last Christmas. It made perfect sense at the time.
