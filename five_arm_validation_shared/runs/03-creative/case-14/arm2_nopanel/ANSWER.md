# The Fool's Gambit

I have solved three hundred and forty-seven cases, and I have never once been wrong. I say this at the outset, because the record of what follows will look, to the uncharitable eye, like wrongness. It is not. It is the story of the one mind on this earth that my mind cannot model — not because it is greater than mine, but because it is, in the most literal sense I have ever used the word, nothing.

He called himself, in the single note he ever left me, "Chad."

The note was a grocery list.

I assumed, of course, that it was a cipher. The Great Chad — I never learned another name — announced his crimes in advance, as gentlemen did in the last century, but his announcements were things like "eggs, defrost ham, call about the gutter." I spent eleven days on the gutter. There was a gutter, it turned out, outside the flat of a woman he had once known; I had it taken apart brick by brick and found nothing but leaves and a teaspoon. The teaspoon was not his. I checked.

His crimes, you understand, were not difficult. That was the horror of them. A criminal who steals the crown jewels is a problem in geometry: guards, cameras, glass, weight. A criminal who steals nothing is a problem in metaphysics.

He stole nothing, mostly. Over one autumn he took: the left shoe from forty-one pairs in a single shop (leaving, in every case, the right); the taxidermied squirrel from the lobby of the Mercantile Bank; the brass numeral 4 from the door of number 42; and, from the city museum, one (1) decorative fern. The fern was plastic. The museum's director wept anyway, which I noted as the only rational response anyone in this affair has yet produced.

I had predicted the museum, you see. I had him nailed to the museum. Three weeks of surveillance, my best people, all of us watching a fern. He robbed the police evidence locker across the street instead — of a stapler. When we reviewed the tapes, we found he had walked past my surveillance team twice: once carrying the stapler, and once, for reasons never established, carrying the stapler in the other hand.

Do you begin to see? I want you to see, because it took me a month.

My method is not magic. It is arithmetic. Every criminal wants something, and wanting leaves a shape in the world: the shape of the shortest path between a man and his desire. Give me a theft and I will give you the thief's want, and from the want, the man. I have reconstructed entire lives from a smudged thumbprint and a lie about a train timetable. Desire is a current; I merely read the water.

Chad had no current. He was a pond.

I built him a profile anyway — I am not a quitter — and the profile was beautiful. Petty theft with no resale value suggested sentiment: each object a memory, each crime a love letter. The left shoes! Clearly a man who had lost a left shoe in childhood; some grief, some — my colleague Dr. Voss was in tears at the elegance of it. We predicted the shoe shop's warehouse. We waited in the warehouse among ten thousand shoes for six nights.

He stole the warehouse's clock. The clock, Voss said, represented time, which connected to the childhood grief, and I want it known that I did not strike her, though the file will show that I left the room.

Here is what I finally understood, at four in the morning, in a warehouse, holding a clock that had stopped: I had been running my arithmetic on a man who does not do arithmetic. Every model of human behavior assumes the human is trying. Chad was not trying. He was not even failing well. He was a random walk in a trench coat, and my great mind — that instrument which finds pattern in all things — had become a liability. A mind like mine cannot look at noise without hearing music. I had spent a month decoding a grocery list. The list said "eggs." I believe it meant eggs.

So I did the unthinkable. I stopped thinking.

Not entirely. I am not Chad. But I took a coin from my pocket — a 1971 florin, slightly bent, kept as evidence in the matter of the bent florin, case two hundred and nine — and at every intersection of my hunt, I flipped it. Where reason said the shoe shop and the coin said the docks, I went to the docks. My colleagues despaired. Voss wrote in her notes that I had "gone native."

On the ninth day of the coin, at a crossing by the canal, it came up tails twice, and I turned down a street I had no reason to enter, and there he was: a large, mild man in a trench coat, unscrewing the letter E from a sign that read CHEMIST.

"Stop," I said. "In the name of the law, and of reason, and of my own sanity — why the E?"

He looked at the E, and then at me, with the open, untroubled face of a man who has never once been asked to account for himself, and he said:

"Dunno. Liked it."

Gentle reader, I have interviewed assassins. I have sat across from a forger who could weep on demand in fourteen dialects. None of them ever told me anything so completely true. There was no motive. There was no cipher. There was a man, and an E, and a Tuesday.

I arrested him, of course. He went quietly, asking me on the way to the car whether I knew anywhere that did a good sandwich. I did. I took him to one. He is, whatever else, the only criminal I have ever dined with, and he ordered exactly what I ordered, which I choose not to interpret.

The case is closed. The E was returned to the CHEMIST, though the sign now reads CHMIST, because the glazier, in a fit of something I decline to call stupidity, mounted it one letter off, and the chemist insists it brings in customers.

I keep the coin in my pocket still. Not as a talisman — I do not believe in talismans; belief is for people without method. I keep it as a reminder of the one case in which my method failed, and of the last thing arithmetic can know: that the shortest path between two points does not exist for a man who is not going anywhere.

Heads, I would have caught him eventually.

Tails, I caught him sooner.
