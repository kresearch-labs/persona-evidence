# The Knock

The knock came at dusk, three soft raps, the way rain asks at a window.

He had been the last man on earth for one thousand and fourteen days. He knew the count because he kept it, the way a sailor keeps depth, marking each morning on the pantry door in pencil: a small, patient arithmetic that was the only ceremony left to him. On the first day of the third year he had stopped writing *day* before the number. The word had begun to feel like a boast.

He sat alone in a room — the sitting room, because the name still meant something — with a book open on his knee that he had read eleven times and would read again, because the alternative to reading was thinking, and thinking had a door in it, and behind that door was everything.

Then: the knock.

He did not move at first. Sound had gone out of the world the way heat goes out of a stone, slowly and then completely, and he had grown used to a silence so total he could hear his own blood working, the house settling its bones at night, wind testing the shingles like a burglar. A knock was a word from a language he had stopped speaking.

In the first months he had been the one who knocked. He had gone door to door through the town and then through the next town, four thousand doors by his estimate, knocking and waiting and knocking again, leaving chalk marks on the frames — a slash for no answer, a circle for answered and empty. He had developed a technique, three raps, pause, three more, loud enough to reach a cellar, gentle enough not to wake the dead, though waking the dead was precisely the hope he was entertaining and he knew it. He had stood on porches with his fist raised like a man praying in a posture no church had approved. He had called *hello* into houses until the word curdled in his mouth and became a sound with no one in it.

Then he had stopped, and the not-knocking had been the second death, worse than the first.

Now the knock had come to him, and he understood, sitting there with the book sliding off his knee, that he was afraid — not of what waited on the porch, but of the moment before knowing. As long as he did not open the door, the knock could be anything. It could be his daughter, ten years old again, playing the joke she used to play, hiding her giggle behind her hand. It could be the wind, which had learned to imitate everything the people had done. It could be a branch, a bird, the house dreaming aloud. As long as the door stayed shut, the whole world was still possible, and he had learned the hard way what the world did to possibility.

The knock came again. Three raps. Patient. Deliberate. A human interval between them — that was what undid him. Wind did not pause. Wind did not *mean*.

He rose. His knees announced him. In the hallway the light was the color of weak tea. He passed the mirror and did not look at it; he and the mirror had agreed years ago to be courteous to each other and no more.

His hand was on the latch when a thought arrived that made him close his eyes: that he had rehearsed this moment so many times, standing in this exact hallway, that the rehearsal had worn a groove, and now the real thing was falling into the groove, and he could not tell which of him was speaking when he said, in a voice sanded down to a whisper:

"Who is it?"

The pause on the other side of the door was long enough to hold a whole biography. Then a voice — a woman's voice, roughened, careful, the voice of someone who had not used it in a long time and was testing whether it still worked.

"I saw your smoke," she said. "Two days ago. I've been walking toward it since."

He stood with his forehead nearly touching the door. Through it, faintly, he could hear breathing, and under the breathing a smaller sound, a jingle, the click of claws on wood — a dog, a living dog, shifting its weight on his porch, which had carried no weight but weather for a thousand days.

"I knocked on every door in the valley," the voice said. "Every one. I marked them. Chalk."

"A slash for no answer," he said. "A circle for empty."

The silence on the porch changed quality then, the way a room changes when a stranger in it becomes a friend.

"I thought I was the last," she said.

"So did I," he said. "I kept count."

"What day is it?"

"One thousand and fourteen."

A small laugh came through the door, or the beginning of one, the kind that is half crying and has given up pretending otherwise. "I stopped at nine hundred," she said. "It felt like bragging."

He lifted the latch. This is what he noticed, and what he would remember: not her face, not the dog, not the last of the light — but the door itself, how light it was in his hand, how easily it swung, how all those years of stories about the end of the world had never once mentioned that the door would be so light, and open so simply, and that the hard part, the impossibly hard part, was only ever the hand that reached for it.

She was shorter than her voice. The dog was gray about the muzzle and immediately, shamelessly in love with him. Behind them the road ran dark into the valley, and on it, faint in the dusk, chalk marks on fence posts and door frames all the way down — slashes, and slashes, and then, near the gate, freshly drawn, a circle.

He had been the last man on earth for one thousand and fourteen days.

He was one for a moment longer, and then he wasn't.
