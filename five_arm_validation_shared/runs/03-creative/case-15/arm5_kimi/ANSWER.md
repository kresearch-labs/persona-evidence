# Inventory

Every morning, before anything else, he said his own name aloud.

"Martin Hale." Into the cold kitchen, into the breath-fog — so the name would not go the way of the street names, the way of his wife's maiden name, which he had lost one October and found again only by reading it off a letter he kept folded in his wallet like a photograph.

Eleven years alone, by his count, and the count was the thing. He counted cans in the pantry (four hundred and six), stairs to the second floor (thirteen), seconds between lightning and thunder in the summer storms that still came, indifferent, over the dead town. Numbers didn't leave. Numbers didn't get sick.

He was counting the spoons — a ritual without purpose, eleven spoons, there had once been twelve — when the knock came.

Three raps. Polite. Patient. The knock of someone who believed a door was a small thing.

He set down the spoon. The house, which had made its own sounds for eleven years — the ticks of cooling wood, the mice he never caught, the wind worrying the loose shingle — went silent, as if it too were listening.

Nobody knocked. In eleven years, nothing had knocked. Branches fell. Doors blew open. The world had a grammar of accident, and he had learned to read it, and this was not accident. Three raps has a shape. Three raps has a hand behind it.

He thought of the rifle in the hall closet. He thought of the letter in his wallet. He thought — and this shamed him, though there was no one to be shamed before — of the spoons, and how eleven was not a number that divided well.

The knock came again. Three raps. Not louder. Certain.

He stood in the kitchen doorway and looked down the hall at the front door, and the hall seemed very long, the way it had when his daughter was small and ran it in her socks, and he understood that the choice in front of him was not about the door at all.

Because here was the thing he had not let himself know: he had come to love it. Not the loneliness — the loneliness was a stone he carried — but the arrangement. The spoons. The count. The grief, shelved and labeled. He had made a museum of one, and he was its only visitor and its only exhibit, and a knock at the door was a crack in the glass.

"Hello?" A woman's voice, hoarse with disuse, breaking on the second syllable the way voices do when they have been talking only to themselves.

He knew that break. He heard it in his own name every morning.

His hand was on the deadbolt before he had decided anything. He stood with his palm flat against the lock, and his life came to him not as memory but as weight: his wife's head on his shoulder in the last spring, the hospital corridor, the long silence after the broadcasts stopped, the years of learning to be one thing instead of two.

On the other side of the door, the woman waited. He could hear her breathing. He could hear — and this undid him — that she was counting. Softly, under her breath. One, two, three. Counting his door, maybe. Counting her courage.

He thought: if I open this door, everything ends. The museum closes. The count breaks. I will have to be a person again, and a person is a thing that can be lost.

He thought: if I don't, I will hear that counting for the rest of my life.

His hand turned the deadbolt. The sound was enormous — a sound like a branch falling, like a door blowing open. The grammar of accident, except that it wasn't.

She was thin and gray-eyed and older than her voice, and she looked at him the way he looked at his pantry shelves, taking inventory of what the world had left.

"You're real," she said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't relief. It was an accusation and a prayer at once, and he understood it perfectly, because it was what he would have said.

"Martin Hale," he said. Not hello. His name. The thing he had kept alive for eleven years by saying it to no one — and now it had somewhere to go.

She told him hers, and he said it back to her, once, carefully, the way you set down something that might break. Then he stepped aside to let her in. Behind him, on the kitchen table, the spoons lay where he had left them, all eleven, uncounted, and the morning went on without the count.
