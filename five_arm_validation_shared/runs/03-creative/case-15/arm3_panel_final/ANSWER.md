# The Knock

The last man on earth sat alone in a room. There was a knock at the door.

He did not move at first. He had learned, over the eleven years since the hospitals emptied and the roads went quiet, that most sounds were the world settling — pipes contracting, shelves giving up their loads, wind finding a way in. The house had a whole vocabulary of abandonment, and he had memorized it. A knock was not in it.

It came again. Three knocks, patient, evenly spaced. The way a person knocks when they know someone is home.

He rose from the chair by the window, and his knees announced him the way they always did, and he crossed the room with his hand already out for the latch, because what else was there to do? You could sit and let the knocking go on forever, or you could open the door. Those were the only two philosophies left.

She was perhaps sixty, gray-haired, wearing a coat too heavy for the season, and she looked at him with an expression he had to search eleven years to find the name of. Relief. No — recognition. As if they had met before, though he was certain they had not.

"You're real," she said.

"I think so," he said.

"May I come in?"

He stepped aside. She moved through the house the way a woman moves through a house she has been in before, glancing at the kitchen doorway, the stairs, the window he had been sitting at. She did not ask about any of it. She sat down in the second chair, the one he had never moved, the one facing his, and folded her hands.

"I've been walking for a long time," she said. "House to house. Town to town. Most of them are empty. Some of them aren't."

"Empty," he said. "Yes."

"You're the first one who opened the door."

He wanted to say something about that — about the years of speaking aloud to keep the habit of language, about the calendar he still crossed off because the alternative was admitting the days had stopped mattering — but she was looking at him with such a plain, unhurried attention that excuses seemed beside the point. She had walked a long way. She was tired. That was all she had come to say.

"There's a settlement," she said, after a while. "West. Past the mountains, in the valley. Two hundred people, maybe more now. They send walkers out every season. To look."

"For what?"

"For anyone." She smiled a little. "For anyone who opens the door."

He looked at his hands. He thought about the valley, the green of it, the sound two hundred people must make just by being alive in one place — the dogs, the children, the argument over a fence line. He could see it so clearly it hurt, the way you can see a place you've never been.

"I'm not sure I remember how," he said. "People."

"Nobody does, at first." She stood, smoothed her coat. "I'll be here till morning. I sleep in the barn — old habit. If you want, come to the barn at first light. We walk west together. If you don't, I'll go on alone, and I'll mark this house as occupied." She said it like a kindness, and it was: occupied. Not empty. Someone lives here.

She let herself out. He heard her boots on the porch, then nothing, because the world outside was still a world of nothing, and would be until morning.

He sat in his chair by the window. The light went the way it always went, gold to gray to dark. He did not light the lamp. He sat in the dark and listened to the house, and for the first time in eleven years the house sounded to him like what it was: one man breathing in a room, keeping a knock company that had already been answered.

At first light he took down the lamp and the photograph and the calendar, and then he put them all back, because they were his, whoever he turned out to be. He filled the bottle. He locked nothing.

She was waiting by the gate, and the east was the color of a fresh page.

"West," she said.

"West," he said, and opened the door he had been standing behind for eleven years, and walked through it.
