## The Knock

The last man on earth sat alone in a room, and there was a knock at the door.

He had spent four months earning the right to be surprised, and he was. For the first year of the quiet, he'd knocked on every door in three counties — a foolish, human inventory of doors — and learned the truest thing about the end of the world: it wasn't the silence, and it wasn't the weeds in the highways, and it wasn't even the houses with supper still on the tables. It was that every knock went unanswered. He had filled the world's doorsteps with his own knuckles' work, unanswered, until he understood that being last means being the answer.

So he had stopped. He grew a garden where the post office used to be, and read the library in order, A through volume twenty-two of thecollected tax codes (he wept at volume twenty-two; grief finds strange furniture), and talked to the radio for an hour each evening at 7:00, saying the news aloud because someone should, even if the someone was the Mississippi.

The knock came on a Tuesday, at the door of the old schoolhouse where he now lived, because a man should live near proof of the future.

Three knocks. Patient. Unhurried. The knuckles of someone who expects an answer.

He stood, and did not go to the door at once — a year alone teaches you that hope is a fire you feed with small logs. He looked through the peephole first, the way you do, and saw nothing, because the peephole was built for eye-level and the knock had come from the bottom half of the door.

He opened it.

A dog. Golden, gray about the muzzle, thin as the last page of a letter, sitting on the schoolhouse step with the good manners of something that has knocked before. Behind it, up the road, moving at the pace of animals who have decided: *eleven more*. Dogs. A loose, traveling, ordinary pack of dogs, ranged along the fence, waiting.

"You're real," he said, and his voice was a ruin from disuse, and the dog wagged once, diplomatic.

He had stories for every animal in town. Now he was the story: the food-keeper, the porch-keeper, the man who read aloud at 7:00. The pack had followed the sound of his voice across four counties. He understood that later, from the evidence — the way they arrived not starving but *tired*, having walked as a decision; having heard, somewhere in the long nights, a human voice reading aloud into the dark, and turned toward it like plants.

Here is what the last man learned, and he would tell you it was the lesson of the whole terrible year, worth every unanswered door:

The knock was never a threat. Nothing in the empty world comes to take from you; the world has plenty now. The knock comes because *a knock is a question asked politely by the lonely*, and he had forgotten, in his terrible inventory of the dead, that loneliness was never his alone. The dogs had been knocking on doors for a year. Houses with someone in them — that was the thing the whole animal world was looking for. Light, voice, the smell of coffee, an answer.

He fed them. He talked to them while they ate, telling them about the tax codes (volume twenty-two is nothing but exceptions), and the dogs listened with the profound tact of their species, and when the food was gone they arranged themselves around the schoolroom like a committee, and the smallest one slept on his feet, and he understood that he was no longer the last man on earth.

He was, instead, the first citizen of a new town, elected by acclamation, staffed by dogs.

He fixed the doorbell that night, because a knock that good deserved better manners from the house. And in the ledger he kept now — habit from volume twenty-two — he wrote the day's entry:

*Population: 1 human, 12 dogs, 1 doorbell (operational).*

*Note: I answered. That was the whole trick. All year, the world was knocking.*

*Answer your door.*
