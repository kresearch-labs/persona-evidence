# The Eight of Forever

In the year 5024, give or take a calendar reform, Joren Vale stood before his seminar class in the Hall of Deep Antiquities with an object in his hands and nothing in his head.

"Third Era," he began, because you always begin with the date when you don't know anything else. "Roughly three thousand years old. Recovered from the flooded strata of a coastal settlement, in a structure the excavators believe was either a temple or a Toymart. The two are architecturally identical."

A few students laughed. Good. Laughter bought time.

He held it up. A black sphere, about the size of a grapefruit, heavy as a stone. On one side, a circle containing the numeral 8. On the other, a round window of cloudy blue liquid, in which a many-sided white shape drifted like a drowned moon.

"The sacred eight," Joren said. "Note that when laid on its side, it becomes the ancient symbol for forever. This was not a coincidence. Nothing in the Third Era was a coincidence, according to Professor Henn, who believes this object was funerary." Professor Henn believed everything was funerary, but Joren left that out.

He turned it over in his hands. "The interior contains a polyhedron — twenty faces, a holy number — suspended in an alchemical fluid. Each face bears an inscription. When the vessel is agitated, one face rises to the window and delivers a text. The ancients, we believe, consulted it."

This was, he had to admit, a guess. Everything was a guess. The Third Era had left behind mountains of garbage and almost no context, and the whole field of Deep Antiquity was essentially the science of guessing about garbage with footnotes.

"Some scholars," he continued, "hold that it was a navigational instrument, the blue fluid representing the sea. Others —" he glanced at his notes, where he had written the word *others* followed by nothing — "others disagree."

He had prepared three theories. He had believed in each of them for about an hour, the way you believe in a pen until it runs out.

Theory one: religious oracle. Evidence: the inscriptions were short, solemn, and maddeningly vague, which was true of every religion ever catalogued. Theory two: gaming device. Evidence: the twenty-sided die resembled those found in the tombs of the era's adolescent males, alongside painted figurines and something called a *Player's Handbook*. Theory three, whispered to him by his roommate at two in the morning: it was a paperweight. He had nearly wept, because it was the best theory of the three.

"The inscriptions," he said, "are our primary source. I will read from the canon." He shook the sphere, as the excavation report said to do, and read what rose to the window. "'Signs point to yes.'"

The class wrote it down. He watched them write it down, twenty students solemnly transcribing *signs point to yes*, and felt something shift in the room — the particular silence of people deciding to be moved.

"'Concentrate and ask again,'" he read. "'My reply is no.' 'Without a doubt.' 'Very doubtful.'" He looked up. "You will notice the texts do not explain themselves. They answer. Which means the ancients were asking. Three thousand years later, we have their answers and have lost every one of their questions."

He had not planned to say that. It was the first true thing in the speech, and it landed the way true things do: heavily.

"So what is it?" he said. "The assignment asks me to tell you. Here is my honest report." He set the sphere down. "I don't know. I've read Henn, I've read Xu, I've read the excavation logs, and I don't know. It might be a god. It might be a toy. In the Third Era the difference may not have been meaningful — they built cathedrals to teams that played games, and fought wars over toys that played at being gods."

A beat. He was off his notes now entirely, which was either disaster or the other thing.

"What I believe — and this is not in the literature, so cite me at your own risk — is that it doesn't matter what it answered. It matters that they asked. They held the future in both hands, like this, and shook it, and waited for something to rise up out of the blue and tell them yes or no. We do the same to them. Every excavation is a question. Every shard is an answer we can't read. They are our oracle now. And when we hold up their world and ask what it meant, mostly what rises to the window is —" he picked it up and shook it, once more, and read — "'Reply hazy, try again.'"

The silence lasted three seconds, which in a seminar room is a standing ovation.

Afterward, Professor Ila — who had lived through two calendar reforms and was rumored to be funerary herself — stopped him at the door. "Henn will say you got it wrong," she said. "Henn says everything is a tomb." She almost smiled. "You got one thing right. They asked. So do we. B-plus."

That night, in his dorm, Joren sat with the sphere on his desk, the blue fluid settling, the white moon asleep at the bottom. He picked it up. He felt foolish. He did it anyway.

"Will I pass the course?" he asked, and shook it.

The many-sided shape turned over slowly in the dark, rising toward the window the way everything rises toward the light, and when it arrived he read the answer the ancients had left for him, three thousand years ago, personally:

WITHOUT A DOUBT.
