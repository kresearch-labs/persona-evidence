# Vessel 4471

The night before my Ordeal I sat in the reading room with the object in front of me and asked it, out loud, what it was.

It said nothing. It has said nothing for three thousand years, and I saw no reason to expect a change of policy at midnight.

In the morning I would stand before the assembled second year and the preceptors and speak for a quarter hour on a single artifact of the Anterior Age. The artifact is assigned by lot, on the theory that a historian must be able to speak of anything, and the lot had drawn me a ceramic vessel of no particular rarity, catalogued as Vessel 4471, recovered from a landfill stratum beneath a city that once stood on the northern shore of a sea which has since moved.

It is a cylinder of fired clay, eleven centimeters tall, glazed white, with a looped handle on one side. The interior is stained a faint brown. Around the belly, in painted blue letters that the firing half erased, in Late English, are the words:

WORLD'S BEST DAD

I have been staring at those words for three weeks. I will tell you what I told the Round Hall, and then I will tell you the truth, which is not the same thing.

I began as we are taught to begin, with the physical facts, which are the only things that cannot be taken from you. The vessel was slip-cast, not thrown: its walls are even, its form is one of thousands, perhaps millions. It was cheap when it was made. This matters. The Anterior Age mass-produced everything, including its sacred objects — or its jokes; as I argued, the distinction may not have existed.

The handle says the vessel was lifted while full, and lifted often. The glaze is worn dull on the rim and on the curve of the handle where a hand would close. Residue analysis found traces of a dark, bitter infusion — the Anterior Age drank many such, for stimulation, in quantities that frightened their own physicians. So: a drinking vessel, used daily, for years. For decades, the wear says. Decades is a long time to keep a cheap vessel, in an age that threw away almost everything.

Then the words.

"Dad" is attested across the whole corpus, always near words for home, for authority, for small children. The orthodox reading is a household deity — a genius of the hearth, a protector of the dwelling. The heterodox reading is that it meant simply the male parent. We cannot decide, because the Anterior Age wrote everything down and saved almost nothing; they kept their records on devices that lasted forty years, which is why we know the names of their kings and not the contents of their kitchens.

"World's Best" is an epithet, a superlative, in the formula of a prize. Compare the prize amphorae of the ancient games: *I am the prize of the contest*, they say, filled with sacred oil. And so, as others have argued before me, there must have been contests — annual, widespread — in which fathers competed, and the victor's household commissioned a vessel.

But the vessel is cheap, and mass-produced, and millions were made. No contest strikes a million medals. So I offered the only reading I could make fit: the contest was private, and domestic, and judged by children, and there was a victor in every household that kept one. Every father could be world's best, somewhere, to somebody. The vessel did not record a victory. It declared one.

Then I reached the chip.

There is a chip at the base, old damage, a small pale crescent in the glaze. It was dropped early — the wear resumed over the scar. And it was not thrown away. In that stratum the excavators found thousands of vessels discarded intact, whole, abandoned. This one was dropped, chipped, and kept, and used, and lifted and set down, lifted and set down, some tens of thousands of times — and then, at the end, for reasons we will never know, it was thrown away anyway, or lost, or left behind in a house that emptied.

I said all of this to the Round Hall in a clear voice, and it was well received, and then Preceptor Anse asked the question she asks everyone, the question we are trained to dread.

"And what was it for?"

And I said: I don't know.

I said it plainly, because there was nothing left to dress it in. I can tell you what it was made of, and when, and how many of its siblings were fired alongside it. I can tell you it was held, and heated, and filled, and dropped, and kept. I cannot tell you what it was. Every commentary disagrees with every other and with nothing in the ground. I have held it every day for three weeks and it has told me nothing, and I am no longer sure the not-knowing is a failure. Perhaps it is the subject. Three thousand years is long enough to lose the contents of a kitchen.

The hall went quiet in a way I had not earned. Anse looked at me a moment and wrote something down, and I still do not know whether it was mercy or a mark against me.

Here is the last thing, which I did not say aloud. The brown stain in the vessel matches, molecule for molecule, the infusion I drink every morning before the reading room opens. Whatever else the three thousand years took, they did not take that. I stood there with my paper cup and wanted, more than I have ever wanted anything in this discipline, to pour it into Vessel 4471 and lift it by the handle and drink — the way it was made to be held, the way ten thousand mornings had held it before mine.

I did not. I drank from the paper cup and put the vessel back in its foam, because I still don't know the rules.

But somebody gave it, and somebody kept it, and for decades it was the world's best at being what it was. Of that much, at least, the ground and I are certain.
