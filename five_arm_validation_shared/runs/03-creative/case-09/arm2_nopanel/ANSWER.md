# The Button

The object sits on the lectern next to my notes, and it is smaller than I remembered, and in nine minutes I am supposed to tell four hundred people what it is.

Good evening. My name is Ansel Vey, third-year material history, and tonight I present Catalog Object 88-2214: a black glass rectangle recovered from a dwelling stratum beneath the old northern suburbs, sealed in a layer we date to the twenty-first century of the Common Era. Three thousand years ago. Give or take a lifetime.

I want to begin with an admission, because Professor Okafor tells us an honest speech beats a confident one, and because you would hear it in my voice anyway: I do not know what this object is. Nobody does. I have spent a term with it, and the honest speech I have is about why.

Here is what we can say. It is a flat rectangle, a hand and a half long, thin as a finger is wide. The face is black glass, unbroken except at the bottom, where a single circle sits pressed into the body — the only control the object has. The back is pale metal, and set into it is one small round lens. At the base there is an oval opening, which we believe is where something was once inserted, though nothing that fits it survives. There are no hinges. No blades. No moving parts at all. When we shake it, nothing rattles. It weighs about as much as a meal.

Three thousand years is long enough to erase almost anything, and it erased this civilization with unusual thoroughness, and here is the paradox I want to hand you tonight: the twenty-first century called itself the Information Age. It wrote down more than every age before it combined — every face, every song, every argument, billions of times over. And almost none of it came through. They kept it on spinning disks and cells that hold a charge for years, not centuries, and the network it traveled on dissolved the moment the power stopped. The clay of Babylon outlasted it. We have their plastics and their glass and their garbage, whole landfills of packaging, and not one complete book from the century that drowned in words. Whatever this object was, it stood at the center of that flood. And it is silent.

So we speculate. I will give you the three leading theories, and then I will tell you which one I believe, which is none of them.

The first theory calls it a religious icon. Nearly every household had one; some had several, in graduated sizes. They were carried on the body, kept by the bed, handed to children to quiet them. The glass face gives back your own reflection — an instrument of self-regard, the theory says, a mirror you could worship. The single button shows wear consistent with tens of thousands of presses: a rosary of taps. The lens on the back faces away from the owner, outward at the world — the eye of a god, or the eye of a witness. People sheathed them in leather and printed skins, the way we clothe icons. When one broke, it was mourned. There are graves where a child was buried holding one.

The second theory calls it a writing slate. This is the theory I defended in my seminar paper, and Professor Okafor dismantled it in red ink in eleven minutes. It is true the size suits a tablet, that the face was touched constantly, that some specimens bear residues in patterns like smudged script. But no stylus has ever been recovered with one, and the surface carries no engraving. The words, if there were words, lived inside the object — held on materials that died with it. *A book that forgets*, Okafor wrote in the margin, and gave me a B. I have come to think the margin was the best part of my paper.

The third theory calls it currency. It had value — we agree on that — because people protected it, traded it, stole it. But currency you spend and forget. This, people kept for years, and repaired, and replaced only reluctantly, and the replacement was an event. You do not weep over a coin.

Here is what I believe, and it is not a theory, because it explains nothing. On the glass of this specimen, in the residue layer, the conservation team lifted a partial thumbprint. It sits just above the button, slightly left, exactly where a thumb would fall if you held the object the way we think its owner held it — in one hand, face up, thumb ready. The print is small; probably a woman's, the analysts say, though they will not commit. There is also a scratch on the back, a shallow crescent, the kind a key makes in a pocket. And on the case that came with it — the case survived, leather, cracked — someone had fixed a small printed image of a cat. A cartoon cat. Orange. Grinning.

Someone held this thing every day for years. Their thumb wore a road across the glass. They carried it so constantly that they armored it in leather and decorated the armor with a joke. It was, whatever it was, a companion. It may have shown them faces. It may have spoken. It may have listened — and I suspect listening is underrated in our theories, because we are historians and we only ever imagine ourselves talking.

So here is my conclusion, and I warn you it is not a conclusion. I do not know what this object is. If it was a lamp, it has gone out. If it was a book, it has forgotten everything. If it was a god, it has stopped answering. If it was a window, someone boarded it from the inside.

But this morning, before the hall opened, wearing gloves, in the conservation room with the lights down, I pressed the button. The way she did — the thumbprint's owner — ten thousand times a day, without once thinking about it, the way you do a thing that always works.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. Three thousand years is long, the little cell at its heart is dead, and whatever it knew, it knew only while it was awake.

But I want to be honest with you about the moment. I pressed it, and nothing happened, and I pressed it again — and every one of you leaned forward just now, hearing that. Four hundred historians, and one dead black slab, and for half a second we were all doing exactly what she did on some ordinary morning three thousand years ago: pressing the button, and waiting for it to wake.

That is my speech. Thank you. I still don't know what it is. I know she loved it, and for one evening in a dark hall, I think we understood her.
