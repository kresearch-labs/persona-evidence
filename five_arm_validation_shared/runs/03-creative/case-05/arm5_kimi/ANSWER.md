# The Sentence

Everyone at the Vanguard agreed on two things: Dana Voss was the nicest person any of them had ever met, and no one wanted to be in the room when she worked.

She brought muffins on Mondays. Blueberry, because Dr. Chrono liked blueberry. She remembered birthdays, allergies, the names of everyone's kids. She once stayed up until three in the morning helping Glacier assemble a crib, and the crib was perfect, and Glacier cried a little, because that was the kind of person Dana was.

Her codename, assigned by PR before anyone understood what she did, was Sweetheart. By the time anyone understood what she did, it was too late to change it, and Dana said she thought it was lovely, and everyone felt worse.

The morning Baron Bedlam took the harbor, Dana was baking.

By the time she arrived — still in her apron, flour on one sleeve — the Baron had sunk two ferries, raised a neutron obelisk in the middle of the bay, and thrown Captain Valiant through a bus. He was standing on the obelisk's viewing platform, monologuing, the way he always did, about the smallness of mankind.

Dana set the muffin tin on a parked car, climbed the rubble, and waited politely for him to finish. He didn't. So she said, in her ordinary voice, the voice she used for asking about people's mothers:

"You rehearsed that speech in the mirror this morning. Your father used to practice his in the same mirror, and his was better, and you know it, and that's the part of all this you can never say out loud."

Baron Bedlam sat down on the platform. He was still sitting there when the police arrived, and he asked one of the officers for a blanket, and then he asked if he could use the phone to call his mother.

Captain Valiant, who had been through a bus, watched from the stretcher. "I've been hit by a bus," he said, to no one. "I'd take the bus again."

The Vanguard had rules about Dana. Not official rules — nothing HR would sign off on, although HR had a file — but rules. Don't let Dana come to the briefing. Don't let Dana do the negotiating. If Dana tilts her head to the left, the conversation is over and someone is about to have a very bad decade.

The thing nobody said at the briefings was that the villains weren't the problem. Villains, you could hit. The problem was watching Dana work and realizing, in the silence after one of her sentences, that she knew yours too. She knew everyone's. She walked around headquarters all day, refilling coffee and asking about your kids, carrying the exact sentence that would fold you in half, and choosing — every hour of every day — not to say it.

Kid Comet asked her once. He was nineteen and fearless in the way of people who have never been seen clearly. He came down to breakfast in his new costume and said, "Be honest, does it look cool?"

Dana was tired. That was her excuse afterward, and everyone accepted it, because the alternative was that she was only human. She glanced up from her tea and said, "It looks like you're still hoping your brother shows up to something."

Kid Comet ate his cereal facing the wall. He didn't speak for two days. Dana made his favorite soup and left it outside his door with a note that said *I'm so sorry*, and she was, and that was somehow the worst part: she meant it. She always meant it. The nicest person in the world and the most dangerous, and they were the same person, and nobody at the Vanguard could figure out how to hold both of those thoughts at once.

Then the Hollow Man came.

He arrived without a past. No family, no record, no attachments — a man who had, by his own account, cut out everything soft in himself and burned it. He didn't monologue. He didn't want anything, which made him the first villain in Vanguard history that nobody could negotiate with, and he dismantled the team in eleven minutes, methodically, like a man taking apart a clock.

And the terrible thing — the thing nobody said afterward, in the infirmary, over Dana's muffins, which everyone ate because they were upset and the muffins were very good — was the relief. Finally, a fight. Finally a villain Dana couldn't touch, because there was nothing in him left to crush.

Dana listened to all of this quietly. Then she asked where he was.

They found him at the reservoir, building something vast and gray that was going to switch off every heart in the city like a breaker. The team hung back — new rule, unspoken: let Dana go first, and don't watch too closely — and Dana walked out across the dam alone, in her cardigan, and stood in front of the Hollow Man, and looked at him for a long time.

"There's nothing you can say," he told her. "I checked. I made sure."

"I know," Dana said gently. "You didn't cut out your heart. You just never found anyone who wanted it."

The machine kept humming. The Hollow Man did not sit down, the way Baron Bedlam had. He stood very still for almost a minute, and then he turned the machine off himself, carefully, the way you would close a door in a house where someone was sleeping.

Monday. Muffins. Kid Comet took one, which he hadn't in six weeks, and sat down across from Dana, and didn't quite look at her.

"Do I already know mine?" he asked. "My sentence."

"Yes," Dana said. "That's why it would work."

He nodded slowly. He ate the muffin. It was blueberry, and it was pretty good, and after a while he said, "My brother's coming to the game Saturday," and Dana said, "I know, sweetheart. You've been smiling different all week."

Across the room, Captain Valiant watched her refill the coffee pot, and shook his head with something that was not quite fear and not quite love, and said, to no one, "Please never change."

He meant it both ways.
