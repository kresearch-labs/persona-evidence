# The Gentle Art

Everyone liked Juniper. That was the problem.

She brought muffins to briefings — blueberry, still warm, with the little lemon-zest thing she did. She remembered that Hovergirl's cat was named Chairman Meow and asked after his arthritis. When I got concussed in March she sat by my hospital bed and read me the good parts of a novel, skipping the boring parts, because she knew which parts were boring. By every measurement the League had, Juniper was the nicest person on the roster.

She was also, and I say this with love, the most frightening.

Her gift wasn't strength or flight or anything you could put on a poster. Juniper instinctively knew the exact thing to say to a person to crush them. Not insult. Not defeat. Crush. She would open her mouth, say one quiet sentence, and a grown adult would sit down on the nearest available surface and begin reconsidering their life.

The first time I saw it, we were stopping a bank job. Nothing serious — six guys in ski masks, one of them clearly new, holding the door gun sideways. Standard Tuesday. Juniper drifted over to the new one, the way she does, like she was about to offer him a muffin, and said:

"You're actually very good at this, and I don't think anyone's ever told you, and that's why you're here on a Saturday wearing wool in July."

The guy took off his mask. "It is July," he said, in a small voice. Then he sat on a marble planter, put the gun down, and told her about his brother-in-law for eleven minutes while the rest of us zip-tied his coworkers. He waved goodbye when we left. He asked if she did weekends.

"That's not a power," said Hovergirl afterward, in the way people say a thing is not a power when they are trying very hard to believe it. "That's just being observant."

It was a power. We all knew it was a power. Nobody said so, because the alternative was admitting how it felt to watch.

It's hard to describe. It's like watching someone perform surgery using only eye contact. She never raises her voice. She never says anything cruel. Half the time what she says isn't even an insult — it's just true, in a direction you didn't know truth could point. And you stand there, a bystander, feeling your own skeleton get quietly inventoried, and you weren't even the target.

The big one was Doctor Entropy.

You remember Doctor Entropy. Big gray coat, big gray machine, monologue about the heat death of the universe delivered from a hovering platform above City Hall. He'd been building toward this for two years. We'd stopped him four times; each attempt got more elaborate and more monologuey. This time the machine was the size of a cathedral and he had a laser pointer for his slides.

He got to the part about how entropy always wins, how everything you love is just order waiting to be undone, and the crowd was getting that glazed look, and Hovergirl whispered, "Somebody shut him up," and then everybody looked at Juniper, and Juniper said, "Okay," and floated up to the platform.

The whole square went quiet. I want to be clear that we were all a little afraid, and none of us were in danger.

She waited for him to finish his slide. She let him have the pause. Then she said, gently, so gently:

"You invited your father to the demonstration in April, didn't you. The small one, in the lab. And he said he'd try, and you've been checking the door ever since, and that's why the machine has a viewing gallery."

Doctor Entropy did not move for a long moment.

"There's a viewing gallery," he said, "because of safety regulations."

"You put in the windows in March," said Juniper. "He said 'finally' at your graduation. One word. You've been building a machine loud enough that you can't hear it ever since."

I have never seen a man deflate in a public setting the way Doctor Entropy deflated over City Hall. The coat just — settled. He switched off the laser pointer, which somehow was the worst part, and looked at the machine the size of a cathedral, and said, "I'm going to need to sit down," and then he sat down, on the platform, in front of everyone, and cried into his own collar.

The machine was switched off by four p.m. He surrendered, cooperated fully with the authorities, and asked — quietly, at the end, not making eye contact — whether she did private consultations. She said she'd have to check her calendar, because she'd promised to help Hovergirl repaint, and that was somehow the most devastating thing anyone said all day.

The debrief that night was not a normal debrief.

"I hated that," said Hovergirl. "I want to be clear. I hated every second."

"You cried," I said.

"I cried in a supportive way. There's a difference. I felt it in my chest and it wasn't even about me, Atlas. Do you understand? I was a bystander and I felt inventoried."

"Skull got inventoried," agreed Bolt from the couch, face down. "I wasn't even on the roof. I was in the van. I heard it through the comms and my skull got inventoried."

"She said six words," said Hovergirl, "and I watched a man's entire childhood leave his body through the ears."

Juniper passed the muffins around and asked if anyone wanted tea, because she is nice, and she is nice, and that's the part that gets you.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about a person who knows exactly what to say: it works both ways.

Later, walking home, I asked her. I don't know why. Maybe the van conversation had gotten under my skin. I said, "What would you say to me? If you had to."

She thought about it for maybe two seconds, which I appreciated, and then she said, "You keep the second-best muffin for yourself every time and give the best one away, and you think nobody notices, and everyone notices, and it's the best thing about you."

I cried into a blueberry muffin on a public bench at eleven at night, is what I'm saying. The lemon-zest thing. It's excellent.

So, yes. Juniper is the nicest person on the roster, and I would follow her anywhere, and I hope — genuinely, sincerely hope — that I am never on the other side of her. At least when she's crushing a supervillain, you get to enjoy it.
