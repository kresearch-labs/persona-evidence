# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-05

## The Nicest Bad News in the Business

The thing people got wrong about Harmony Vale — teammate, bruiser, immune to irony — was that she wasn't scary. She was *kind*. Devastatingly, surgically kind. The kind of person who remembered your dog's name and your court date and asked about both.

That was the problem. Her superpower was that she always knew exactly what to say to crush someone. Not insults — she was terrible at insults, once tried "your mother was a lizard" against a shape-shifter and had to apologize when it turned out to be true. No: Harmony's gift was finding the one true sentence that a person had spent their whole life bracing against, and saying it *gently*.

Supervillains could survive lasers. They could not survive being understood.

Take Doctor Vellum, who had frozen the city's water supply in immortal gold. Clockwork golden rain fell on everything, turning pigeons and parking meters into beautiful, useless statues. The hero team — me, Harmony, Starlance, and the twins — breached his observatory lair, and Starlance did the normal thing first.

"Vellum! Your reign of gilded tyranny ends now!"

Vellum, perched on his orrery, smirked. "The city will thank me when it appreciates beauty." Standard villain monologue, maybe forty seconds in.

"Vellum," Harmony said quietly, stepping forward, filing her voice down to something warm. "Your mother was a clockmaker, wasn't she? The one who died waiting for you to visit?"

The orrery stopped.

"She polished brass the way other people pray," Harmony went on, tilting her head. "And you became an art-forger because you couldn't stand that nothing you made ever gleamed like her work. Every gold thing you freeze, you're trying to finish her last polish. But it doesn't work, does it? Because she didn't love the shine. She loved the *mending*. And you've never mended anything in your life."

Vellum made a small sound like a music box dying. He sat down on the orrery's base. Starlance lowered her lance, visibly uncomfortable, the way she always got — like she'd walked in on her parents discussing feelings.

"Anyway," Harmony added, "the water thing is hurting people, and you know it, so we're going to take the scepter now, and I think you should call your brother. He's been leaving messages. You never listen to them, and it's the only voicemail you keep."

We took the scepter. Vellum came quietly, weeping about brass polish.

"You could just *punch* them," Starlance said afterward, while Vellum sat quietly in the containment van, dabbing his eyes with a gold handkerchief.

"And then they'd come back," Harmony said, apologetic. "You know how it is."

We did know. Her villains didn't come back. Some sent thank-you notes.

The worst — the *best* — was Doctor Static. Eight feet of crackling menace, floating above the exchange, threatening to blink the city into permanent blackout. He had force fields we couldn't dent and a monologue about the weakness of human connection.

Harmony walked to the front and looked up at him with nothing but sympathy.

"Peter," she said. (His name was Gerald, but he had picked *Doctor Static*, and using the name he'd chosen for himself was part of the kill shot.) "You built the suit because in your apartment, when the power goes out, the refrigerator stops humming, and the silence is where your mother's arguments used to live. You can't sleep in a quiet room. You're not threatening the city's power grid. You're drowning out a memory."

The electricity around him guttered like a candle deciding.

"And the suit *is* impressive," she added. "Your father would have been proud of it. He'd just also want you to call him back. He doesn't actually care about the patents."

Doctor Static landed. The blackout threat dissolved. Starlance had to go stand by the van for a minute and breathe.

"Was that *necessary*?" she asked, gesturing at what remained of a grown man's emotional perimeter.

"He surrendered," Harmony said, bewildered, like it was a coincidence. "Less property damage. Nobody even got punched."

The uncomfortable truth — the one we all knew and none of us said in front of her, because she was *nice* — was that watching Harmony work was like watching a surgeon who could also, incidentally, do the thing with the eyes that makes people confess. She never lied to anyone. That was the horror of it. Every devastating sentence was *true*, delivered with the exact softness that made it land. Superheroes lose fights. Harmony's opponents lost *arguments with themselves*, in public, gently.

Later that night, dispatch called: the Puppeteer of Prosperity — a guy who'd enslaved the financial district with enchanted wallets — had turned himself in at the precinct, unasked, and was asking specifically for "the kind one." Something about "she'd understand that it was really about my brother."

"She's not a therapist," Starlance grumbled, logging the scene.

"No," I said, watching Harmony autograph the Puppeteer's confession form with a little smiley face, "she's a mirror that knows where you hide the stuff. The therapy's just what happens afterward."

The city got quieter after that. Fewer explosions, more existential reckonings. Our win rate went up; our reputation at villain bars got complicated. And Harmony — nice Harmony, gentle Harmony, who brought muffins to stakeouts — would pat your arm on the way to the fight and say, in that soft voice, exactly the sentence you'd been dreading since childhood.

But only to the bad guys. Mostly.
