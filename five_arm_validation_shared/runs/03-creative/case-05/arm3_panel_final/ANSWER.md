Everyone thinks my superpower is the flying. It isn't. The flying is fine. The flying is honestly the third most interesting thing about me, behind the muffins and the thing I'm about to tell you.

My real superpower is that within about ninety seconds of meeting someone, I know the exact sentence that will take them apart at the seams. Not an insult. Insults are easy and they bounce off. I mean the sentence. The one true thing said at the one worst moment, in the one tone they can't defend against.

I want to be clear that I'm a nice person. I bring muffins to stakeouts. I remember the interns' birthdays. I once spent a whole weekend helping Captain Radiant alphabetize her trophy shelf, and I liked it, and we found a trophy she'd won in 2011 that she'd been looking for. But the universe handed me this one specific gift, and it is not a nice gift. It only sounds nice when I say it.

Example. Last spring the Vantablack Duke parked a doomsday device on the roof of City Hall and gave the city until midnight to acknowledge him as its rightful genius. Standard stuff. The team went up. The Duke beat us the way he always beats us, which is thoroughly and while explaining his backstory, and by eleven we were lying in various attitudes of defeat among the rooftop gravel while he monologued about the machine that would make the city remember his name forever.

And I lay there listening — I'm a good listener, it's part of the whole package — and I heard the shape of it. The science fair. The brother. The judges who gave first place to a volcano that was, frankly, also my sentence, but that's his burden, not mine.

So I got up. I brushed the gravel off my costume. I straightened my cardigan, because I wear a cardigan over the costume, it's a whole thing, and I floated over to him and I said, kindly, the way you'd tell someone they have spinach in their teeth:

"The city was always going to forget the science fair. Cities forget everything. But your brother never did — he named his son after you, and you'd know that if you'd gone home even once in nineteen years."

The Duke's hand came off the detonator. He sat down on the roof ledge. He said, "Dennis named his kid Theo?" in a very small voice, and then the man who had held this city hostage eleven times put his face in his gloves and cried, and his chief henchman Carlos patted him on the shoulder and said, "You should call your brother, boss," and then Carlos called his own dad, right there, at 11:40 p.m., and they talked for an hour, and the device just sort of powered down from neglect.

We didn't even have to fight. Wrenchwitch says that's the worst part.

Because here's the thing about watching me work: my teammates hate it. Not me — me they like, muffins, birthdays, etcetera. But when I do the thing, they all get this look, like they're watching someone perform surgery with a compliment. Captain Radiant described it as "polite defusing, except the bomb is a person." Wrenchwitch said, "It's like watching you pet a dog and casually read its diary out loud." They made a rule after the Duke. Rule number nine, actually, right under "no wishing on mysterious artifacts": Bubbles does not accompany the team to interrogations, negotiations, or hostage situations, on the grounds that the last hostage situation ended with a bank robber enrolled in community college and weeping openly about his mother.

They also renamed the group chat "What Did Bubbles Say." I find this hurtful and accurate.

The thing they don't get — the thing I can't explain without sounding like a fortune cookie — is that the power was never about saying the sentence. Anybody can say a sentence. The power is that I know it, and then I carry it around. I know Captain Radiant's. It's about her father, and the medal, and I have known it for six years and I have never once said it, because she's my friend and she's happy, or as happy as a woman who alphabetizes trophies gets. I know Wrenchwitch's. I know the mayor's, and the mayor's is genuinely devastating, and sometimes at budget meetings I have to bite the inside of my cheek to keep it in. Being nice isn't not knowing how to hurt people. It's knowing exactly how, and choosing muffins instead.

Villains don't get the muffin version, though. Villains get the work.

Madame Midnight took the bridge last month. Actual bridge, actual midnight, very theatrical. She'd studied us. She had countermeasures for Captain Radiant's light, dampeners for Wrenchwitch's tech, a whole folder on everyone's weaknesses, and she stood at the center of the span and told us we were predictable.

She had not studied me, because I'm not in the folder. I'm in the appendix.

I floated out onto the bridge, and I straightened my cardigan, and I said: "You didn't take the bridge because you needed it. You took it because it was your mother's commute, and if the city can't keep a bridge open, it can't keep a promise, and you've decided that means she was right to leave."

The lights on her rig dimmed. She said, "It was her commute," to nobody, and then she untied the bridge, and then she asked, very quietly, if that was obvious from the outside, and I said, "Only to me," which is the nicest true thing I know how to say.

The team watched from the shore. Nobody cheered. Captain Radiant had both hands over her mouth. Wrenchwitch said, "I felt that from here," and she meant it badly, but I'll take it.

The Duke sends a card at the holidays now. He's a school science teacher. Dennis and Theo visit. Carlos's dad comes to the recitals.

And every so often, one of the heroes gets a few drinks in and sidles up to me and says, casually, way too casually, "So. Hypothetically. If someone wanted to know their — "

And I always say no, and hand them a muffin, and they always take it, and they always look relieved, and that's the whole job, really. I know the sentence. I keep the sentence. The city sleeps.

The muffins are blueberry. Nobody's ever asked what the sentence about the muffins would be.
