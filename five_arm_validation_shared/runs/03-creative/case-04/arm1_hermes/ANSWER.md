# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-04

## Thirty-Five

The first letter came the year I turned one, which means I've never known a birthday without one. My mother kept them all in a shoebox with my hospital bracelet: *See you in 35 years*, in handwriting like piano wire. No signature. Postmarked nowhere — the postmark was smudged, as if the letter had been handled by someone wearing gloves.

My parents treated it as a joke from some far-flung aunt. Aunts move. Aunts count down. By the time I was ten and the letters read *See you in 25 years*, I had a theory: it was my grandfather, who died before I was born, writing from somewhere, promising something. By fifteen, when the number hit 20, my therapist suggested the letters were "a way of externalizing hope." I got an A on that essay. Hope externalized.

Here is what the letters never had: instructions. No *do this*, no *be there*, no address. Just the countdown, in that piano-wire hand, getting smaller by a year, like a bomb with a heart.

I built my whole life around the doorway they implied. College — because you can't meet your destiny unread. I studied astronomy, because whoever wrote to me clearly came from far away, or knew someone who did. I turned down a job in Lisbon because the letters came *here*. I married Sam, quietly, in a courthouse, and told him about the letters on the third date — as a test. He didn't laugh. He asked, "In 35 years from when?" and I loved him for doing the math.

The year the letters said *See you in 5 years*, we moved to a house with a porch, because I decided if someone was going to arrive, I wanted to be sitting down. Sam thought I meant a midlife crisis arriving. I let him.

*Four years. Three. Two.* My father died in there somewhere, and I remember thinking, at the funeral, that the letters had never once mentioned him — which meant whoever wrote them knew me, but only me. That narrowed the universe considerably.

*One year*, and I spent it getting my affairs gently in order, the way you do when you suspect a truck. *Tomorrow*, said the letter that came last month. Just that. *See you tomorrow.* Same wire-handwriting. Same date — my birthday, the date it had always been.

Sam parked the car on the porch side. We made coffee, because whatever walks through the door at the end of a 35-year countdown, you meet it caffeinated.

At 3:12 in the afternoon, a girl came up the walk.

She was maybe eight. She had my chin and my mother's walk, the one where you land each step like an apology. She was carrying a letter.

"Are you Grace?" she said. "I'm Grace too."

I looked at Sam. Sam, very carefully, put down his cup.

"I'm supposed to give you this," she said, "but I'm not allowed to say who sent it. Only — it's a rule in our family. We write to you every year on your birthday. It's my job when I'm older, but I get to do the last one now, because —" she frowned, checking her memory against some adult's instruction — "because you'll want to meet the sender yourself."

The letter said, in piano-wire handwriting:

*See you in 35 years.*

New numbers. My daughter handed it over with the gravity of a court clerk, said her mother was waiting in the car, and left.

We sat on the porch a long time.

"Grace," Sam said finally. "It's you. It's always been you. You've been writing to yourself since before you could write — time-loop, whatever you want to call it. You're the correspondent."

I looked at the letter. That was the obvious story, and I'd thought of it myself around year thirty, and I'd hated it, because it made the letters a closed circuit, a trick of paper. But sitting there, holding the girl's letter, I understood the actual shape of it — not a loop. A *relay*. Somewhere ahead of me, an older Grace had spent decades writing backward down her own life, one birthday at a time, to a child she remembered being, keeping a promise she couldn't explain yet. Not to change anything. Just so I would never have a birthday alone.

And in 35 years — tomorrow, next year, whatever — when the newest Grace is eight and holding her first letter, I will be the one writing *See you in 35 years* in handwriting like piano wire. I'll know what it costs. I'll know exactly which birthdays need an extra word.

"Okay," I said, and it came out steadier than I felt. "Then we'd better start practicing the handwriting."

Sam laughed the way you laugh when the strange thing turns out to be *your* strange thing. Inside, the coffee had gone cold, and outside, the first letter of the rest of the countdown sat on my knees, addressed in my own future hand.

I read it again anyway. *See you in 35 years.*

"Yeah," I told it. "See you."
