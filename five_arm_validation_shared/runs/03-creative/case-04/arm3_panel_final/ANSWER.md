# See You Tomorrow

The envelope was waiting when I came down for breakfast, propped against the salt cellar like it had always lived there. Cream paper, no stamp, no postmark. My name in blue ink, in handwriting I had never been able to trace and had stopped trying to trace around my thirtieth birthday.

*See you tomorrow.*

I sat down and laid the thirty-five letters beside it, in order, the way I had laid them out every year since I was old enough to understand what I was keeping. The first one said *see you in 35 years.* The next said 34. Then 33. One number less, every year, on the same date — the date I was born, though the letters never said happy birthday, never said anything but the count. My mother swore she never saw who delivered them. My father, when he was alive, used to say the postman came before the postman came, and then he would laugh at his own joke until he coughed.

When I was six, I asked my mother who would write to a baby. She said, "Someone who knew you before you knew you," and I thought that meant God, and for a while the letters felt like prayers being returned to sender.

At twenty I tried to catch the courier. I sat up all night in the hall with the lights off. At some point I must have slept, because morning came all at once, and the envelope was on the mat, and the house smelled faintly of rain that hadn't fallen. At thirty I hired a man who knew about locks and cameras. He watched the door, the windows, the chimney. The letter arrived inside a room he had sealed. He asked for double pay and never spoke of it again.

So I stopped asking *how* and started asking *what*. What does a person want, sending a count down through a life like a fuse burning toward something?

The letters never changed tone. No warmth, no threat. Just the arithmetic of patience. But I gave them tones anyway. In my teens they were a dare. In my twenties, a debt — I assumed I owed someone something, and the letters were invoices for a service rendered before my memory began. In my thirties they became a promise, which is the most dangerous reading, because a promise can be kept by anyone, and I began to imagine faces.

There was a woman once. Years in, when the count stood at eleven. She said she liked that I was waiting for something without knowing what, and I nearly told her about the letters, and then understood that telling her would make the waiting hers too, and I had no right. She married someone else. I have wondered since whether that was the thing the letters were for — whether the count was measuring how long I could hold a door open before I let it close.

At the count of five, I wrote back. I left a letter of my own on the mat: *Who are you?* The next year, my letter was gone and the new one said, as always, *see you in 4 years.* But the handwriting on the envelope trembled, just slightly, and I have thought about that tremble more than I have thought about most people.

Now the last letter says tomorrow, and I am fifty-three years old, and I don't know what to wear.

That's the part that makes me laugh, alone at this table with thirty-six envelopes: not the mystery, not the decades, but that my hands are shaking like a boy's before a first dance. I have imagined this day ten thousand ways. A lawyer with a will. A sibling I never knew. The woman, older now, holding the tremble accountable. A stranger with my eyes. My own handwriting — the letters were always from me, sent back along some seam in time, a lifetime of encouragement posted in advance.

I have prepared for all of them and none of them. I cleaned the house. I bought a cake, which felt absurd, and then I iced it, which felt like an answer to a question no one asked.

Tonight I will do what I have done every year on this date: I will read the whole sequence aloud, first letter to last, the numbers falling like stairs, and I will sleep in the hall with the lights off, the way I did at twenty, because whatever is coming has always known where to find me, and I would rather be found awake.

*See you tomorrow.*

Tomorrow, then. I'll put the kettle on at eight.

Whoever you are — and you must be someone, because no one counts that high for nothing — I have kept every word you sent, and I have one of my own to give you, and I have practiced it in the mirror so it won't come out as tears:

*You're late. Come in anyway.*
