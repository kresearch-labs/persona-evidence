# The Counting

The letters came every year on my birthday, and they said only one thing.

The first arrived the week I was born: a pale blue envelope, no return address, no signature. *See you in 35 years.* Blue ink, a hand that slanted left. My father put it in a shoebox on the top shelf of his closet, and every year the shoebox came down and got one letter heavier, and every year the number got one year smaller, and neither of us said anything about either.

I was six when I understood the numbers were counting down. *See you in 29 years.* I decided it was a bomb. I had seen a movie about a bomb. When I asked who was writing to me, my father said, "Your mother liked to plan ahead," which was the only sentence he ever gave me about her, and I built a religion on it. He knew more. I understood that much even then. The not-saying was the inheritance he kept intact and passed on.

The theories changed as I did. At nine: a godmother, hiding. At twelve: God, keeping receipts. At fifteen: a kidnapper giving lawful notice. At nineteen, away at college, I showed the whole shoebox to a roommate who read them in order and said, "Whoever this is, they've never missed once," and I said, "I know," and that was the entire content of what I felt, which was not fear. It was attendance. Whatever else was true of my life — the moves, the bad years, the year my father died and left me a house and no further sentences — one thing arrived on time, every year, on the same pale blue paper.

*See you in 11 years. See you in 10. See you in 9.*

At thirty I noticed the hand had not aged. Thirty years of letters and the same blue ink, the same leftward slant, no tremor, no fatigue, none of the slow drift by which a person's writing betrays their changing. I laid them out on my kitchen table in order, thirty-one envelopes on a table for two, and understood: they had been written at once. Before I could walk, someone had sat down in some room and written me a lifetime of mail, dated it, and handed it to the years like a stack of appointments.

The postmark, once I thought to look, had never changed. A town three hours north. I was twenty-six. I could have driven up in an afternoon. I didn't. The letters never invited me; they only promised. It felt as though going uninvited would be opening the door before the knock — and besides, I had one thing in my life that had never once been late, and I did not want to know how it was made.

The last letter came this year. The envelope was cream, heavier, and inside, on a card: *See you tomorrow.* Beneath it, for the first time, an address. The hand was the same hand, but the slant trembled now, and the ink had dried slowly enough to blot. Written recently. Written by someone keeping an appointment.

I drove north through rain that came in sideways off the fields. The house was small and white, with a garden kept by someone with more will than time. She was in a chair by the window, thin as a comma, and on her lap lay a notebook full of that same leftward hand.

"You got all of them," she said. Not a question.

"Thirty-five," I said.

"I wrote them in a week," she said. "The week you were born. The doctors gave me a number and I decided to take it literally."

She had been twenty-four. There was a thing in her blood, they told her, slow but punctual: thirty-five years, give or take. My father had wanted to spend those years in hospitals. She wanted me to have a mother who was always about to arrive, not one who was always leaving. So she went north, to a town where nobody owed her anything, and in seven days she wrote thirty-five letters, and carried them to the postmistress with a standing order of stamps and one instruction: the same date, every year, and never a word more.

"Why never a word more?"

"Because the number was the promise," she said. "A promise that shrinks is still a promise. I thought if you had one thing that always came, you could forgive everything else that didn't."

We had the day. She made tea with the concentration of someone spending a limited supply of strength, and walked me through the garden at the speed of the garden, and told me small things in no order — that my father had written her twice a year for ten years and she had answered once; that she had seen me at fourteen, from across a street, and walked the other way on purpose, and regretted it the specific way you regret a letter you didn't send. The notebook was full of drafts. Letters with words in them. Thirty-five years of things to say, written and never mailed. She gave me the notebook instead of choosing.

She lived eleven more months, which by her arithmetic made her early. I'd say she ran over, generously, the way a writer does, unable to quite let go of the last sentence.

I have the shoebox. I have the notebook. And some nights I take out a pen — blue ink; it seemed important — and a card, and sit with them a while without writing. I know how it's done now. You sit down once, in the best week you have, and count backward from the whole of what you're left with, and then you spend the rest of your life making it true.
