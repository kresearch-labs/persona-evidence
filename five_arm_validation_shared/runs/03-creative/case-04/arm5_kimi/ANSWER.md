# Thirty-Five

The first letter arrived the day I was born. My mother kept it in the cedar box with my hospital bracelet and a lock of hair so fair it could have belonged to anyone. Blue ink, a slanting hand, no signature, no return address. *See you in 35 years.*

A year later, to the day, the second one came. *See you in 34 years.* Then thirty-three. Then thirty-two. The number fell the way water falls — patiently, without any doubt about where it was going.

When I was small I believed everyone got them. I was six before I understood that the letter was mine alone, and nine before I thought to ask who sent it. My mother was shelling peas at the kitchen table, and she did not look up, and the pause went on so long that I understood the pause was the answer. "Someone who knew your father," she said at last, which was a way of saying nothing, because my father was a word we did not use in that house. He had died before I was born. That was the whole of him: a death and a date.

After that I stopped asking, and the letters kept coming, and my mother would hand them to me without a word, and I would take them up to my room and read a sentence I already knew by heart, because it was always the same sentence, only smaller.

People imagine a countdown as a kind of threat, but mine was the opposite. Mine was a spine. Whatever else was uncertain — and plenty was; we moved twice, my mother married badly once, I was not a child who made friends easily — there was always the number, falling in the dark like a bead on a string, one a year, reliable as weather. I measured my life against it. *See you in 20 years* found me learning to drive in a supermarket parking lot. *See you in 15* found me in a dormitory three states away, and how it found me I never knew, because I had told no one where I was going, and yet there it was, forwarded in my mother's hand, and beneath her address sticker the blue ink, unchanged, patient, counting.

*See you in 12 years* came the October my mother was dying, though we did not yet call it that. By the next October she was gone, and the letter came anyway, to the house I could not bring myself to sell, and I sat on the kitchen floor and cried — not only for her, I understood later, but because the letter was now the only thing left in the world that remembered my birthday without being asked.

I told someone once. A man I loved, or was trying to. We were in bed and it felt like the kind of hour for secrets, so I gave him mine. He laughed — not cruelly, but the way you laugh at a ghost story — and said it sounded like the beginning of a novel, and I watched myself from the ceiling become a story instead of a person, and I never told anyone again. He left anyway. They all left, or I made them leave; it is hard to build a life with a woman who is keeping an appointment she cannot explain.

The last decade went faster than it should have. *Ten. Nine. Eight.* I noticed I had begun making decisions with the number in the room. I turned down a job in Seattle. I kept my name. I stayed out of the ocean on my birthday, out of airplanes, off ladders — as if the appointment were a thing I owed someone and my body was the payment. At *three* I started running in the mornings, because it occurred to me that whoever was waiting might be old, and I wanted to be the kind of person who had taken care of herself for him. *Him.* By then I was sure it was a man, though I could not have said why. The ink, maybe. The patience.

*See you in 1 year.* I did not sleep that night, or most nights after. I became superstitious. I became twelve again, watching the mailbox.

And then, this morning — the third of October, my thirty-fifth birthday — the last one. Same envelope. Same blue ink. *See you tomorrow.* And beneath it, for the first time in my life, a second line: an address, and a time. Nine o'clock. A diner on the state road, two hours north, a place I had driven past a hundred times without seeing.

I knew before I got there. I knew when I saw the diner, and across the highway from it, behind two fences and a coil of wire, the long gray buildings I had also driven past a hundred times without seeing.

He was in the last booth, an old man with my cheekbones, a cup of coffee going cold in front of him and a fountain pen laid beside it like a piece of evidence. He did not stand. He looked at me the way you look at a door you have been walking toward for thirty-five years — afraid to open it, more afraid not to.

"I was sentenced the week you were born," he said. "Your mother came once. She told me she'd told you I was dead, and I understood that she had to, and I asked her for one thing. I asked to be allowed to count."

His sister had posted the letters, he said. She had kept track of me all those years, through every move, every name I almost took. He never asked her how. Some things you leave alone, because they are working.

He did not tell me what he had done, and I did not ask, though I will, someday; there is time now, which is a sentence I am still learning to say. He did not ask my forgiveness. He only asked if I wanted coffee, and when the waitress came I heard myself say yes.

Thirty-five years I had been counting down to zero, and zero turned out to be this: a man across a table, both of us out of time to serve, waiting to see if I would stay.

I stayed until the coffee went cold. Then I stayed a little longer.
