# Next Door to Helven House

People cross the street to avoid walking past Helven House. They do it casually, like they remembered something on the other side, like it isn't a thing everyone in town has done their whole lives. The postman leaves the house's mail in my box with a rubber band around it and a note that says *yours to sort*. It is not mine to sort. But I have lived next door for fifty-one years, and I have learned that the house appreciates small courtesies.

Everyone has a story about Helven House. Most of them are wrong. The Vickers boy did not go in on a dare and come out white-haired — he came out fine, and his hair went white two years later, the way hair does. The Millers did not flee in the night. They left on a Tuesday, in daylight, and Mrs. Miller waved at me from the car. It was the wave of a person who has decided to be normal so hard that it shows.

My story is smaller. It is only that my sister went in, and the house gave her back.

We were nine. It was August, and Ruthie had lost her red ball over the low stone wall at the back of the Helven garden, where the blackberries grow. She went over the wall to get it. I stood on our side of the grass — there is no fence, there has never been a fence, the grass just changes its mind about being ours — and watched her cross the lawn, and the lawn was very long. That is the only way I can say it. She was walking the whole time and the house was not getting closer, and then it was, all at once, the way a dream decides.

She went in through the back door, which was open. The back door of Helven House has been open my entire life. I have never seen it closed, and I have never seen anyone open it.

I did not scream. I stood in the yard for four hours, until the streetlights came on, and then Ruthie came out the front door with her red ball under her arm and walked home across the long lawn, and it took her eleven steps. I counted.

She was fine. That is what I tell people. She ate dinner. She did her times tables. She grew up, moved to Spokane, has two daughters and a dog named Biscuit. She calls me every Sunday.

But here is the thing I have never told anyone, not even Ruthie, especially not Ruthie. When she came out that evening, she walked past me and said, "Don't be mad, I only lost it for a minute." I was so relieved I did not think, until years later, the night before her wedding: *I never told her the ball was lost for a minute. She was in the house for four hours. Who told her it was a minute?*

The house is not cruel. Fairness is the whole arrangement. It returns what it borrows. The Millers' cat came back after a week, fat and purring. The Vickers boy's bike, which he swore he left in the Helven driveway, turned up in mine, tires pumped, chain oiled. Once, in the hard winter of '98, I found a cord of firewood stacked against my porch that I did not buy, on the exact day my oil ran out. I left a pie on the Helven porch rail to say thank you. The plate came back washed.

That is the arrangement. I mow the lawn on my side of where the grass changes its mind. I sort the mail and leave it on the porch rail, and it disappears, and I do not ask where. I tell the town's children the true stories, the small boring ones, because the house does not like a fuss. A fuss is when people come with cameras, or priests, or — once, in 1987 — a developer with a plan for condominiums. His car would not start, and then it would, and he drove away very fast, and nobody has come with a clipboard since.

The house is quiet when it is regarded politely. That is the whole haunting. Everyone in town performs their little street-crossing ritual and thinks they are afraid of the house, when really, without knowing it, they are being *polite* to it. I taught them that. Fifty-one years of small courtesies, and the town learned by osmosis.

I am seventy-four now. My doctor uses the word "manageable," which means its opposite. And here is the story I actually sat down to tell:

Last month a young couple bought the Henderson place on the other side of Helven House. They have a little girl, maybe eight, maybe nine, with a red jacket and a way of standing very still and looking at things. Last week I saw her at the edge of her yard, looking at the Helven lawn, the way you look at water before you decide about it.

So yesterday I did what I have been putting off. I baked a pie. I walked it across the grass — it took me eleven steps, I counted, the house has always been honest with me — and I set it on the porch rail, and I said, out loud, to the open back door that no one has ever opened:

"Not her. Take me instead, when it's time. I've been a good neighbor. She's just a child."

The house did not answer. It has never answered; that is not the arrangement. But this morning my plate was on my own porch, washed, and on it was a red ball. Not Ruthie's — Ruthie's is in a box in my attic, I checked, I check every year. A new one. The kind they sell now at the shop on Main Street.

The little girl next door has been out in her yard all morning. She is looking for something.

I am going to introduce myself to her parents. Someone has to teach them the courtesies — the mail, the lawn, the pie, the long way across the street that isn't really about fear. Someone has to live next door to Helven House and be its good neighbor, so that it stays a good neighbor back.

I had hoped it would be me forever. The house knows that now. I think — I hope — that is why it sent the ball back with the plate. A promise is a promise, even from a house. Especially from a house.

But I am keeping my eye on that little girl. And every Sunday, when Ruthie calls, I ask after her daughters, and I do not say why, and she does not ask. She never asks anything about the house. She moved to Spokane, which is very far away, and flat, and dry, and she has never once, in fifty-one years, come home to visit.

She was fine. She is fine.

I count her Sundays like steps. Eleven, eleven, eleven. So far, every one of them has come back to me.
