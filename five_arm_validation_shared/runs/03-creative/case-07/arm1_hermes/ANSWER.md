# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-07

## Next Door to Helven House

The town avoids Helven House the way you'd avoid a throat you'd once seen swallow something. They cross the street. They tell new families the house is empty, which is a lie the way a dam is a wall.

I live fourteen feet from its porch. I have lived here my whole life, all twenty-six years of it, because my grandmother bought this house in a probate sale nobody else bid on, and because — she said, lighting her pipe on the porch — "Somebody should keep the neighbors."

Not the neighbor. The *neighbors*. Plural. That's the first story.

Here's what the town knows: Helven House is haunted, and in 1971 a family left in the night, leaving supper on the table. True, but shallow. Here's what I know, because my grandmother fed the house for fifty years and I've been feeding it since she died:

The house doesn't haunt people. It *collects* them. There's a difference, and the difference is why the town is wrong to be afraid and right to be careful.

The people in Helven House are not ghosts of the dead. They are the unfinished. The house takes in whoever dies nearby with something burning in them — a question, a promise, a chore nobody else will do. The fiddler from 1890 who owes a tune to a bride who never heard it. The surveyor who mapped this county wrong in 1902 and wants, desperately, to say so. A schoolteacher, a horse, a mailman who died mid-route and still, some mornings, you can hear his bicycle bell coming up the lane at dawn, arriving nowhere.

My grandmother's arrangement was simple: you don't go in, they don't come out, and once a year — the night the frost comes — you put a plate on the porch. Bread, a boiled egg, something sweet. "Not for eating," she said. "For *knowing*. A house that's fed tells you its weather."

The stories. You asked for the stories.

The night my grandfather died, the whole family sat in the kitchen, and every clock in our house stopped at the minute he passed — except the one facing Helven House, which ran five minutes fast, and has ever since. We believe it's running his last five minutes for him, gently, so he doesn't have to.

The winter the Kowalski boy ran away, half the town searched the woods. My grandmother stood on the porch and called toward the dark house: "He's not one of yours, Helven. He's got a mother living." And the porch light of Helven House — which nobody ever turns on, there's no power in it — blinked twice, and the boy was found at the bus station the next morning, and never knew why he'd suddenly remembered his grandmother's phone number.

The house has moods. In good years the windows glow warm at dusk, the way lit rooms do when someone is home reading. In bad years — when something dies angry within a mile, or when developers come with their surveys — the house goes gray, and the shutters knock, and my grandmother would say, "Someone's new. Feed early."

I've only been inside once. When my grandmother died, of course, because she was half theirs by the end. The door was open. It smelled like bread. Every unfinished person the house held was standing in the hall — the fiddler, the surveyor, the schoolteacher, the horse's gentle steam — and they were *waiting*, not to take me, but to show me the plate. Her plate, fifty-one years of frost nights, washed and set in the hall cabinet, because the house keeps its debts too. The fiddler played one tune, the one he'd owed, and it was for her, and I understood that I was inheriting a job, not a house.

So no, I don't avoid Helven House. I court it. I feed it. I tell it the county news, because the surveyor needs corrections and the schoolteacher loves gossip.

And here's the last story, the one the town doesn't have: last winter, the frost came early, and I put the plate out, and when I went to bring it in there was a note in handwriting I knew from my grandmother's recipe cards:

*"You're doing it right. — all of us."*

The house isn't haunted. The house is *furnished* with people, and somebody has to mind the door. The town can keep crossing the street. Their side of the road is fine.

Mine has neighbors.
