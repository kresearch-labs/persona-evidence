# Twelve Windows

Everyone in town goes out of their way to avoid Helven House. They cross to the far side of Corrick Road when they walk past. They let their gardens go wild along that stretch of fence. When the wind comes from the north, they shut their windows and pretend the sound coming over the wall is only the wind.

Not us. Our eaves nearly touch its fence line. My grandmother was born in that house, and she taught me that the fence isn't a boundary. It's a courtesy. The house has never needed to reach further.

People say, *you have stories* — because everyone knows our family does — and I say yes, and I don't tell them any. Here, on our side of the fence, is what I can tell you instead.

The house has eleven windows on the street side. Count them from the road: eleven. Count them from our kitchen and you get twelve, and the twelfth is the one to watch. It's the one that's lit at three in the morning, though the house has had no electricity since 1974.

The house doesn't rattle chains or bleed down its own staircase. It keeps. It takes what it's given and gives back quiet: no accidents on the Corrick bend, no fires, no bad wells, babies that sleep. Sixty years of a town with nothing worse to remember than weather. You can buy a lot of quiet if you're rich in the right currency, and the house's currency is stories. Told inside the fence, a story is a gift. Told outside it, the house takes the story — and everything the story was holding.

That's the part the town never learns, because the ones who learned it aren't around to explain. My mother told the one about her brother at a wedding in Vell, twenty miles off, and by the time she got home she had a photograph of a boy she didn't recognize and a gap in the family Bible where a name had been, in her own hand. She thought she was going mad. Grandmother sat her down and made her count the windows from the kitchen, and when she reached twelve she stopped crying — not because twelve is a comfort. Because twelve means the house is listening, and if it's listening, it has already eaten.

I did it too, at fourteen. Danny Marsh, who went through the ice on the Helven pond in 1961 and knocked on the house's door afterward, dripping, and was let in — I told Danny Marsh at a sleepover four houses down, in June, with the window open. I remember the story ending. I remember the Kesslers laughing. I don't remember the walk home. I remember Grandmother waiting up, and the twelfth window dark for the first time in my life, and her face: not angry. Relieved. Dark means it's finished eating.

Danny Marsh has no grave now. The pond has no story. The town remembers that a boy drowned once, somewhere, the way you remember weather. Our family keeps the ledger — a school notebook, Grandmother's hand, then my mother's, then mine. Names, dates, what was given. The house doesn't want the ledger. The ledger isn't a story. It's the receipt.

Grandmother died in the spring, in her bed, one wall from where she was born. That night every window was lit, all twelve, and my mother stood at the sink with her back to the house and would not look. You don't look when they're all lit. She never explained the rule and I've stopped needing explanations. A lifetime next to something teaches you its manners, if not its grammar.

She said one thing I've never put down. I'd asked her why us — why our family doesn't cross the road like everyone else. She was shelling peas and didn't look up. *The town pays in avoidance,* she said. *We're the collateral.* And when I asked what that meant: *It isn't a house with people in it. It's the people, with a house around them. The way a wall has mortar.*

The south door was bricked up in 1911, the year the last Helven died. Grandmother called it the house being polite. A house that keeps things doesn't leave a door where just anyone can wander in and hand it something it hasn't agreed to hold.

Last Tuesday the twelfth window was lit at three in the morning, and I stood at our sink and counted, the way I've counted since I was small. Eleven. Twelve. And in the twelfth, a woman with her sleeves pushed up, doing at a table the motion I know best in the world: smoothing a page in a notebook.

I got the ledger out. Every keeper signs the last line of their hand — Grandmother taught me that; it's how the next keeper knows who kept it. My mother's signature is there. Mine is there, dated the day I took the notebook. Grandmother's is gone. Not crossed out. The paper is blank where the ink was, the way the family Bible went blank, the way a name goes when the house has finished keeping what it was holding.

She isn't owed anymore. The receipt is spent.

I've written this account straight through, and it's three in the morning now, and all twelve windows are lit. I checked the south door the way you check a thing you don't want to be true.

The bricks are stacked beside it, neat as a mason's afternoon. The door stands open. The path from it to our fence has been swept.

People will say the house finally took one of us, and they'll cross the road a little faster for a while. They'll be wrong. Nothing was taken. In our family we settle our debts in person, and the path is swept, and I have the receipt in my hand.
