# The Stories You Have

Everyone in Marrow Creek goes out of their way to avoid Helven House. The mail carrier leaves parcels at the end of the drive and walks backward to the road, the way you would from a dog you didn't trust. The kids who dare each other to touch the fence never get past the gate, and the gate is as far as anyone has wanted to go for as long as anyone can remember. On the town map there is a blank where the house should be — not officially, but in the way of maps, which record what people do rather than what is there.

You don't avoid it. You can't. You have lived next door your whole life, in the house your great-grandfather built with his hands and his inheritance, and your bedroom window looks straight down the slope of your yard into the Helvens' — nobody named Helven has lived there in sixty years, but the name stuck, the way names do when there is no one left to argue with them.

You have stories.

Not the town's stories. The town's stories are secondhand and all the same: cold spots, weeping in the walls, a light that moves from window to window in the upstairs hall like someone carrying a candle through empty rooms. Those are fine stories, and mostly true, as it happens. But they are tourist stories, and you have better ones, because yours are yours.

There was the winter you were nine, when you went out on the creek ice and it let you down without a sound, and the cold got into your coat and pulled — not water pulling, hands pulling, patient hands that knew exactly how long you could go without breathing. Something else pulled back. You never saw what. You woke on the bank with your clothes steaming in the sun and a smell in your hair like old paper and lamp oil, and when you got home your mother did not ask how you got out of the creek. She looked past you at Helven House for a long moment and said, "You stay on your side of the fence." But her voice was wrong — grateful and frightened in the same breath, like two things left in a jar too long.

There was the dog. Biscuit got out on a Tuesday and was gone three days, and you were sure — everyone was sure — because he had gone over the fence into the Helvens' yard. He came back on the Friday, thin and burr-tangled and fine, and after that he never set paw past the fence line again. He would stand at it, though, and stare at the house, and wag his tail. Slowly. Like greeting someone he owed money to.

There was the storm two Octobers ago, when the big limb on the oak cracked and came down toward your mother's room, and in the morning it lay in the Helvens' yard, snapped off clean six feet above the ground, as if something had caught it and set it down. Your father never said a word about it. He just started leaving the porch light on at night — the one that faces the house. Not for you. You understood that even then. For it.

So no, you don't avoid Helven House. You wave at it, sometimes, the way you would wave at an old neighbor. And it has never once done anything to you but be there, the way a lighthouse is there, the way a large dog is there.

The town would say that is the haunting talking. The town says a lot of things.

Here is what you know that the town doesn't. Last spring, when your mother died — quickly, the doctors said, as if something had simply reached in and turned a valve — you found her journal in the drawer of the hall table, under the batteries and the takeout menus. It had been your grandmother's before it was hers, and her mother's before that, and it is not a diary. It is a ledger. Dates going back a hundred and forty years, one line at a time, in four different hands.

*It is kind,* the first entry says. *That is the trouble with it. Kindness is only hunger with good manners.*

And then, further in, your grandmother's hand, smaller and faster:

*We do not live next to Helven House. It lives next to us. It cannot cross the fence. It has never needed to. It gives us our stories instead — the ice, the dog, the falling limb — and a family full of debts always stays to pay them. We are not its neighbors. We are its keepers, and it is ours, and every generation one of us learns which of us it is.*

The entry after that is dated three days before your grandmother died. It says only: *It has chosen.*

You have read the ledger a dozen times since, looking for your mother's hand, for any line that would tell you what she understood at the end, and there is nothing. Her last entry is a grocery list. That is somehow worse.

Tonight the wind is up, and you are at your window because you always are, watching the candle-light move from window to window in the upstairs hall of Helven House, the way it has every night of your life. Except tonight it stops. At the window that faces yours. And stays.

And you realize you are standing at the glass with your hand already raised in a wave, and you don't remember getting up, and you are not afraid. That is the worst part. You feel the way you feel coming home.

You have stories. It has you. And the fence, you understand now, was never there to keep anyone out.
