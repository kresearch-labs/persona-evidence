# Root and Iron

The morning Maren came home from the war, she dug a hole at the edge of the field where the farm gave way to woods, laid her sword in it, and shoveled the earth back over. Then she planted an apple sapling on the spot, so she could mark the place with something living instead of something dead. Her hands shook the whole time. They had been shaking for a year.

*Never again,* she told the tree. The tree, being a tree, did not argue.

That was eleven years ago.

The tree grew. It gave its first real harvest the autumn Tomas turned five, and Maren, who had once marched forty miles on moldy bread, burned the tart. The years after were fence rails and lambs and roof shingles, a husband buried in the third year of them, fever, a broom that lived by the door. When Tomas asked what she had done in the war, she said, "I marched," in a voice that ended conversations. He learned not to ask. The whole valley was like that. Every family had a hole somewhere — a grave, an empty sleeve, a door nobody opened.

The riders came in the eleventh spring: six of them on thin horses, deserters off some new fighting in the south, though to Maren they looked like every army's leavings, men who had been fed to a war and spat out wrong. They took grain from the miller and a mare from the Alders, and their leader, a man with a burn up one side of his neck, sat his horse in the yard awhile, studying Tomas, who was twelve and tall for it.

"He'll do," the man said. "We'll be back in a week for the boys. The baron pays a bounty on recruits, and we mean to collect it."

At the meeting in the mill that night, the village talked itself in circles. Pay them in grain. Hide the boys in the high woods. Send to the baron for protection, as if the baron were not the one paying. Maren stood at the back and said nothing. She knew men like these. Once, men like these had known her.

She went home and sat in the dark until Tomas slept. Then she took the spade and a lantern and walked out to the apple tree.

The digging was the worst of it, worse than she had let herself imagine. Eleven years of roots had found the sword. They had wrapped it the way fingers wrap a haft, pale and thick, and every one she cut bled sap onto her hands, and above her the tree gave small shudders, its leaves hissing though there was no wind. She told herself a tree could grow new roots. She did not let herself finish the thought about what else might grow back.

The blade came up rusted at the edges and sound at the core. The way she had left it. The way it had left her. She worked it with oil and stone by the fire, and somewhere in the second hour she noticed her hands had stopped shaking. Eleven years of peace had not done that. She noticed it the way you notice a draft in a room you believed was sealed.

Tomas came out of his room near dawn and saw the sword across her knees and her face above it, and she watched him understand that his mother was someone he did not know.

"You said you marched," he said.

"I did," she said. "Go back to bed."

He did not go back to bed. He stood in the doorway while the sky went gray, and neither of them said anything more.

She was on the road at first light, standing in the middle of it where the riders would have to come, the sword belted on over her old coat. She had no plan beyond the standing. When the six of them came up the road with the morning behind them, the leader raised a hand and halted them a good way off, and for a long moment nobody moved.

Then he leaned in his saddle and looked at her — at the sword, at how she wore it, at whatever was in her face that had not been there the week before.

"I know you," he said slowly. "Kestrel Ridge. You were at Kestrel Ridge." He paused. "They said the captain at Kestrel Ridge was dead."

"I was," she said.

He looked at her a while longer, and at the sword, and did the arithmetic that men like him survived by. Then he turned his horse, and the others turned with theirs, and they went back down the road the way they had come. They did not come through the valley again.

Whether it was mercy or fear, Maren never asked herself. She was afraid she knew.

She walked home. The apple tree stood at the edge of the field with half its roots cut, and she packed the earth back into the hole and stamped it down and carried water to it until the ground would hold no more. Spring would tell her whether it lived.

She did not bury the sword again. She leaned it inside the door, where the broom used to stand, and went to make breakfast.
