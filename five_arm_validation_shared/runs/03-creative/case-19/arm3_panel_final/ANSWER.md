# The Whetstone

Mara buried the sword at dusk, three feet down beneath the pear tree, wrapped in the cloak she had worn out of the war. She dug with the fence-post spade and laid it in with both hands, the way you set down anything you mean to leave. Then she said the words out loud, because the field was empty and no one would hear her fail.

"Never again."

The ground took cloak and blade and closed over them without comment. She tamped the earth flat with the back of the spade, and that was the whole ceremony. There had been enough of the other kind.

She had used the sword for six years, and it had done what swords do. The last of it had been a gate in the rain, and a boy on the wrong side of it, and after that she had carried it home across two provinces because it seemed wrong to leave it lying in somebody else's mud. The vow was not that she would forget the war. The vow was that nothing of hers would ever be in it again.

The farm had other opinions about her hands. There were fences, and a roof that argued every winter, and in the third spring a son, Tam, who arrived early and loud and stayed that way. She taught him pruning and grafting and the names of the tools that make things. When he whittled sticks into the other kind she took them away, and then, feeling the meanness of it, gave them back. When the anvil rang in the village she discovered errands on the far side of the house.

The pear tree fruited. The years went. She got good at not flinching.

In the seventeenth summer the riders came up the valley with a list. Every household owed a man or his worth in silver, and the Voss farm owed a son. They stood Tam in the yard and stood a spear beside him, and he was taller than the spear, which the sergeant noted with approval, because a sergeant's whole trade is measuring boys against metal. The date was set. The sergeant was polite about it. They were always polite; that was how you knew the war had come to stay.

That night she lay in the dark listening to the barn, where Tam was working a wooden post the way she had once worked a whetstone — the same double-knock, the same patience. He had never been taught. That was what she heard under the rhythm: not that he was practicing, but that no one had ever shown him anything, and in three days someone would, quickly, and what they taught quickly was not how to come home.

She got up before the moon was down and took the lantern and the spade to the pear tree.

The roots had gone through the cloak in seventeen years, fine white threads stitching themselves into the wool, and she had to cut him — it — free with the pruning knife. The blade came up freckled with rust but true, and she scoured it with sand and goose fat until the steel showed dull silver, and then she took the whetstone from the kitchen drawer and worked it along the edge until the edge answered. The grip still knew her. That was the shame of it, and the comfort; she stood in the wet grass with her hand around it and let it be both.

She did not test the edge on anything. There was nothing in the barn she needed.

Tam was awake — of course he was awake — and he watched her come in with it held across her two hands, like water carried to a neighbor.

"You said you'd never," he said.

"I said I'd never use it again." She held it out. "I'm not using it. I'm giving it away."

He took it and it changed in his hands, the way a thing does when it stops being memory and starts being weight. He held it wrong. She reached to fix his fingers and stopped, and left them, because the grip would teach him, or the sergeant would, and she was not going to be there either way.

"Bring it back dull," she said.

"Dull?"

"Dull," she said. "That's all."

He slept with it by the door like a dog. She sat by the window until the light came, the whetstone in her pocket, turning it over, its two faces worn to the shape of her thumb.

They marched at noon, a dozen boys from the valley with their spears and their bundles, a thin new fence walking up the road. She stood at the gate and did not wave, because waving was for departures that might not repeat themselves, and this one would — harvest, and then winter, and then whatever the list said next. Tam looked back once, at the bend. She raised her hand then. One wave, spent where it could be seen.

The hole under the pear tree stayed open. She meant to fill it in and kept on not meaning to; the rain could do the filling, rain was patient, and the ground had held the shape of the sword for seventeen years and could hold the shape of the hole a while longer, for company.

She had kept the whetstone. Whatever he brought back — dull, and she would make it sharp; sharp, and she would make it dull — there would be work for her hands while he talked.
