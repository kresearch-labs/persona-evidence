# What the Ground Keeps

Maren came home in the month the pears ripened, three years after she had left, walking the last ten miles on a leg the autumn rain had made stiff. The war was over. People in the villages she passed through said it the way you test ice with your weight — quickly, ready to be wrong.

She had the sword because a quartermaster had pressed it into her hands at the demobilization field and refused to take it back. *Oldest blade in the company,* he said. *It should retire with honors.* She carried it the last hundred miles without cleaning it, and it rode her hip like a debt.

Her father's farm stood where she had left it, at the edge of the village, the thatch patched badly by neighbors who had meant well. She slept one night under her own roof, and on the second night, with the lamp turned low, she took the spade and the sword out to the old pear tree in the corner of the garden. She dug between the roots where the ground was soft, wrapped the blade in oilcloth, and laid it down.

"Never again," she said. Not to God, and not to the dead. To the ground, which keeps what it is given.

Then she filled the hole and stood on it a moment, as if to seal a letter.

The years that followed were the years she had promised herself. She learned the farm again: bees, hens, the grafting knife, the market cart on Fridays. Her leg forgave her most of what the war had done to it. Calluses came back to her hands, honest ones, earned on wood and rope instead of on the grip of a weapon. In the third summer the pear tree, as if in payment, bore more than the household could eat.

The smith's boy came with the harvest. His name was Tam; his father had died at the river crossing in the last year of the war, and his mother the winter after, and the village had passed him from kitchen to kitchen like a dish nobody wanted to wash. He was eight years old and hungry, and Maren fed him for the sake of her own dead, and he never entirely went away again.

She taught him everything she knew except one thing. Grafting, bees, the weather, letters, the mending of walls. When he asked about the war she answered in the fewest words the question allowed. When he asked why the pears from the corner tree tasted sweeter than any others in the parish, she said, "Deep roots," and left it there.

He was sixteen the year the drums came back. It was the same road, the same dust, the same sergeant's smile; only the colors over the muster field had changed, and not even the colors, only the badge on them. The village owed five men. Tam put his name down himself, before she knew there was a list, because he was sixteen and believed the war was a story she was too tired to tell.

There was nothing to arm him with. The smithy had been cold for six years; the parish had sold its old blades to the army's iron wagons in the hard winter, every one, down to the rusted. He would march with a boar spear, the sergeant said, and be issued better at the coast. The sergeant said it kindly. He said it the way men say things they have said a hundred times.

Maren tried to let it be. She tried for three nights, lying awake under the roof beam, doing sums: a boar spear is a wall weapon, a boy can learn a wall, the line will hold, the line always holds until it doesn't. On the third night she got up, took the lamp and the spade, and went out to the pear tree.

The ground gave up the oilcloth easily. The leather of the grip had gone green in places, and the oil had dried to a skin, but when she wiped the blade with wool it came back to her the way a face comes back — the same steel, the same balance, the same small worn crest at the ricasso where her thumb had always sat. Steel keeps. That was the whole trouble with steel. It keeps.

The last time she had held it drawn was in a gateway during the fighting retreat, and what she remembered was not the fighting. She remembered the snow that came down afterward, patient, unhurried, falling on everything equally, and her own thought, standing there: *this is what I will carry home.* She had been wrong about many things. She had not been wrong about that.

At dawn she took Tam into the yard and put the sword in his hands.

He held it wrong, and she corrected him, and then she made him hold it out at arm's length until his arm shook, and then she made him sheath it fifty times, slow, counting. "The first thing and the last thing you learn is the sheath," she said. "Any fool can take it out. Now swear. Never draw it unless you must, and never for a story anyone tells you."

"I swear," he said, sixteen and meaning it with his whole chest.

"Good," she said. "Mean it. And when the day comes that you must — it will come — break it cleanly, and don't make a religion of the pieces."

They stood under the pear tree while the muster formed on the road, the pears heavy above them, ripe, going unpicked. He looked younger with the sword than without it. She did not tell him to come back, because he would either do it or he wouldn't, and words were the one thing she had never been able to bury.

She watched him out of sight. Then she went in and packed the pears for market, because Friday was Friday.

That evening she filled the hole again, tamping the earth down with the flat of the spade, and the tree took no notice and went on bearing.

She had sworn never to use it again. She never had.
