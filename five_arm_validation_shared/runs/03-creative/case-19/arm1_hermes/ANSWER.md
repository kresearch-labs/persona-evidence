## The Sword Under the Apple Tree

She buried it the day she came home, in the orchard behind her mother's house, under the crooked apple tree where as a child she'd once buried her father's compass and a jar of plum stones, believing buried things turned into other things.

The sword had been her whole war. Fifteen years, three campaigns, a command at the fords, a scar from collarbone to hip. She washed it in the river, oiled the blade, wrapped it in the campaign banner she'd slept under for a decade, and dug the hole at dusk with the same entrenching shovel that had dug graves for eleven of her soldiers. She did not say a speech. She said six words: "Stay down. I'm done with you." Then she put forty pounds of iron in the ground and became a farmer, and the town learned to stop asking, and the sword — that's the thing about oaths sworn over buried steel — the sword *listened*.

Years are an orchard's trick. The apple tree grew around the burial spot like a dog growing around a fence post, roots wrapping the wrapped blade, and her life grew the same way: an orchard, a small herd, a marriage to the miller's daughter who kept the ledgers and laughed at the right times, two children who knew their mother had "been a soldier" the way children know their parents were once tall.

Twenty-two years. The village had a border dispute in the way villages have weather — always coming, never arriving. Then the soldiers came for real: not the old levy with its familiar faces, but a mercenary company flying no lord's colors, burning the granary at Millbrook, taking what they called "toll."

The council came to her door because the old people still told stories and the young men had never held a wall. She listened from the kitchen with flour on her hands, and said, "You have archers. You have a bridge. You have stones and a mill race. Fight the fight you have."

"We have no one who has held a line," said the councilwoman, an old friend of her mother's. "Not one."

She sent them away at dusk with drill schedules and the benefit of her voice, and she went to the orchard, and she stood under the crooked apple tree for a long time with her hands very clean and very empty.

The sword had rusted where the oil had failed. She cleaned it in the kitchen by lamplight, her husband holding the lamp and asking nothing, which was the marriage working exactly as designed. The edge came back with three hours of whetstone. Edges remember. That's the horror and the mercy of edges.

She did not become a soldier again, she told herself. She was *advising*. She inspected the bridgehead at dawn, moved the archers forty yards to where she would have wanted them twenty years ago, and put the mill race to work as a moat that would fill in ninety seconds. The raiders came on the third night with fire and ladders, and found the wall manned by farmers who had been taught, in six evenings, the two things that matter: don't break the line, and wait for the word.

The word was hers, given from the wall, and the fight was short and ugly and successful, and one man from the next village died, which she carried home to his family personally because she knew that road.

Afterward the council asked — the whole village, assembled in the orchard, the way crowds happen — whether she would take command of the district's defense. Command. A commission. Her name on muster rolls again.

She looked at the sword, leaning against the crooked apple tree, clean and terrible and patient.

"No," she said. "I'll train whoever asks me. I'll inspect walls, and I'll tell you what I see, and when the bell rings I'll stand where I'm needed. But the sword goes back in the ground tomorrow." She looked at the assembled faces. "Because here is what I learned holding one: the day you *want* to use it is the day you shouldn't. A soldier's promise isn't never to fight. It's to fight only when the fight comes to the orchard — and then to put it away again, before the sword starts choosing the fights."

They asked her what the six words at the burial had meant, twenty years ago — the village had its spies. She considered lying, considered the sword, and gave them the true version:

"Stay down. I'm done with you."

She put it back under the roots the next morning. The roots had grown a cradle for it, as if the tree had known the whole time.

The raiders never returned; word travels on torch-smoke. The village drilled anyway, all winter, because she made them, and twenty years from now some farmer will dig up a sword under an apple tree — her granddaughter, if the tree has its way — and find the note wrapped with the hilt, the one she wrote this winter:

*Edge remembers. So do you. Dig only when the orchard needs it.*
