# The Sunrise Act

Every morning at six, Aurelia Cross stood in the east-wing hallway of the Megalocity Meadows Retirement Home, raised her palm to the dead sconce outside her door, and tried to light it.

The bulb was fine — maintenance had replaced it twice. It was the wiring, they said, a junction buried somewhere in the wall, not worth the drywall. Aurelia tried anyway. She had lit the whole of Megalocity Harbor during the Blackout Riots. For forty years the papers had called her Lumen. Now she stood in her robe with her hand open like a beggar's, and after a minute a glow came up beneath the skin — faint, the color of weak tea — trembled, and went out.

"You'll strain something," said Erasmus Vale from the doorway of 4B.

"It's a hallway, Erasmus. It should be lit."

"It's a hallway," he agreed, and shuffled past her toward breakfast, his cane ticking on the carpet. Where the dawn through the window caught him, his shadow stretched long and elegant down the corridor, the way it once had across half the city. The papers had called him Doctor Dusk. He had stolen a sunrise once, kept it in a jar for eleven days. She was the one who made him give it back.

They did not fight anymore. Nobody at the Meadows fought anymore. Captain Vantage used his flight ring to hover up and dust the ceiling fans. The Quilter, who had once stitched time itself, hemmed the day-room curtains and told anyone who'd listen that everything holds if you baste it. At rec hour, Erasmus did shadow theater — paper herons, a skyline, a dog that became a wolf — and the old capes and old masks applauded from their wheelchairs, and Aurelia sat in the back and told herself she was not jealous of the dark.

In October, the activities director put up the sign-up sheet for the Harvest Follies. Aurelia wrote her name in large letters: THE SUNRISE ACT. Her old finale. The horizon, the rim of the sun, the whole sky coming up gold over Megalocity. She had done it for mayors. She had done it from the deck of an aircraft carrier.

Erasmus read the sheet and found her in the garden.

"A duet," he said. "My silhouettes against your light. The herons need a dawn to fly across."

"I work alone."

"You worked opposite me for forty years," he said. "It's not the same as alone."

She pretended not to hear him. She rehearsed in the laundry room at night, building the horizon inch by inch, and every night the sun came up smaller, and every night Nurse Okafor found her there and took her pulse and said nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said.

The night of the Follies, the day room was full. Captain Vantage did a loop over the audience, six inches off the ground, to wild applause. The Quilter made a scarf change hands. Then the lights went down, and Aurelia walked out in her old white costume — it still fit; she had basted it — and raised her hands.

The horizon came. A murmur went around the room. The rim of the sun followed, gold, rising, and for a moment she was thirty again and the harbor was spread out below her and the whole city was holding its breath —

— and the light stuttered. Coughed. Went out.

Silence. Somewhere a chair creaked. Aurelia stood in the dark with her hands still raised, trembling, and could not think of a single thing to do.

Then, from the wings, a heron flew across the stage.

Erasmus's shadows were thin now, smoke where they had once been velvet, but they came anyway: the heron, then the skyline, then two figures on a rooftop, young, squared off against each other, and the room understood before she did. She turned. He stood at the edge of the stage with his arms raised, waiting, and she finally saw what he had been asking all along.

She lifted her palm and gave him the last of it — a candle's worth, no more — and his shadows went huge and crisp and beautiful against it, and the day room watched the whole forty years play out on the wall: the duel, the jar, the sunrise handed back like something borrowed. When it ended, the applause went on for a long time, and Brass Badger had to blow his nose twice.

The next morning at six, Aurelia stood in the east-wing hallway and raised her palm to the dead sconce, and stopped.

Down the corridor, Erasmus waited with his cane, on his way to breakfast, slow these days, slower in the dim. She lowered her hand to hip height and made the small tea-colored glow — not for the bulb, not for anyone to see — and held it there so he could read the carpet's edge. His shadow stretched long and elegant down the hallway beside her.

The sconce stayed dark. The hallway didn't.
