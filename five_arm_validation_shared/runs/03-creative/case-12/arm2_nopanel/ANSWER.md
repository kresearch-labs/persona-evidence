# Slow Rain

Nobody at Megalocity Meadows used their old names anymore — except on Tuesdays.

Tuesdays were Demonstration Hour in the sunroom. Harriet Quill, once the Stiletto, who could throw a playing card through sheet steel, now used the same wrist to slice bagels from across the common room, thin and even, to modest applause. Old Volt kept the fish tank warm. The Human Dictionary, whose gift had been every word there was, spent it on the crossword and on correcting the newsletter, which the newsletter resented.

Marisol Vega had been Tempest. She had once held a hurricane over Bay City like a lid on a pot, then set it down gently in the sea when the last busload of evacuees was clear. Presidents had called her. Children had worn her silhouette on lunchboxes.

These days, if the light was right and her heart was calm, she could make it rain on the herb garden. A little rain. A polite rain.

"You should sign up for Tuesday," said the man in the next chair.

"I did sign up. Then I took my name off."

"Then you wrote it back on in my handwriting, so I'd be blamed." Edmund Crane smiled. His smile was the only part of him that had never faded. "I taught you that trick. The Zeppelin job, 1987."

"You tricked me with that trick."

"And you've kept it in beautiful condition."

Edmund had been the Gray Man. For forty years he unmade things — bank vaults, force fields, once, famously, a comet — and the unmaking never stopped at the edges of what he pointed at. It kept going, quietly, into him. His left hand was translucent now. When he held it up you could see the chair back through it, a smudge like a fish in cloudy water. The staff at the Meadows had stopped pretending it wasn't happening and started keeping his favorite chair by the window, where the light made him look solid.

Last month Marisol had gone up for Demonstration Hour anyway. She had reached for the storm the way you reach for a banister in the dark, and gotten one clap of thunder, small as a knuckle on a door, and a drizzle that never reached the azaleas. Someone in the second row had said *aww* — kindly, horribly, the way you say it to a baby holding a spoon. She had bowed. She had not signed up since.

Except, apparently, in Edmund's handwriting.

The night before the demonstration she took her walker out to the garden, which was against the rules and therefore easy, because the night orderly watched television with the volume up. She stood among the herbs and reached. The sky thought about it. A cloud assembled itself out of the dark, thin as spilled milk, let down three drops, and quit.

"You're forcing it," Edmund said from the porch.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"I'm mostly transparent, Marisol. Sleep goes right through me." He came down the ramp slowly, one hand on the rail — the solid hand. "Back in the harbor district, you never forced the weather. You asked."

"I was forty."

"You were insufferable," he agreed. "But you asked."

She sat down next to him on the bench. For a while neither of them said anything, which was the oldest thing they did together. They had spent thirty years defining themselves against each other — he unmade, she overcame; he was patience, she was weather — and then the era ended the way eras do, not with a last battle but with a merger, a buyout, a museum wing. The others had gone one by one. Captain Radiant's photo hung in the lobby now, next to the sign-up sheet, smiling the way the dead are allowed to smile. The two of them were what was left of the run, and some nights that felt like a fact and some nights like a wound.

"I've been unmaking things for people all week," Edmund said. "Knots in sewing yarn. The rust on Harriet's cards. The jar of pickled beets nobody could open." He turned his translucent hand over and watched the porch light come through it. "You know what nobody tells you about unmaking? When it's small, it's a gift. It's the only power I've ever had where giving it away is the entire point."

"How much does it cost you?"

"Less than it's worth."

"Edmund."

"Marisol." He said her name the way he used to say it across rooftops, and she let it go, because she had learned — late, but thoroughly — that you cannot unmake a man's decisions for him. Only he could do that. It was, she supposed, the one thing he had left.

Tuesday came up gray and mild. The sunroom filled. Harriet opened with the bagel and collected her modest applause. Old Volt made the fish tank glow like a night-light, and the room went quiet the way rooms do when they remember being young.

Then Marisol's name was called — her old name, because on Tuesdays they used the old names.

She stood at the front and reached for the storm. She asked this time. She asked the way you ask when you have finally run out of pride: politely, and terrified.

The sky did nothing. The room shifted. Somewhere a wheelchair squeaked, and someone began, kindly, horribly, to say *aww*, and it sounded like the end of a career.

Then Edmund rose from the back row, and the light went through him like stained glass, and he raised his translucent hand toward the window — toward the gray, unremarkable sky — and unmade the heat that held the clouds up.

It was a small unmaking. It was exactly enough.

The rain came down straight and cold and true, the rain of her prime, drumming on the sunroom roof, and Marisol laughed out loud for the first time in a year and gave it a wind — a little one, a curl of it that sent the drops spiraling past the glass. And Edmund, still holding the sky open, unmade gravity's grip on the rain for one full heartbeat, so that it fell upward, glittering, a whole garden's worth of weather climbing the light.

The sunroom came apart with applause. Harriet cried a little and blamed the onions in the bagel. In the second row, a woman who had once been her sidekick stood up and shouted the old name like a cheer.

Afterward they sat on the porch while the rain went on without either of them — ordinary rain, the kind that belongs to everyone.

"Same time next Tuesday?" she said.

"I'll bring the sky," Edmund said. "You bring the nerve."

His hand, when she took it, was almost not there. But it squeezed, and she felt it, and that was the whole trick, the one neither of them had ever needed to write down: you hold on with what you have left, and it is enough — and then next Tuesday, you do it again.
