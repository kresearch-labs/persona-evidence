# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-12

## The Meadows

The flight club met Thursdays on the lawn, weather permitting and otherwise.

"Full clearance this week," announced Iphigenia "Iffy" Corazón, seventy-nine, formerly the Gravity Well, hovering a dignified three inches off the grass in her floral muumuu. "Doctor's orders were *move more*, and I intend to interpret that like a lawyer."

Beneath her, the walking club did its lap: Doctor Meridian, who could no longer fly but could still, at tremendous personal cost, make himself lightly buoyant, shuffled along like a man wading through the world's most polite surf. Beside him, pacing, the Vanquisher — eighty-one, former world conqueror, now world conqueror of the shuffleboard bracket.

"Meridian," the Vanquisher said, without heat, "your buoyancy is drifting into my lane."

"It's a shared sky, Dynamic."

"It's a shared *sidewalk*."

"Which is why we're negotiating. How we always did." The Doctor smiled his old razor smile. "Rematch at cribbage?"

"After I finish creaming Hazel at bocce."

Hazel — Doctor Wraith, in her day the most feared telepath on two continents — sat in the bocce court's shade, ninety-three and magnificent, playing the entire opposing team by floating each ball with a fingertip and a squint. Her opponent, Captain Velocity, formerly the fastest man alive and now the fastest man alive *for eleven seconds*, kept score with a stopwatch and total peace.

The Meadows had rules, posted by the nurses and enforced by consensus: powers at half, no rescues without a partner, and absolutely no re-litigating the Incident Board. The Incident Board hung in the activities room — a corkboard of grudges, decades old, that residents were permitted to move their own pins on but never anyone else's. Solar Flare and Nightwatch had been pinned in stalemate since 1987. The board was sacred. The board was also, everyone knew, why the two of them now played bridge together every single morning, partners, bickering, perfect.

Wednesdays were the hard day. Wednesday was when admissions came, and this Wednesday they brought in Dynamo's old arch-foe, Doctor Ruin — wheelchair now, oxygen tank, the silhouette gone soft. Dynamo heard the elevator before he saw him and was, uncharacteristically, at the door when it opened.

They looked at each other a long moment, two men with a combined four hundred years of grudge.

"Ruin," said Dynamo.

"Dynamic," said Doctor Ruin. "You got old."

"You got old *first*."

"Because I started first." Ruin gripped the offered arm, and something in his spine unknotted. "Is the cafeteria any good?"

"The pudding is a weapon. I'll show you the good chairs."

They were nemeses for forty years; it turned out that was a kind of friendship, the way a whetstone is a kind of companion to a knife. By Friday they were playing cooperative video games against the twins and shouting at each other exactly as much as they ever had, which the nurses agreed was probably therapeutic for everyone but the twins.

The powers never fully left. That was the quiet miracle of the place. Iffy still made the good chairs rise to meet sore hips. Hazel still carried the call-light system on a thread of will, so no one ever waited alone at night. Doctor Meridian, buoyant, ferried trays during staffing shortages, muttering that he'd once lifted a battleship and now the hard part was gravy. And twice a year, on the equinoxes, the flight club did its *Formation* — nine old people, wheelchairs and walkers left in a tidy row, hovering in slow formation over the meadows at dusk, lights on their cardigans, the whole retirement home out on the lawn with cocoa.

The new residents always asked the same question, watching: *Why don't you fight anymore?*

And old Kestrel — ninety, cage-fighter's knuckles, gravity-well trick she could still do with a marble and a coffee cup — gave the answer that got written into the welcome packet:

"Dear, we spent forty years proving who was strongest. There's no more of that to prove. Now we're just proving we can still *fly*, and believe me —" she'd tap the marble, and it would orbit her teacup like a patient moon "— at our age, that's the harder act."

On Thursdays, if you drove past the Meadows at golden hour, you might see them: a line of old heroes and old villains drifting in slow formation over the grass, arguing about altitude, towing the bocce trophy between them on a string.

Nobody on staff ever had the heart to make it stop. Nobody could, really. Some of them could lift buildings once. All of them, together, can still lift an afternoon.
