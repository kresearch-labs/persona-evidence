# The Last Laugh

Every Tuesday the residents of Megalocity Meadows gathered in the sunroom for bingo, and every Tuesday Doctor Vortex cheated.

He did it openly now, which was the strange part. In the old days he'd stolen entire skylines, held the mayor hostage in his own limousine, once swapped the moon's orbit for a week just to watch the astronomers cry. Now he sat at the corner table with a deck of cards he'd learned to shuffle into any order he liked, and he cheated, and everyone pretended not to notice, because the alternative was admitting that the greatest criminal mind of the century had been reduced to winning bingo with marked cards.

"You're doing it again," said Captain Meridian from across the table. His voice still carried the old parade-ground boom, though it had lost the echo that once made bank robbers surrender on principle. "I can see the cards bending."

"I have no idea what you mean," said Vortex, and turned over B-4.

Meridian had been the one who caught him, forty years ago, at the top of the Vesper Building. They'd fought for six hours in the rain, and afterward they'd sat on the ledge together sharing a thermos because neither of them could remember how the fight was supposed to end. That was the thing nobody put in the documentaries: the ending was always improvised. You won, or you lost, or you got tired, and then you went home and did it again next month.

At Megalocity Meadows, the fighting was over. What remained was everything else.

Down the hall, Gorgon kept her eyes closed most days — not because she couldn't control the petrifying anymore, but because closing them made the other residents feel she was trying, and trying counted for something here. She'd been the one who turned the fountain in the courtyard into a statue garden, back in her active years. Now she sat by the window and let Meridian describe the birds to her, and once a week she opened her eyes for exactly three seconds to look at the photograph of her old crew, and nothing in the photo had ever turned to stone, which everyone agreed was progress.

In the activity room, the Human Dynamo — who had once powered half of Eastport with his bare hands during the Blackout of '89 — spent his afternoons charging the TV remote. It was a small thing. It was not a small thing to him. The nurses had stopped replacing the batteries years ago because Dynamo took it as a personal mission, and a man who has held a city in his hands needs a personal mission, even if the mission is four ounces of plastic.

Vortex won the bingo round, as he always did. He stood to collect his prize — a coupon for the cafeteria, redeemable for one (1) complimentary pudding — and paused at the microphone.

"A speech," said Meridian. "He's going to give a speech."

"I am not going to give a speech," said Vortex, and gave a speech. It was about the old days, about the time he'd stolen the harbor bridge and Meridian had caught him halfway across with the rivets still warm. He got the details wrong on purpose, because Meridian would correct him, and correcting him meant Meridian would stand up, and standing up was the thing Meridian's doctors said he shouldn't do, and hadn't, in eleven months.

Meridian stood up.

"Three bridges," he said. "You stole three bridges, and I caught you on all of them, and the third time you cried."

"I did not cry."

"You cried like a man whose mother was watching."

The room laughed, and Vortex laughed too, and for a moment the sunroom smelled like rain on hot pavement, which was the weather on the night of the third bridge, and everyone felt it at the same time without anyone naming it.

Later, after the lights went down, Vortex found Meridian in the corridor doing the slow, furious arithmetic of getting from the wall to his door without the wheelchair.

"Let me," said Vortex.

"I don't need your help."

"I wasn't offering help." Vortex snapped his fingers — a small, tired snap, a shadow of the gesture that had once opened bank vaults in three cities — and the floor beneath Meridian tilted, gently, two degrees, so that the last six feet became downhill. "I'm cheating. It's what I do."

Meridian rolled forward, caught himself, and stood at his door breathing hard and grinning like a man who'd just gotten away with something.

"Three bridges," he said.

"Three bridges," Vortex agreed, and went back down the hall to his room, where the marked cards waited on the nightstand in an order only he knew, and the pudding coupon sat in the drawer with the others — forty-one of them now, unredeemed, because winning was the point and pudding had never been.

Outside, the city went on without any of them: sirens somewhere, a helicopter, the distant sound of a world that still needed saving and had found other people to save it. Inside, Gorgon opened her eyes for three seconds. The photograph held. Down the hall, Dynamo charged the remote. And in the sunroom, the cards shuffled themselves one last time in the dark, quietly, the way old men practice things no one will ever see, just to be sure they still can.
