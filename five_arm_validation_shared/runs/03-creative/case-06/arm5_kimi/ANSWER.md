# The Eleven-Week Man

The plan was perfect. I'd watched the Meridian Trust on Halsted for eleven weeks. I knew the guard's limp, the tellers' rotation, the armored car's Tuesday window. I knew the man who sold coffee on the corner every morning, because I was there every morning, and a man who stands still long enough starts to notice the other men standing still.

At 9:14 on a Thursday I walked in with a note and a nylon bag and hands that did not shake.

The teller's name tag said DANA. She read my note — *I have a gun. Fill the bag. No dye packs, no alarms* — and looked up at me with something I could not place. Not fear. Pity.

She slid a note back across the counter.

*So am I. Don't ruin this. Thirty seconds.*

I was still reading it when the doors blew open behind me.

Three men in ski masks, shotguns up, shouting the choreography of every robbery I'd ever rehearsed in my head. "Everybody down! This is a robbery!" The lobby screamed and dropped. I dropped too, out of pure professional reflex, and that was when the man in the gray suit beside me — briefcase, loafers, had been waiting in line like a mortgage applicant — produced a snub-nosed .38 and leveled it at the crew.

"Nobody moves," he said. "This bank is being robbed."

"That's what we just said," the lead mask said.

"By me," the suit said. "It's being robbed by me."

From the floor, an old woman in a church hat sighed like the world had personally disappointed her, reached into her purse, and came out with a derringer. "Oh, for heaven's sake," she said. "I have been waiting in this line for forty minutes."

The guard — limp and all — drew his sidearm. Not on them. On the vault. "Nobody's getting into that cage except me," he said, and his voice had the calm of a man who had been skimming the cash deliveries for six years and had picked today, of all days, to stop being careful.

Nine guns. I counted them from the floor with my cheek against the marble. Nine guns, and mine was a note.

The manager came out of his office with his hands up, and he was laughing. It was the worst sound in the room. Mr. Okafor, fifty-five, photographed in the Tribune for community outreach. He looked at the masks, the suit, the derringer, the guard, me, and he laughed until he had to hold the doorframe.

"The vault is empty," he said. "There is no money. There hasn't been any money in nine years." He spread his hands like a magician finishing a trick. "I've been carrying this bank on my own books since the merger. The deposits are numbers. The numbers are mine. You're all robbing a photograph of a bank."

Nobody lowered a weapon. That's the thing about a gun: once it's out, it wants a reason.

"You're lying," the lead mask said.

"Open it," Okafor said, and tossed him the vault keys, and the jingle they made on the marble was the loneliest sound I have ever heard.

I looked at Dana. Dana was not looking at the guns. Dana was looking at her screen, where a progress bar was crawling toward a hundred percent, and I understood all at once, the way you understand a punch after it lands. A wire that size needs the branch in robbery protocol — the fraud ceiling lifts when the alarm state does, because the system assumes the money is about to move anyway. She hadn't been caught in my heist. She had been waiting for one. Any heist. A room full of them was more than she'd ever prayed for.

The bar hit a hundred. She exhaled, stood up slow with her hands raised, and as she passed me on her way to the floor she pressed something into my jacket pocket with two fingers, smooth as a pickpocket running in reverse.

"For the diversion," she whispered.

Then the coffee cart man kicked in the door.

He came in low and fast with a badge on a lanyard and a pistol that knew its job, and behind him the street filled with windbreakers that said FBI. "Eleven weeks," he said to the room, almost apologetic, and I realized he hadn't been selling coffee. He'd been taking inventory. Every man who stands still long enough starts to notice the other men standing still — and he'd noticed all of us.

What happened next was not a shootout, because there was nothing to shoot over. It was a surrender with too many authors. Masks came off. Guns went down on the marble one by one, like the end of a very strange Mass. The suit sat down in a waiting chair and put his head in his hands. The old woman asked an agent if she could still make a withdrawal, and nobody laughed, because the vault was open and everyone could see she was the only honest customer the bank had left.

In the confusion — and a room where nine robbers are being sorted into pairs of handcuffs is a deep confusion — nobody was watching the man whose only crime was a note. I stood. I straightened my jacket. I walked out past the coffee cart, and the agent behind it looked at me for one long second and then looked back at the room, where the guns were.

On the sidewalk I finally put my hand in my pocket. A cashier's check, folded twice. More than my note would have ever gotten me, even if the vault had been full.

The plan was perfect. It just wasn't mine.
