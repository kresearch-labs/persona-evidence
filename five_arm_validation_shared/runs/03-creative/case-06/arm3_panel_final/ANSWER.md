# The Tuesday Shift

The plan was simple because simple survives: in at 9:14, out by 9:31, before the Brink truck and after the night-drop, when the vault sat fat and the lobby sat empty. You'd done the math on a napkin. Three minutes inside the cage, eleven steps to the service door, and a new name in a city where nobody asked questions.

You were the only one in the lobby wearing a delivery jacket, which was the point. Nobody looks at a courier.

Except everyone was looking at everyone.

You noticed it at the door — the way the man in the gray suit held it a half-second too long, scanning the room like a man checking for rain. The teller with the steady hands who didn't greet you. The old woman by the pens who stood like a soldier at rest. The security guard, whose eyes were wrong — not bored, but counting.

You walked to the counter. You slid the note across — the one you'd printed in a font chosen to look like the bank's own — and the teller read it, and her face did something you'd never seen in eleven years of branch work.

She looked relieved.

"Finally," she whispered, and reached under the counter, and came up with a pistol she racked with the calm of long practice. "I thought I was going to have to do this alone."

That was the first wrong thing. The second was the sound of a deadbolt — the man in the gray suit, locking the front door from the inside, keying a code he should not have known. The third was the old woman by the pens, pulling a shotgun from her coat in pieces, assembling it like a woman who'd done it in the dark before.

"Nobody move," said the guard, drawing his weapon — and then, absurdly, "This is a robbery."

"Ours," said the teller.

"Mine," said the guard.

"Actually," said the gray suit, "it's mine. I've had people in this branch for six weeks."

You stood there with your note and your toy — because that's what it was, a replica, weighted with washers — and you understood the shape of the disaster. The vault was open for two minutes a day. Everyone in this room had found the same two minutes. Everyone in this room had done the same math on the same napkin.

The lobby held nine people. You counted. A courier, a teller, a guard, a gray suit, an old woman with a shotgun, a young man in a bike helmet who had been "waiting for a notary," a woman in scrubs who had been "making a deposit," a janitor with a cart, and a teenager who had been "opening an account" and was now holding a sawed-off like he'd seen it in a movie.

Nine robbers. Zero customers. The bank, at 9:14 on a Tuesday, was the most criminal room in the city.

"Vault opens at 9:22," the teller said. "Time-lock. Nobody gets it before then, so we can shoot each other for eight minutes or we can be professionals."

"Professionals," said the gray suit, and everyone, absurdly, nodded.

That was the mistake. Professionals have a plan for the other professionals.

The eight minutes became a negotiation conducted with weapons lowered and eyes never lowering. Territories were drawn: the teller knew the drop sequence, so she took the cash drawers. The guard knew the camera loop, so he took the security room. The old woman knew the vault's second door, behind the deposit boxes, so she took the boxes. The gray suit claimed the dye packs, which meant the bait money, which meant he'd done this before.

You claimed the alley door, because you'd always claimed the alley door, and because you were beginning to understand that the only way out of a room full of robbers is to be the one who leaves first.

At 9:21, the janitor's cart squeaked.

Everyone had forgotten the janitor. That was the fourth wrong thing.

He wasn't a janitor. The cart held a floor buffer and a set of drills, and the drills were for the vault's hinges, because the janitor — the real plan, underneath everyone's plan — had been hired three weeks ago by someone who knew the time-lock could be beaten from the inside, and that someone was not in the room.

"Who hired you?" the gray suit demanded.

The janitor smiled. "The same person who's watching you right now."

And the fifth wrong thing was the television in the corner, the one playing the muted morning news, which cut — a frame, a flicker — to a shot of this branch, this lobby, from a camera above the door. A live feed. Someone was watching the room full of robbers rob each other, and the someone had arranged the whole morning like a dinner party.

The time-lock clicked. 9:22.

Nobody moved. Nine professionals, and every one of them suddenly sure the money was the trap.

"Walk away," the old woman said quietly, to all of you. "The vault's a stage. Whatever they want, it isn't cash."

The teenager laughed and went for the door, and the door was locked, and the lock was new, and the code the gray suit had used to seal it now refused him. The lights went amber. The shutters came down over the windows with a sound like a guillotine being polite.

"Containment protocol," the teller breathed. "They're not robbing the bank. They're using us to open it, and then they're sealing it."

The janitor set his drills down, rolled his cart to the vault as the door swung wide on its timer, and began loading bags — not cash, the boxes, the safe-deposit boxes, specific ones, numbered, pre-listed — while the rest of you stood in the amber light like exhibits.

You did the only smart thing left. You took off the delivery jacket, folded it over your arm, sat down in a lobby chair, and became a man waiting for the bank to open.

When the police arrived — four minutes later, too fast, as if summoned — they found a lobby of calm citizens and one janitor who had, by every camera, simply finished his shift. The gray suit had a story. The teller had a story. The old woman was gone, and so was the guard, and the teenager was crying, and you were a courier with no packages and no note and no weapon, because you'd left all three in the bin by the pens.

The detectives walked the room and found nothing but a bank that had, for eight minutes, contained nine crimes and no criminals.

The heist had gone wrong, you decided, in the only way it could have. You'd planned to steal from the bank.

The bank had planned to steal from you.
