# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-06

## Everybody's Inside Job

The plan was simple, which should have been my first warning. Simple plans survive contact with normal people. Mine had to survive whatever this lobby full of people was.

Here's what I walked into at 10:02 on a Tuesday, wearing a courier jacket and a duffel of fake invoices: forty-one customers in line, four tellers, one branch manager — and every single person in the building, in that first frozen second when the lights flickered, reaching for something. A wire. A vial. A rosary with a hidden blade. A laminated "MAY I HELP YOU" sign that I later learned was steel-cored.

The old man beside me unfolded a cane into a rifle. The teller nearest the vault smiled like a choir member and drew a pistol from the change drawer. A woman dressed as a lost tourist opened her map to reveal a floor plan identical to mine, with my own pen marks on it, because I'd sold her the map six weeks ago through an intermediary and she'd had the audacity to *use* it.

"Gentlepeople," I announced, raising my hands. "It appears we've all had the same idea."

Somebody shot the ceiling out of professional habit. The alarms started, which was a problem, because the alarm was *my* problem — I'd built a silence window into it. My silence window. Which now, apparently, benefited everyone.

Here's the thing about a bank full of robbers: nobody calls the police. It's the only crime scene in the world where the victims are also suspects. The branch manager, Almeida — I found out her name later — understood this instantly, and did the most gangster thing I saw all day: she sat down at her desk, laminated sign across her lap, and started taking notes. "You have eleven minutes before the security patrol rounds the corner," she said. "I suggest you sort out who's robbing whom."

What followed was eleven minutes of the most polite violent negotiation in financial history.

The counterfeit group — the Delgados, family business, third generation — wanted the safety-deposit boxes, not the cash. They'd heard a client had parked bearer bonds in box 214, and the bonds had already been claimed by the bank's own insurer in a fraud the Delgados could prove but not afford to litigate. The teller with the pistol, Mireya, had been laundering money for her own branch manager for years and wanted the ledger — Almeida's ledger — because if the audit landed Thursday she'd be the fall. The old man with the cane wasn't after money at all; his late wife's letter was in box 118, misfiled in probate, and the bank had lost it three times. The tourist woman — Ana — wanted to know who had been selling maps of the bank, because she'd been casing this place for a year and was furious that someone had monetized her reconnaissance.

That was me. I raised my hand. She was not consoled.

And the vault? The vault's time-lock was my design — an inside man had fed me the specs — and it opened at 10:09, which meant the whole miserable orchestra converged on a room that could genuinely hold everyone's treasure. The bonds were real. The ledger was real. The letter was in 118, creased and yellow, dated 1963. I read the room right: everyone here had been wronged by this bank in a specific, documentable way, and the bank's security was a posture, not a practice.

So I did the only profitable thing left. "New proposal," I said. "Everyone gets their document. Nobody takes cash — cash is traceable, cash is a felony, and forty-one fingerprints on the drawer is a career. We walk out with paperwork, the bank eats its own audit, and the insurance pays. And Almeida—" I nodded at her ledger "—retires the Mireya problem quietly, or Thursday eats us all."

Mireya lowered her pistol. Almeida's pen did not stop moving. The Delgados and the old man looked at each other across a lobby that had, in eleven minutes, become something between a hostage situation and a homeowners' association.

We were out by 10:14, the vault relocked, the silence window closed behind us. Nobody called the cops, because nobody could afford the questions. The letter reached the old man; the ledger reached the auditor by Tuesday; the bonds reached the Delgados' lawyer; Ana reached into my courier jacket on the way out and took back her map — "You owe me royalties" — and then, because it was that kind of morning, her number.

The bank, humiliated by its own alarm logs, settled everything without pressing charges. And I got out of the heist business — mostly — because once you've seen forty robbers agree on terms, honest work feels like a step down.

I kept in touch with the crew, informally. We meet on Tuesdays. The bank still doesn't know.
