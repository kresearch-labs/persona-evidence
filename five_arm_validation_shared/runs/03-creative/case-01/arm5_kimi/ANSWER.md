# The Gates

The first thing I noticed about heaven was that it had a waiting room.

The second thing I noticed was her.

She was sitting on a bench outside the gates — and they were gates, actual gates, wrought iron and pearl, just like the jokes said — with her elbows on her knees and her chin in her hands, like someone who had been sitting there for a very long time and had run out of patience somewhere around year forty. Her wings were folded wrong. I didn't know wings could fold wrong, but hers did, the way a person's shoulders hunch after a double shift.

She looked up when I arrived, and her face did something complicated.

"You," she said.

"Me," I said, because what do you say to that.

She stood up. She was shorter than I expected. I don't know why I expected a guardian angel to be tall. Maybe because mine had always felt like a weather system — something vast and invisible that occasionally knocked me sideways for my own good.

"Daniel Okafor," she said, reading from a clipboard that appeared in her hand the way things do in dreams. "Sixty-eight years. Do you know how many interventions that was?"

"I feel like you're going to tell me."

"Four thousand, two hundred and eleven." She let the clipboard dissolve. "That's not a normal number. That's not a number that happens. The average is three hundred. Three hundred, Daniel. You were four thousand."

I looked at the gates. They were beautiful. They were also closed.

"Can I—" I gestured at them.

"In a minute." She sat back down on the bench, and after a moment, I sat down next to her. Up close, she looked tired in a way I recognized. My mother had looked like that. I probably had something to do with both of them looking like that.

"I want to go through the list," she said. "Is that petty? I've been told it's petty. I don't care anymore."

"Okay," I said.

"Age seven. You climbed the water tower behind the school because Marcus Webb said you wouldn't. Do you remember that?"

"Vaguely."

"You fell. You fell forty feet, Daniel. I caught you with both hands and a prayer I wasn't technically authorized to say. You got up, dusted yourself off, and told Marcus Webb he owed you five dollars." She rubbed her eyes. "I aged. Angels don't age. I aged."

"I'm sorry?"

"Don't apologize yet. We're on age seven." She held up her hand and started counting on her fingers. "Age sixteen, the motorcycle. Age nineteen, the other motorcycle. Age twenty-three, you decided to find out if you could swim across the reservoir. You could not. Age twenty-six, the rooftop in Lagos during the storm. Age thirty-one—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—the war."

I was quiet for a moment. "You were there for that."

"I was there for all of it. That's the job." She looked at her hands. "The convoy, the checkpoint, the night you carried that boy four kilometers with shrapnel in your leg. I was screaming at you the whole time. You couldn't hear me. They never hear me. I was screaming *stop, stop, you'll die,* and you kept walking."

"He lived," I said. "The boy. He lived. I got a letter from him every Christmas after."

"I know," she said. "I read them over your shoulder. It was the only perk."

A breeze moved through whatever place this was. Somewhere behind the gates, faintly, I could hear music — not choirs, exactly, something warmer and stranger, like every song anyone had ever loved playing at once, far away.

"Here's what I don't understand," she said. "I filed reports. Did you know we file reports? Every intervention, a report. And in four thousand, two hundred and eleven reports, I kept writing the same thing in the notes field, and I kept getting called in to explain it, and I never could."

"What did you write?"

"*He knew.*" She turned to look at me, and her eyes were very old and very tired and very, very angry, and underneath all of that, something else. "Every time. The water tower, the reservoir, the checkpoint. You knew the odds. You did the arithmetic — I watched you do it, that little pause you had, that breath you took — and you went anyway. Not because you were stupid. I've guarded stupid. Stupid is easy. You were *brave*, Daniel, and brave is a nightmare. Brave is a full-time job with no sleep."

"I didn't know anyone was watching," I said quietly. "I would have— I don't know. Written fewer reports for you."

"That's the worst part," she said, and now she was almost smiling, which seemed to cost her something. "If you'd known, you'd have been careful for *my* sake, and then you'd have been a different man, and then—" She stopped. She looked at the gates.

"Then what?"

She was silent for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, older, the weather system I'd always half-felt and never heard.

"Then the boy doesn't get carried. The woman on the bus in '09 doesn't get her purse back and doesn't make her interview and doesn't become the surgeon who saves eleven hundred people. The man you talked down off the parking structure in 2017 doesn't go home to his daughter. I ran the numbers, Daniel. We can do that here. I ran them after you died, because I had nothing but time and a clipboard and four thousand reports that all said *he knew*." She took a breath she didn't technically need. "You being you — reckless, exhausting, impossible you — the arithmetic comes out to forty-one thousand people whose lives bend because you didn't sit still."

I didn't have anything to say to that. My eyes were stinging, which surprised me. I'd have thought you were done with that, after.

"So," she said, standing, brushing off a robe that didn't need brushing, "here is my official position, which I am stating for the record, once, and never repeating: you drove me absolutely insane. You were the worst assignment in the history of assignments. When you finally died — peacefully, in your sleep, which was the *one* considerate thing you ever did — I requested a transfer. To anyone. To a houseplant."

"And?"

"And they laughed at me." She walked to the gates, and the gates, without being touched, began to open. The music swelled. The light came through like morning through a curtain. "They said I'd requested you. Before either of us was anything. They said I looked at your file — the whole file, everything you'd ever do, every gray hair you'd give me — and I said *that one. I want that one.*"

She stood in the light, wings unfolding at last, folding right this time, and held out her hand.

"Come on," she said. "You've kept me waiting long enough. And Daniel—"

"Yeah?"

"There's a water tower in there. Don't even think about it."

I took her hand, and we went through the gates together.
