# The File

The light at the end was not a tunnel. It was a waiting room — pale, quiet, smelling of rain on hot pavement — with a single desk at the far end, and behind the desk a woman in a cardigan the color of weak tea.

I knew her before I saw her face. I knew her the way you know a voice on the phone after one word.

"Sixty-one years," she said, without looking up. "Four months, eleven days. And you couldn't have waited until I finished my coffee."

"I died," I said.

"You died at seven-forty in the morning, watering tomatoes, in your slippers." She turned a page. "I had just sat down."

The lamp caught her name tag. It didn't have a name on it. It had mine, and a range of years, the way headstones do.

"You're—"

"Rilla," she said. "For your purposes. I've had others. You knew me as the feeling that made you check the oven twice."

"I knew you as the feeling that made me check it four times."

"That was me being thorough."

She opened a folder thick as a phone book and began dealing index cards onto the desk like a bad hand of canasta.

"Is that every—"

"Every one. Cross-referenced." Card, card, card. "June 1971. A parking space, your wedding day."

"It worked."

"It was a fire lane. You prayed a fire lane into existence and a caterer got a ticket. I hope you're proud."

"December 1979. A snow day."

"You got the snow day. The plow driver got a divorce. These things have weight. Nothing up here is free."

"March 1988," I said.

She stopped dealing. "March 1988," she said, and her voice changed, and she squared the deck and tapped it twice, the way I had always imagined her doing, back when I still believed the prayers went somewhere. To someone. A someone with hands.

That was the year of the hospital. I didn't say so. Neither did she.

"You want to know what drove me insane," she said. "It wasn't the parking spaces."

"Wasn't it?"

"It was March." She said it the way you'd say the name of a war. "The rules allow nudging. Suggesting. Arranging. I could get you a parking space. I could not get you a Tuesday. Do you understand the difference? You prayed for a Tuesday every night for eleven weeks, and every night I stood there with my hands full of nudges and nothing they fit."

I hadn't known angels could raise their voices. Hers didn't, exactly. It went thin, the way paper does.

"You were there," I said. "That last night. In the room."

"I was there every night. That night I held the door for the doctors." She took off her glasses and polished them on her cardigan, which was a thing I had done my whole life and never told anyone and certainly never prayed about. "That's what I'm for, at the end. Doors."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere behind me, the light hummed.

"You stopped praying after that," she said. "Eleven weeks of Tuesdays, and then nothing. A file that stops growing is not a comfort, whatever it looks like from your side."

"I didn't know you kept score."

"I don't keep score. I keep you." She said it flatly, like a correction, and reached into the folder for one more card, and held it out.

It was blank. No date, no words. Just the faint dent of a pen that had pressed hard and written nothing.

"What's this one?"

"October ninth. Nineteen ninety."

I turned it over, as if the back might explain the front. "I don't remember October ninth."

"I know," she said, and took it back, and filed it between two dated ones, careful, the way you file the important things.

Then she turned a clipboard toward me. "Sign here. Cause of death, next of kin, and whether you were satisfied with the service."

"Nobody reads that."

"I read that. I read everything. That's the whole job."

I signed. She stamped the page — a small, final sound, a period at the end of a sentence — and behind her, where there had been a wall, there was now a gate, open, with ordinary morning on the other side. Not glory. Morning. Someone was mowing a lawn somewhere; I could hear it.

"You never said thank you," she said. "Sixty-one years. Not once. You said 'again,' and 'hurry,' and once, memorably, 'this is a scam.'"

"The parking space was a scam."

"The parking space was a miracle with a paperwork problem." She stood and straightened her cardigan, and for a second she looked less like a clerk and more like what she actually was, which I will not try to describe, because I'd get it wrong. "Go on. You're expected."

I got as far as the gate before I turned around. She was already seated again, already reaching for a fresh file — someone else's. I could see the name on the tag. A stranger's. A range of years with no end date on it yet.

"Rilla."

She looked up.

"Thank you. For the Tuesdays."

For a moment she was very still. Then she said, "You were the job," which from her, I understood at last, was the other thing people say.

I walked through. Behind me, already speaking to someone new, I heard her say: "All right. Let's see what you've got."
