# The Wait

The gates of heaven are smaller than you'd think. White, yes, and tall, but narrow — more garden fence than fortress — and outside them, on a folding chair that had rusted at the hinges, sat my guardian angel.

I recognized her the way you recognize a song from your childhood: not the face, which I'd never seen, but the posture. Shoulders braced. Chin up. The exact way I used to sit at hospital bedsides.

She looked at me and her face did three things at once — relief, fury, and something older than either.

"You're late," she said.

"I just died."

"Eleven years ago."

I stood there with my hands in the pockets of a body I no longer needed and tried to do the math. The last thing I remembered was my sister's hand in mine going slack, and before that, eleven years of mornings in my apartment, coffee for one, the radio telling me the weather in a city I never went outside to check.

"I've been alive," I said. "I've been right there. Fourth floor, the window with the cracked pane—"

"You've been dead since the scaffolding fell," she said. "March the ninth. You walked out of your body like a man leaving a movie he didn't like, and you went home, and you stayed."

"I would have noticed."

"You didn't notice anything. That was the whole problem. That was seventy-one years of the whole problem."

She picked up a clipboard from the chair. It was fat with pages, swollen from damp, the way paper gets when someone carries it through weather no one else can feel.

"My name," she said, "is Sahaquiel. You called me the hum in your chest. You called me the shiver at the back of your neck. You called it luck, when you called it anything at all, and you called it that because it was easier than believing someone was up all night doing the job."

She opened the clipboard.

"The bicycle, when you were eight. The car came through the hedge and I screamed *turn* and you heard wind. The pool at twelve — you were under for two minutes and I sat on the bottom of that pool with my hands on your ribs, and I am not supposed to do that, do you understand? We are supposed to *suggest*. I held your lungs closed like a fist around a secret, and when you coughed, your mother thanked God, and I got a commendation, and you got a popsicle."

"Sahaquiel—"

"The pills at thirty-four. The bridge at fifty. The ladder, the ice, the man with the knife on the 4 bus. I have a ledger," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle, "of every single time I shouted and you heard weather."

I sat down on the ground next to her chair, because my legs had gone the way legs go.

"Why are you here?" I asked. "At the gates. Don't you have — I don't know. Harp practice."

"The rules say a guardian waits at the gate for her charge," she said. "It's meant to take a moment. The soul crosses over, the angel hands over the ledger, and then there's a ceremony, and then you're done. Retired. Garden. I have been sitting in this chair for eleven years because you would not walk through the door."

"I didn't know there was a door."

"You knew. I told you. Every day for eleven years I stood in your kitchen and said *it's over, it's done, come with me*, and you looked through me like you'd looked through me your whole life, and I thought — I thought I had finally been driven mad, because I was talking to a dead man who would not admit it, and I could not leave, and I could not make you hear."

"Why couldn't I hear you?"

"Because you'd spent your whole life ignoring your conscience," she said. "Why would you start listening when it finally had something kind to say?"

That landed the way the truth lands — not hard, but deep, like a stone dropped into a well you thought was dry.

"I wasn't ignoring you," I said. "At the end. These eleven years. I was waiting."

"Waiting."

"My sister. Ellie. She was alive. I wasn't going to walk through some gate into a place she couldn't follow. I've left enough people behind. I wasn't going to do it on purpose, from the other side."

The angel put down the clipboard. For a long moment she looked at me the way you look at a locked door when the key has been in your pocket the whole time.

"She died last Tuesday," she said gently. "You felt it. That's why you came."

"I followed her."

"You followed her." She laughed — one syllable, wet at the edges. "Do you know what I thought? Eleven years, I thought: he's stubborn. He's spiteful. He will haunt that kitchen until the building falls down around him. And it was love. It was the one thing I never once had to shout at you about, and I wasn't there to see it, because I was here, waiting, being insane about the waiting."

She stood. She folded the chair — it closed with a click like a period at the end of a very long sentence — and she tore the pages out of the clipboard, one by one, and let them go. The ledger of my survival lifted over the gate and came apart into white, into nothing, into weather.

"Ellie's angel is inside," she said. "Her name is Rikhtiel. We've been playing cards. She cheats. Ellie's been asking for you since Tuesday, and Rikhtiel has been telling her you're coming, and Ellie — who listened to her conscience, by the way, for the first time in her life — said, *he'll come. He always comes.*"

The gates were open. They had been open the whole time. That was the part that undid me — not the light, not the singing, but the fact that the door had never once been shut.

Sahaquiel offered me her arm.

"For the record," she said, "you were worth it. Every shout. Every pool. Every night."

I took her arm, and I believed her, which was the first time in my life I believed anything on the first try.

"Come on," she said. "You're late for everything. Don't be late for this."
