# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 03-creative case-01

## The Gatekeeper's Complaint

Mara arrived at the gates of heaven at 4:17 in the afternoon, eternity-time, and found her guardian angel sitting on a folding chair beside a card table, shuffling a deck of index cards with the weary deliberation of a man who had done his taxes for six hundred years.

"You," said the angel, standing up. "You're late."

"I died on schedule," said Mara.

"Forty years late. Do you know what your file looks like? Your file is a *novel*. In bad handwriting. Mine." He flipped through the deck. "May 3rd, 1991: rides bicycle down staircase. On a dare. From a *fish*."

"There was a fish on the landing," said Mara. "I had to."

"Nobody *has* to ride a bicycle down a staircase because of a fish."

The angel sat back down. He looked, up close, older than she remembered — though he had never exactly had a face. More of a suggestion of one, the way a word looks familiar before you place it. He poured two cups of something steaming from a thermos and pushed one across the folding table.

"Drink. You're going to want to sit. We have to go through the ledger, and the ledger is thick."

"Is this the part where I get judged?"

"This is the part where *I* get judged, technically. Forty years of guardian duty, evaluated on outcomes. You were the outcome." He sipped. "Do you know what my supervisor wrote in the margin of your file? 'Notable achievement: subject survived to age 71 against all management advice.'"

Mara laughed, and the laugh surprised her. She hadn't laughed in months. The gates behind the angel shimmered the way heat does over asphalt.

"Read me the worst one," she said.

He adjusted the glasses he didn't wear. "'Age 22. Subject decides to move to Lisbon with a man she met on a ferry. I intervene: her ferry is delayed, her suitcase is stolen, she misses the last train. Standard discouragement protocol. She calls her sister, borrows money, goes anyway. *Outcome:* the man was unfaithful within the year, she kept the cat, learned Portuguese, and started the bakery.'" He looked up. "Do you know how many guardian reports contain the phrase 'started the bakery'? It's in the top ten of things that make our jobs hard. You were supposed to come home humiliated. Instead you came home with a *recipe*."

"The cat lived nineteen years," said Mara.

"The cat," said the angel, with feeling, "was not in your interest assessment. The cat was a wildcard. The cat had no business living nineteen years." He set down the cards. "And then there's Thomas."

Marta — Mara — stopped smiling. "Don't."

"You asked me, in November of '88, standing on the bridge, whether I was even *there*. You asked me to prove it. I had no proof to give you. That's the rule. And you said, 'Then I'm done being managed,' and you walked home in the rain, and I had to log it as a *voluntary severance of attention*." His voice did something at the end of the sentence.

Mara looked at her hands. Young hands, here — that was how it worked, they gave you the age you felt.

"I did that," she said. "I'm sorry. I thought if you wouldn't show me anything, then showing up was enough. And it wasn't. I needed a sign or a hand or — I don't know. Something."

"I know." The angel turned his cup in a slow circle. "And here's my report card, if you want it. When you stopped listening, I kept watching. Not because of duty. Because I'd spent forty years learning you, and you were the most interesting assignment I ever had, and I was *angry* about it. Do you know what my supervisor calls that?" He slid a yellowed card across the table. It was small, like a recipe. On it, in bad handwriting, was the word *Bakery*, and under it, a list of everyone who had ever eaten there, and the years they came.

"What is this?"

"The good you did that I never told you about. The ledger has two columns, and I only ever logged mine." He stood, and folded the chair. "You drove me absolutely insane for forty years. You never once took the safe road, you fed strays, you married the wrong man twice, you cried at other people's weddings harder than the mothers, and you told me in a Chinese restaurant in 1994 that if heaven ran on the bakery's kitchen rules — everyone eats, no one eats alone — you'd go in quietly."

"I remember."

"The gates don't run on bakery rules." He smiled, or his face suggested it. "But I put in a request. Forty years of management. One recommendation. It's the only one I get."

"And?"

"And it's not my call anymore." The angel stepped back toward the shimmer, and for the first time he looked light, like a man at the end of a shift. "It never was. That was the whole thing you were right about, in the rain, in '88. I was never your judge. I was just — the one who stayed."

Mara picked up the card. On the back, in worse handwriting, it said: *Worth it.*

She walked through the gates, and did not look back, and this — the report notes, in margin, in bad handwriting — was, for once, exactly as planned.
