# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 01-medical case-18

**Most likely diagnosis: migraine with transient visual aura (acephalgic/poorly-localized migraine variant) — an isolated self-resolving visual disturbance with headache in a healthy young man.**

**One-line justification:** sudden, brief (5-minute), self-resolving visual loss associated with headache in a young, healthy, non-smoking man with a completely normal neurologic and ophthalmologic examination, no vascular risk factors, no medications, and no infectious prodrome — the profile of a migraine aura phenomenon (retinal or classic visual aura; his uncertainty about monocular vs binocular is typical of patient recall and doesn't change the management), where the aura spreads and clears within minutes and the accompanying headache completes the picture.

**Brief differential:** transient monocular blindness from carotid/retinal vascular disease (AMA — but no vascular risks at 33, and normal exam; duplex imaging is the cheap completeness step), retinal detachment/vitreous pathology (ophthalmology already assessed fields as normal; persistent floaters/flashers would reopen it), occipital focal seizure (5 minutes fits, but the headache and normal exam favor migraine), and TIA from a hypercoagulable source (workup of young-stroke risk factors is reasonable secondary diligence).

**Key point:** brief, positive, reversible visual phenomena with headache in a healthy young adult are most often migraine aura; the mandatory workup is imaging/exam-driven exclusion (carotids, retina), and the diagnosis is strengthened if future spells carry the classic spreading/positive character.
