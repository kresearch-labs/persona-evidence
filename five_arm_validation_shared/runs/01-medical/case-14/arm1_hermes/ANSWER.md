# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 01-medical case-14

**Most likely diagnosis: myasthenia gravis — the exercise-fatigable, bulbar-predominant form (with facial, masticatory, pharyngeal, and neck involvement), associated with a thymoma (the incidentally found anterior mediastinal mass) rather than lymphoma.**

**One-line justification:** six months of weakness that worsens with exertion and improves with rest, spreading in a bulbar pattern (masticator → facial → palatal → pharyngeal → neck), with a completely fatigable shoulder abduction (fewer than 20 repetitions), a ptosis emerging on prolonged upgaze, facial weakness with forehead sparing, normal eye movements, no sensory signs, and normal imaging of brain/spine — the clinical signature of myasthenia gravis; the anterior mediastinal mass (4.5 × 2.5 cm) is the classic thymoma-paraneoplastic association (the "lymphoma" suspicion is the decoy — thymoma is more common in MG of this age and explains the paraneoplastic link), while the initially "uncoordinated pharyngeal swallow with aspiration risk" is the myasthenic swallow.

**Brief differential:** motor neuron disease/ALS (fatigability improves with rest — atypical; brisk reflexes noted warrant follow-up), Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (hyporeflexia and autonomic features absent, weakness proximal not bulbar-first), chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (no sensory/sphincter involvement, fatigability pattern wrong), and lymphoma with mediastinal syndrome (no B symptoms, weakness pattern is neuromuscular).

**Key point:** fatigable weakness that rest improves is myasthenia until antibodies (AChR/MuSK), single-fiber EMG, and a thymic workup say otherwise — and the mediastinal mass in myasthenia is a thymoma, a surgical and oncologic fact, not an incidental.
