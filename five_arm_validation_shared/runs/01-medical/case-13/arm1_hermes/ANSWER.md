# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 01-medical case-13

**Most likely diagnosis: giant cell (temporal) arteritis with arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (A-AION) — rapidly progressing to near-bilateral blindness.**

**One-line justification:** a 75-year-old woman with a year of masseter pain (jaw claudication), three months of temporal/occipital scalp pain, month-old enlarged, weakly pulsatile temporal arteries, an ESR of 113 mm/h, and fundoscopy-proven ischemic optic neuropathy (pale swollen disks, deteriorating acuity to 0.08/light-perception within a week) — the ACR clinical picture of giant cell arteritis, in which arteritic AION is the feared irreversible complication and her sequential bilateral visual loss is the emergency.

**Brief differential:** aortic/large-vessel arteritis variants (same family), Takayasu arteritis (younger patients), non-arteritic AION (presents with less severe, usually unilateral loss and no jaw claudication/ESR spike), tension-type/occipital neuralgia (cannot explain ESR 113 or optic disk ischemia), and carotid/ophthalmic artery atherosclerosis.

**Key point:** jaw claudication + enlarged tender temporal artery + ESR >100 + sequential visual loss in anyone over 50 is GCA until biopsy proves otherwise, and high-dose glucocorticoids must start the same day — the vision already lost rarely returns, and the other eye is at stake.
