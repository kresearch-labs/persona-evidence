# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 01-medical case-09

**Most likely diagnosis: tuberculous meningitis (TB meningitis of the brainstem/basal meninges) masquerading first as a peripheral facial (Bell's-type) palsy — the cranial neuropathy was the opening note of basal meningeal tuberculosis.**

**One-line justification:** a man with remote pulmonary tuberculosis presents with a unilateral lower-motor-neuron facial palsy (a classic cranial-nerve involvement of basal TB meningitis), then progresses within days to high fever, meningismus, somnolence, and bulbar signs (slow pharyngeal reflex, slurred speech, aphagia); MRI shows multiple brainstem foci, and the CSF is the signature: high opening pressure (245 mmH2O), marked pleocytosis, low glucose (3.0 mmol/L), and very high protein (2.19 g/L) — the classic CSF tetrad of tuberculous meningitis, with Gram/India-ink/AFB negativity expected (TB needs culture/PCR, stains are often negative).

**Brief differential:** cryptococcal meningitis (India ink negative; still possible and should be sent for CrAg), bacterial brainstem encephalitis/abscess (CT repeatedly unremarkable, subacute course), viral brainstem encephalitis (protein 2.19 g/L is far too high), and lymphoma/carcinomatous meningitis (the old TB + fibrotic chest streaks keep TB first).

**Key point:** a lower-motor-neuron facial palsy with prodromal malaise in a patient with TB history is basal meningeal disease until disproven — dexamethasone partially suppressed the inflammation and delayed the picture, and low CSF glucose + very high protein is TB's fingerprint.
