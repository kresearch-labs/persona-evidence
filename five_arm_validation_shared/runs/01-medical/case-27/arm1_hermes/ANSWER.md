# Arm 1 — Reference answer — 01-medical case-27

**Most likely diagnosis: osteomalacia (severe vitamin D deficiency) — adult-onset vitamin D-deficiency rickets with secondary hyperparathyroid bone resorption, proximal myopathy, and frank osteoporotic-level bone loss; the microcytic anemia points to coexisting iron deficiency (or celiac malabsorption as the unifying driver).**

**One-line justification:** the biochemistry is the osteomalacia signature: low vitamin D (25-OH 10 ng/mL), low calcium (7.6), low phosphorus (1.9 — PTH-driven renal phosphate wasting), and a towering alkaline phosphatase (866 — the bone turnover marker of unmineralized osteoid), producing exactly this clinical picture: diffuse bone pain, waddling gait, proximal myopathy with atrophy (vitamin D deficiency myopathy), difficulty rising from chairs and climbing stairs, hypoactive reflexes, and DEXA T-scores of −2.5 to −3.5 (osteomalacia masquerades as osteoporosis on DEXA); the microcytic anemia (Hb 9.96) in a woman with normal inflammatory markers should prompt celiac/iron-deficiency workup, since malabsorption is the classic hidden cause of severe D-deficiency osteomalacia.

**Brief differential:** primary hyperparathyroidism (calcium low, not high), osteoporosis alone (does not raise ALP or cause myopathy/hypophosphatemia), hypophosphatasia (different biochemistry), and inflammatory myopathy (CK normal, weakness is metabolic-myopathic with the full mineral signature).

**Key point:** bone pain + proximal weakness + low-normal calcium + low phosphate + high ALP in one patient is osteomalacia until proven otherwise — replace vitamin D (with calcium), find why it got that low (celiac/iron deficiency here), and expect the myopathy to reverse with repletion.
